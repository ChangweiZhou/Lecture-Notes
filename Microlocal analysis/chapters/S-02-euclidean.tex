\section{Solutions for \cref{ch:euclidean}: Pseudodifferential operators on Euclidean space}\label{solutions:euclidean}

Throughout, the notation is that of \cref{ch:euclidean}. We write
$\norm a_{N,m}$ for the symbol norms of \cref{euclidean:def:symbols} and use freely
that $\norm a_{N,m'}\le\norm a_{N,m}$ for $m\le m'$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:japanese}}

The function $\ang\xi^m=(1+\abs\xi^2)^{m/2}$ is smooth on $\R^n$, being the composite of
the polynomial $1+\abs\xi^2\ge1$ with $t\mapsto t^{m/2}$, which is smooth on
$(0,\infty)$. Regarded as a function on $\R^p\times\R^n$ it does not depend on $z$, so
$D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi\ang\xi^m=0$ for $\alpha\ne0$, and only the estimates with
$\alpha=0$ have to be checked.

We claim that for every $\beta\in\N_0^n$
\begin{equation*}
  D^\beta_\xi\ang\xi^m=\ang\xi^{m-2\abs\beta}P_\beta(\xi),
\end{equation*}
where $P_\beta$ is a polynomial of degree at most $\abs\beta$. For $\beta=0$ take
$P_0=1$. For any $s\in\R$ and any polynomial $P$,
\begin{equation*}
  D_{\xi_j}\bigl(\ang\xi^sP\bigr)
  =-i\,\tfrac s2\,\ang\xi^{s-2}\,2\xi_j\,P+\ang\xi^sD_{\xi_j}P
  =\ang\xi^{s-2}\bigl(-is\xi_jP+\ang\xi^2D_{\xi_j}P\bigr).
\end{equation*}
If $\deg P\le k$ then $\xi_jP$ and $\ang\xi^2D_{\xi_j}P=(1+\abs\xi^2)D_{\xi_j}P$ have
degree at most $k+1$. Applying this with $s=m-2\abs\beta$ and $P=P_\beta$ gives the claim
for $\beta+e_j$, with
$P_{\beta+e_j}=-i(m-2\abs\beta)\xi_jP_\beta+\ang\xi^2D_{\xi_j}P_\beta$.

Write $P_\beta=\sum_{\abs\gamma\le\abs\beta}c_{\beta\gamma}\xi^\gamma$. Since
$\abs{\xi^\gamma}\le\ang\xi^{\abs\gamma}\le\ang\xi^{\abs\beta}$ for
$\abs\gamma\le\abs\beta$, we get $\abs{P_\beta(\xi)}\le C_\beta\ang\xi^{\abs\beta}$ with
$C_\beta=\sum_\gamma\abs{c_{\beta\gamma}}$, and therefore
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi\ang\xi^m}\le C_\beta\ang\xi^{m-2\abs\beta+\abs\beta}
  =C_\beta\ang\xi^{m-\abs\beta}
\end{equation*}
on $\R^p\times\R^n$, for all $\alpha,\beta$ (the left side is $0$ if $\alpha\ne0$). So
$\ang\xi^m\in S^m(\R^p;\R^n)$, for every $m\in\R$ and every $p$.

For the product, \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(ii) gives
$S^m\cdot S^{m'}\subset S^{m+m'}$. Conversely let $c\in S^{m+m'}(\R^p;\R^n)$. By what we
have just shown and \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(ii),
$\ang\xi^{-m}c\in S^{-m}\cdot S^{m+m'}\subset S^{m'}$, and
\begin{equation*}
  c=\ang\xi^m\cdot\bigl(\ang\xi^{-m}c\bigr)\in S^m\cdot S^{m'}.
\end{equation*}
Hence $S^m(\R^p;\R^n)\cdot S^{m'}(\R^p;\R^n)=S^{m+m'}(\R^p;\R^n)$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:winding}}

\emph{Correction.} As originally stated, the exercise had the factor $\frac1{2\pi}$ in
front of the integral. That expression is $i$ times an integer, so it is not an integer
unless it vanishes: for $a(\xi)=\xi_1+i\xi_2$ the integrand is
$\frac{ire^{i\theta}}{re^{i\theta}}=i$ and the expression equals $i$. The correct
normalization is $\frac1{2\pi i}$, and the statement has been corrected accordingly. We
prove the corrected statement.

\emph{Idea:} the integral is the winding number of the closed curve
$\theta\mapsto a(re^{i\theta})$ about $0$; it is an integer that depends continuously on
$r$, hence is constant. If $a$ could be modified near $0$ to a nowhere vanishing $b$,
the winding number of $b$ would be constant all the way down to $r=0$, where it is $0$.

Here $a(re^{i\theta})$ means $a(r\cos\theta,r\sin\theta)$. Since $a$ is elliptic of
order $1$ there are $\eps>0$ and $C$ with $\abs{a(\xi)}\ge\eps\ang\xi$ for $\abs\xi\ge C$.
Fix $r>C$ and put $g(\theta)=a(re^{i\theta})$. This is a smooth, $2\pi$-periodic,
nowhere vanishing function of $\theta\in\R$, so $g'/g$ is continuous and
\begin{equation*}
  W(r)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{g'(\theta)}{g(\theta)}\,d\theta
\end{equation*}
exists. To see that it is an integer, let
$h(\theta)=\int_0^\theta g'(t)/g(t)\,dt$. Then
$\frac d{d\theta}\bigl(g(\theta)e^{-h(\theta)}\bigr)=(g'-gh')e^{-h}=0$, so
$g(\theta)=g(0)e^{h(\theta)}$. Since $g(2\pi)=g(0)\ne0$, $e^{h(2\pi)}=1$, so
$h(2\pi)=2\pi iW(r)\in2\pi i\Z$, i.e.\ $W(r)\in\Z$.

The integrand
$F(r,\theta)=\frac{\pa_\theta a(re^{i\theta})}{a(re^{i\theta})}$ is continuous on
$(C,\infty)\times[0,2\pi]$, since $a$ is smooth and does not vanish for $\abs\xi>C$. Hence
$W$ is continuous on $(C,\infty)$ (uniform continuity of $F$ on
$[r_1,r_2]\times[0,2\pi]$). A continuous integer-valued function on the interval
$(C,\infty)$ is constant. So $W(r)$ is independent of $r>C$.

Now let $a(\xi)=\xi_1+i\xi_2$ for all $\xi\in\R^2$. This is a polynomial of degree $1$,
hence in $S^1(\R^2)$, and $\abs{a(\xi)}=\abs\xi\ge\ang\xi/\sqrt2$ for $\abs\xi\ge1$, so it
is elliptic. Here $g(\theta)=re^{i\theta}$, $g'/g=i$, and $W_a(r)=1$ for all $r>0$.

Suppose $b\in S^1(\R^2)$ satisfies $b(\xi)\ne0$ for all $\xi\in\R^2$ and $b(\xi)=a(\xi)$
for $\abs\xi>r_0$. Define, for $r\ge0$,
\begin{equation*}
  W_b(r)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{\pa_\theta\, b(re^{i\theta})}{b(re^{i\theta})}\,d\theta,
  \qquad \pa_\theta\,b(re^{i\theta})=r\bigl(-\sin\theta\,\pa_{\xi_1}b+\cos\theta\,\pa_{\xi_2}b\bigr)(re^{i\theta}).
\end{equation*}
The integrand is continuous on $[0,\infty)\times[0,2\pi]$, because $b$ is smooth and
never zero. By the argument above, $W_b(r)\in\Z$ for $r>0$, and $W_b$ is continuous on
$[0,\infty)$, hence constant there. At $r=0$ the integrand vanishes, so $W_b\equiv0$. But
for $r>r_0$, $b(re^{i\theta})=re^{i\theta}$ and $W_b(r)=1$. This contradiction shows that
no such $b$ exists, for any $r_0$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:interpolation}}

\emph{Idea:} on an interval, a bound on $f$ together with a bound on a high derivative
$f^{(N)}$ gives a bound on $f'$ which is close to the bound on $f$ when $N$ is large.
Applied on balls of radius $1$ in $z$ and of radius $\ang\xi/2$ in $\xi$, this shows that
each derivative of $a$ loses only an arbitrarily small power of $\ang\xi$ relative to the
improved bound on $a$. The hint's inequality is the case $N=2$ of the inequality below;
using large $N$ avoids an iteration that would only converge in the limit.

\emph{Step 1: a one-variable inequality.}
Let $N\ge2$ and let $V=(k^i)_{0\le k,i\le N-1}$ be the Vandermonde matrix of the
distinct nodes $0,1,\dots,N-1$; it is invertible. Let $\norm{V^{-1}}$ denote its norm as
an operator on $\C^N$ with the maximum norm. We claim: if $I\subset\R$ is a compact
interval of length $\delta$, $f\in\mathcal C^N(I;\C)$, $M_0=\sup_I\abs f$,
$M_N=\sup_I\abs{f^{(N)}}$ and $0<h\le\delta/(2N)$, then
\begin{equation}\label{soleuclidean:eq:landau}
  \sup_I\abs{f'}\le C_N\,h^{-1}\bigl(M_0+h^NM_N\bigr),\qquad
  C_N=\norm{V^{-1}}\Bigl(1+\frac{N^N}{N!}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Let $t\in I$. One of the intervals $[t,t+\delta/2]$, $[t-\delta/2,t]$ lies in $I$;
replacing $f(s)$ by $f(-s)$ if necessary we may assume it is the first. The points
$t+kh$, $0\le k\le N-1$, lie in $[t,t+\delta/2]$. Taylor's formula with integral
remainder gives
\begin{equation*}
  f(t+kh)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}k^i\,y_i+R_k,\qquad y_i=\frac{h^if^{(i)}(t)}{i!},\qquad
  \abs{R_k}\le\frac{(kh)^N}{N!}M_N\le\frac{N^N}{N!}h^NM_N .
\end{equation*}
Thus $y=V^{-1}\bigl(f(t+kh)-R_k\bigr)_{k}$, so
$\abs{y_1}\le\norm{V^{-1}}\bigl(M_0+\frac{N^N}{N!}h^NM_N\bigr)$, which is
\eqref{soleuclidean:eq:landau} at $t$, since $y_1=hf'(t)$.

\emph{Step 2: one derivative.} Let $\mu,\nu\in\R$, let $b\in S^\mu(\R^p;\R^n)$ satisfy
$\abs{b(z,\xi)}\le C\ang\xi^{\nu}$, and let $\eta>0$. We claim that for each
$l$
\begin{equation}\label{soleuclidean:eq:one-step}
  \abs{\pa_{z_l}b}\le C'\ang\xi^{\nu+\eta},\qquad
  \abs{\pa_{\xi_l}b}\le C'\ang\xi^{\nu+\eta-1}.
\end{equation}
If $\nu\ge\mu$ this follows from the symbol estimates of $b$, as
$\ang\xi^\mu\le\ang\xi^{\nu+\eta}$. Assume $\nu<\mu$ and choose an integer $N\ge2$ with
$(\mu-\nu)/N\le\eta$.

For the $z$-derivative fix $(z,\xi)$ and apply \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:landau} to
$f(t)=b(z+te_l,\xi)$ on $I=[-1,1]$, so $\delta=2$. Here $M_0\le C\ang\xi^\nu$ and
$M_N\le C\ang\xi^\mu$. Take $h=N^{-1}\ang\xi^{-(\mu-\nu)/N}\le1/N=\delta/(2N)$. Then
$h^NM_N\le CN^{-N}\ang\xi^{\nu}$ and
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{\pa_{z_l}b(z,\xi)}=\abs{f'(0)}\le C_NN\ang\xi^{(\mu-\nu)/N}\cdot C(1+N^{-N})\ang\xi^\nu
  \le C'\ang\xi^{\nu+\eta}.
\end{equation*}
For the $\xi$-derivative apply \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:landau} to $f(t)=b(z,\xi+te_l)$ on
$I=[-\ang\xi/2,\ang\xi/2]$, so $\delta=\ang\xi$. For $t\in I$ we have
$\frac12\ang\xi\le\ang{\xi+te_l}\le\frac32\ang\xi$; indeed $\ang\cdot$ is the Euclidean
norm of $(1,\cdot)$, so $\abs{\ang{\xi+te_l}-\ang\xi}\le\abs t\le\ang\xi/2$. Hence
$M_0\le C2^{\abs\nu}\ang\xi^\nu$ and $M_N\le C2^{\abs{\mu-N}}\ang\xi^{\mu-N}$. Take
$h=(2N)^{-1}\ang\xi^{1-(\mu-\nu)/N}\le\ang\xi/(2N)$. Then
$h^N\ang\xi^{\mu-N}=(2N)^{-N}\ang\xi^{\nu}$, and
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{\pa_{\xi_l}b(z,\xi)}\le C\,2N\ang\xi^{-1+(\mu-\nu)/N}\ang\xi^{\nu}
  \le C'\ang\xi^{\nu+\eta-1}.
\end{equation*}

\emph{Step 3: conclusion.} Let $a\in S^m$ with $\abs a\le C\ang\xi^{m'}$, let $\eps>0$,
and fix $\alpha,\beta$ with $k=\abs\alpha+\abs\beta\ge1$. Put $\eta=\eps/k$. Write
$D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi$ as a product of $k$ first order derivatives
$D_{w_1}\cdots D_{w_k}$, each $w_i$ one of the variables $z_l$ or $\xi_l$, and let
$b_i=D_{w_i}\cdots D_{w_1}a$ ($b_0=a$). If $b_{i}$ involves $\beta_i$ derivatives in $\xi$
then $b_{i}\in S^{m-\abs{\beta_i}}$ by \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(iii), and by
induction on $i$, using \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:one-step} with
$\mu=m-\abs{\beta_{i}}$, we get
$\abs{b_i}\le C_i\ang\xi^{m'+i\eta-\abs{\beta_i}}$. For $i=k$ this is
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi a}\le C\ang\xi^{m'+\eps-\abs\beta}.
\end{equation*}
Together with $\abs a\le C\ang\xi^{m'}\le C\ang\xi^{m'+\eps}$ this shows
$a\in S^{m'+\eps}(\R^p;\R^n)$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:cone-support}}

\emph{$c_\phi\in S^0$.} Let $g(z,\xi)=\phi(z,\xi/\abs\xi)$, a smooth function on
$\R^p\times(\R^n\setminus0)$, homogeneous of degree $0$ in $\xi$. Since $1-\psi$
vanishes on the open ball $\abs\xi<1$, the function $c_\phi=g\cdot(1-\psi)$ is smooth on
all of $\R^p\times\R^n$ and vanishes for $\abs\xi<1$. Differentiating
$g(z,t\xi)=g(z,\xi)$ shows that $D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi g$ is homogeneous of degree
$-\abs\beta$, so
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi g(z,\xi)}=\abs\xi^{-\abs\beta}
  \abs{(D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi g)(z,\xi/\abs\xi)}\le C_{\alpha\beta}\abs\xi^{-\abs\beta},
\end{equation*}
where $C_{\alpha\beta}$ is the maximum of the continuous function
$\abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi g}$ on the compact set $K\times\Sphere^{n-1}$, $K$ the
projection of $\supp\phi$ to $\R^p$; outside $K\times(\R^n\setminus0)$, $g$ vanishes. For
$\abs\xi\ge1$, $\abs\xi^{-1}\le\sqrt2\ang\xi^{-1}$. The function $1-\psi$ is in
$S^0(\R^n)$: it is bounded and its derivatives of positive order have compact support.
By Leibniz' formula, on $\abs\xi\ge1$,
$\abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi c_\phi}\le C\ang\xi^{-\abs\beta}$, and $c_\phi=0$ on
$\abs\xi<1$. So $c_\phi\in S^0(\R^p;\R^n)$.

\emph{Products.} Let $(\bar z,\bar\xi)\notin\operatorname{cone\,supp}(a)$, and choose
$\phi$ with $\phi(\bar z,\bar\xi/\abs{\bar\xi})\ne0$ and $c_\phi a\in S^{-\infty}$. Then
\begin{equation*}
  c_\phi(ab)=(c_\phi a)\,b\in S^{-\infty}\cdot S^{m'}\subset\bigcap_MS^{-M+m'}=S^{-\infty},
\end{equation*}
by \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(ii). Hence
$(\bar z,\bar\xi)\notin\operatorname{cone\,supp}(ab)$. Exchanging $a$ and $b$, the
complement of $\operatorname{cone\,supp}(ab)$ contains the complements of both cone
supports, which is the asserted inclusion.

\emph{Empty cone support does not imply residual.} Let $p=n=1$, $\chi\in\CIc(\R)$
supported in $1\le\abs t\le2$, $\chi\ne0$, and $a(z,\xi)=\chi(\xi w(z))$ with
$w(z)=(1+z^2)^{-1}$. On $\supp a$ we have $1+z^2\le\abs\xi\le2(1+z^2)$, so $\abs\xi\ge1$
and $w\le2/\abs\xi$.

We first show $a\in S^0(\R;\R)$. The function $w$ is a symbol of order $-2$ in $z$
(\cref{euclidean:ex:japanese} with $m=-2$), so $\abs{w^{(k)}}\le C_k\ang z^{-2-k}\le C_kw$,
and similarly $\abs{\pa^k_z(w^\beta)}\le C_{k\beta}w^\beta$ (as $w^\beta=\ang z^{-2\beta}$). In
particular $w'/w=-2z/(1+z^2)$ has all derivatives bounded. By induction on $\alpha$,
\begin{equation*}
  \pa^\alpha_z\bigl[\chi^{(\beta)}(\xi w)\bigr]=\sum_{j=0}^{\alpha}p_{\alpha j}(z)\,(\xi w)^j\chi^{(\beta+j)}(\xi w)
\end{equation*}
with $p_{\alpha j}$ smooth with bounded derivatives: this holds for $\alpha=0$ with
$p_{00}=1$, and since $\xi w'=(\xi w)(w'/w)$,
\begin{equation*}
  \pa_z\bigl[p(\xi w)^j\chi^{(\beta+j)}(\xi w)\bigr]
  =p'(\xi w)^j\chi^{(\beta+j)}+jp\,\tfrac{w'}w(\xi w)^j\chi^{(\beta+j)}
  +p\,\tfrac{w'}w(\xi w)^{j+1}\chi^{(\beta+j+1)}.
\end{equation*}
Since $\abs{\xi w}\le2$ wherever $\chi^{(k)}(\xi w)\ne0$, each $\pa^\alpha_z[\chi^{(\beta)}(\xi w)]$
is bounded. Now $\pa^\beta_\xi a=w^\beta\chi^{(\beta)}(\xi w)$, so by Leibniz' formula
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\beta_\xi a}\le\sum_{\gamma\le\alpha}\binom\alpha\gamma
  \abs{\pa^{\alpha-\gamma}_z(w^\beta)}\,\abs{\pa^\gamma_z[\chi^{(\beta)}(\xi w)]}
  \le Cw^\beta\le C2^\beta\abs\xi^{-\beta}\le C'\ang\xi^{-\beta}
\end{equation*}
on $\supp a$ (where $\abs\xi\ge1$), and the left side vanishes elsewhere. Thus
$a\in S^0(\R;\R)$.

Next, $\operatorname{cone\,supp}(a)=\emptyset$. Let $(\bar z,\bar\xi)\in\R\times(\R\setminus0)$.
Choose $\phi_1\in\CIc(\R)$ with $\phi_1(\bar z)=1$, $\supp\phi_1\subset[-R,R]$, and
$\phi_2\in\CIc(\R)$ with $\phi_2(\pm1)=1$; put $\phi(z,\theta)=\phi_1(z)\phi_2(\theta)$.
Then $\phi(\bar z,\bar\xi/\abs{\bar\xi})=1$ and $c_\phi a\in S^0$ by the first part and
\cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(ii). On $\supp\phi_1$, $a(z,\xi)=0$ for
$\abs\xi>2(1+R^2)$. So $c_\phi a$ has all derivatives bounded and vanishes for
$\abs\xi>2(1+R^2)$, hence lies in $S^{-\infty}$ by
\cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(v).

Finally $a\notin S^{-\infty}$, indeed $a\notin S^{-\eps}$ for any $\eps>0$: choose $t_0$
with $\chi(t_0)\ne0$; then $a(z,t_0(1+z^2))=\chi(t_0)$ for all $z$, while
$\abs{t_0}(1+z^2)\to\infty$ as $z\to\infty$. So the answer is \emph{no}.

\emph{What is true.} The failure comes from non-compactness in $z$. If $a\in S^m$,
$\operatorname{cone\,supp}(a)=\emptyset$ and $a(z,\xi)=0$ for $z\notin K$, $K$ compact,
then $a\in S^{-\infty}$. Indeed, for each point of $K\times\Sphere^{n-1}$ there is $\phi$
as in the definition, non-zero at that point. By compactness finitely many
$\phi_1,\dots,\phi_k$ suffice, and $\Phi=\sum_i\abs{\phi_i}^2>0$ on an open set
$W\supset K\times\Sphere^{n-1}$. By compactness of $\Sphere^{n-1}$ there is an open
$V\supset K$ with $\overline V$ compact and $\overline V\times\Sphere^{n-1}\subset W$;
choose $\chi_K\in\CIc(V)$ equal to $1$ on $K$ and $\kappa\in\CIc((\frac12,2))$ with
$\kappa(1)=1$, and let $\theta_i(z,\xi)=\kappa(\abs\xi)\chi_K(z)\overline{\phi_i(z,\xi)}/\Phi(z,\xi)$
(zero where $\chi_K\kappa=0$), an element of $\CIc(\R^p\times\R^n)$. On
$\R^p\times\Sphere^{n-1}$, $\sum_i\theta_i\phi_i=\chi_K$, so
\begin{equation*}
  \sum_ic_{\theta_i}\,(c_{\phi_i}a)=\chi_K(z)(1-\psi)^2a=(1-\psi)^2a\in S^{-\infty},
\end{equation*}
as $c_{\theta_i}\in S^0$. Since $a-(1-\psi)^2a=(2\psi-\psi^2)a$ has all derivatives
bounded and compact support in $\xi$, it is residual by
\cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(v), and so $a\in S^{-\infty}$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:off-diagonal}}

\emph{Idea:} multiplying the kernel by $(x-y)^\gamma$ lowers the order of the amplitude by
$\abs\gamma$; away from the diagonal we may divide by $\abs{x-y}^{2L}$.

Let $A\in\Psido m(\R^n)$. By \cref{euclidean:cor:reduction}(i), $A=\Op(a)$ with
$a=\sigma_L(A)\in S^m(\R^n;\R^n)\subset\Amp m0(\R^n)$, and $K=K_A=I(a)$. By
\cref{euclidean:lem:kernel-identities}, for all $\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\N_0^n$,
\begin{equation*}
  (x-y)^\gamma D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yK=I(b_{\alpha\beta\gamma}),\qquad
  b_{\alpha\beta\gamma}=(-D_\xi)^\gamma(\xi+D_x)^\alpha(D_y-\xi)^\beta a
  \in\Amp{m+\abs\alpha+\abs\beta-\abs\gamma}0(\R^n),
\end{equation*}
using the elementary facts listed after \cref{euclidean:lem:amplitude-char}. If
$m+\abs\alpha+\abs\beta-\abs\gamma<-n$, then by \cref{euclidean:prop:osc-int}(i)
$I(b_{\alpha\beta\gamma})$ is the continuous function
$\int e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}b_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,\dbar\xi$, which is bounded by
$\sup_{x,y}\int\abs{b_{\alpha\beta\gamma}}\,\dbar\xi<\infty$.

Fix $\alpha,\beta$ and an integer $L$ with $2L>m+n+\abs\alpha+\abs\beta$. Since
$\abs{x-y}^{2L}=\sum_{\abs\gamma=L}\frac{L!}{\gamma!}(x-y)^{2\gamma}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{x-y}^{2L}D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yK=F_{\alpha\beta L},\qquad
  F_{\alpha\beta L}=\sum_{\abs\gamma=L}\frac{L!}{\gamma!}I(b_{\alpha\beta(2\gamma)}),
\end{equation*}
as distributions on $\R^{2n}$, and $F_{\alpha\beta L}$ is a bounded continuous function.
On the open set $W_0=\{x\ne y\}$ the function $\abs{x-y}^{-2L}$ is smooth, so,
restricting to $W_0$, $D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yK=\abs{x-y}^{-2L}F_{\alpha\beta L}$ there.
Thus every distributional derivative of $K|_{W_0}$ is a continuous function, and hence
$K|_{W_0}\in\CI(W_0)$ (a distribution whose distributional derivatives of all orders are
continuous is a smooth function, as used in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:prop:action-S}). This proves smoothness off the diagonal.

For the decay, on $\{\abs{x-y}\ge1\}$ we have $\ang{x-y}^2\le2\abs{x-y}^2$, hence
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yK(x,y)}\le\abs{x-y}^{-2L}\sup\abs{F_{\alpha\beta L}}
  \le2^L\sup\abs{F_{\alpha\beta L}}\,\ang{x-y}^{-2L}.
\end{equation*}
Since $L$ can be taken arbitrarily large, every derivative of $K$ is
$O(\ang{x-y}^{-N})$ for every $N$ as $\abs{x-y}\to\infty$, uniformly in the remaining
variables. The same argument on $\{\abs{x-y}\ge\delta\}$, where
$\ang{x-y}^2\le(1+\delta^{-2})\abs{x-y}^2$, gives these bounds there for every $\delta>0$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:weyl}}

\emph{Idea:} the Weyl quantization is the quantization of an amplitude, so the adjoint
and reduction formulas apply directly; the bijectivity follows by inverting the
reduction formula asymptotically and treating the residual part through the kernel.

\emph{$\Op_W(a)\in\Psido m$.} Let $c(x,y,\xi)=a(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)$. By the chain rule,
$D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yD^\gamma_\xi c=2^{-\abs\alpha-\abs\beta}(D^{\alpha+\beta}_zD^\gamma_\xi a)(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)$,
which is bounded by $C\ang\xi^{m-\abs\gamma}$. Hence $c\in\Amp m0(\R^n)$ and
$\Op_W(a)=\Op(c)\in\Psido m(\R^n)$.

\emph{Adjoint.} By \cref{euclidean:prop:adjoint}, $\Op(c)^*=\Op(c^*)$ with
$c^*(x,y,\xi)=\overline{c(y,x,\xi)}=\overline{a(\frac{y+x}2,\xi)}=\bar a(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)$,
i.e.\ $\Op_W(a)^*=\Op_W(\bar a)$.

\emph{Left symbol.} By \cref{euclidean:thm:reduction},
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_L(\Op_W(a))\sim\sum_\alpha\frac{i^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_y
  \Bigl[a\bigl(\tfrac{x+y}2,\xi\bigr)\Bigr]_{y=x}
  =\sum_\alpha\frac{(i/2)^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_xa(x,\xi),
\end{equation*}
since $D^\alpha_y[a(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)]=2^{-\abs\alpha}(D^\alpha_za)(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)$. In
particular, if $a\in S^\mu$ then $\sigma_L(\Op_W(a))-a\in S^{\mu-1}$.

\emph{Injectivity.} Suppose $\Op_W(a)=0$ with $a\in S^m$. If $a\in S^\mu$ for some $\mu$,
then $a=a-\sigma_L(\Op_W(a))\in S^{\mu-1}$; by induction $a\in S^{-\infty}$. Then
$c\in\Amp{-\infty}0$, and by \cref{euclidean:prop:osc-int}(i) the kernel of
$\Op_W(a)$ is the continuous function
$K(x,y)=g\bigl(\frac{x+y}2,x-y\bigr)$, where
$g(w,v)=\int e^{iv\cdot\xi}a(w,\xi)\,\dbar\xi$. Since
$(x,y)\mapsto(\frac{x+y}2,x-y)$ is a bijection of $\R^{2n}$, $K=0$ forces $g=0$. For fixed
$w$, $a(w,\cdot)\in\cS(\R^n)$ (all $\xi^\kappa D^\beta_\xi a$ are bounded), and $g(w,\cdot)$
is its inverse Fourier transform, so $a(w,\cdot)=0$ by Fourier inversion. Hence $a=0$.

\emph{Residual operators.} Let $E\in\Psido{-\infty}(\R^n)$ with kernel $K$. We show
$E=\Op_W(e')$ for some $e'\in S^{-\infty}(\R^n;\R^n)$. By
\cref{euclidean:prop:residual}, $K$ is smooth with
$\abs{D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yK}\le C_{\alpha\beta N}\ang{x-y}^{-N}$. Put
$g(w,v)=K(w+\frac v2,w-\frac v2)$. By the chain rule every derivative
$D^\alpha_wD^\beta_vg$ is a finite combination of derivatives of $K$ evaluated at
$(w+\frac v2,w-\frac v2)$, where $x-y=v$, so
$\sup_{w,v}\abs{v^\gamma D^\alpha_wD^\beta_vg}<\infty$ for all $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$. Exactly
as in the proof of (b)$\Rightarrow$(c) in \cref{euclidean:prop:residual} (with $w$ in place
of $x$ and $v$ in place of $z$), $e'(w,\xi)=\int e^{-iv\cdot\xi}g(w,v)\,dv$ lies in
$S^{-\infty}(\R^n;\R^n)$, and by Fourier inversion and \cref{euclidean:prop:osc-int}(i) the
kernel of $\Op_W(e')$ is $\int e^{i(x-y)\cdot\xi}e'(\frac{x+y}2,\xi)\,\dbar\xi
=g(\frac{x+y}2,x-y)=K(x,y)$. Thus $E=\Op_W(e')$.

\emph{Surjectivity.} Let $A\in\Psido m(\R^n)$, $b=\sigma_L(A)\in S^m$. By
\cref{euclidean:prop:borel} choose $a_0\in S^m$ with
$a_0\sim\sum_\alpha\frac{(-i/2)^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_xb$ (the terms with
$\abs\alpha=j$ grouped, of order $m-j$). Fix $N$. Write
$a_0=\sum_{\abs\alpha<N}\frac{(-i/2)^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_xb+r_N$ with
$r_N\in S^{m-N}$. Since $\Op_W(r_N)\in\Psido{m-N}$, $\sigma_L(\Op_W(r_N))\in S^{m-N}$ by
\cref{euclidean:cor:reduction}(ii). For each $\alpha$, applying the left symbol formula to
$D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_xb\in S^{m-\abs\alpha}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_L\bigl(\Op_W(D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_xb)\bigr)-\sum_{\abs\beta<N-\abs\alpha}
  \frac{(i/2)^{\abs\beta}}{\beta!}D^{\alpha+\beta}_\xi D^{\alpha+\beta}_xb\in S^{m-N}.
\end{equation*}
Adding up, with $\gamma=\alpha+\beta$,
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_L(\Op_W(a_0))-\sum_{\abs\gamma<N}\Bigl(\sum_{\alpha+\beta=\gamma}
  \frac{(-i/2)^{\abs\alpha}(i/2)^{\abs\beta}}{\alpha!\,\beta!}\Bigr)D^\gamma_\xi D^\gamma_xb\in S^{m-N}.
\end{equation*}
By the multinomial theorem the inner sum is
$\frac1{\gamma!}\prod_l\bigl(-\frac i2+\frac i2\bigr)^{\gamma_l}$, which is $1$ for
$\gamma=0$ and $0$ otherwise. Hence $\sigma_L(\Op_W(a_0))-b\in S^{m-N}$ for every $N$,
i.e.\ $A-\Op_W(a_0)=\Op(b-\sigma_L(\Op_W(a_0)))\in\Psido{-\infty}$. By the previous step
$A-\Op_W(a_0)=\Op_W(e')$ with $e'\in S^{-\infty}$, so $A=\Op_W(a_0+e')$ with
$a_0+e'\in S^m$. Thus $\Op_W:S^m(\R^n;\R^n)\to\Psido m(\R^n)$ is a bijection.

As a check, for $n=1$ and $a=x\xi$ the formula gives
$\sigma_L(\Op_W(a))=x\xi+\frac i2D_\xi D_x(x\xi)=x\xi-\frac i2$, and indeed
$\frac12(xD+Dx)=xD+\frac12[D,x]=xD-\frac i2$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:ph-smooth}}

\emph{Idea:} in the variable $r=1/\abs\xi$ a function homogeneous of degree $m-j$ becomes
$r^{-m}\cdot r^j\times$(a function of the direction), so after multiplication by $r^m$
the expansion $\sum_ja_{m-j}$ becomes a Taylor expansion in $r$ at $r=0$; the symbol
estimates of the remainders are exactly the bounds on the Taylor remainders.

We take $m\in\R$; for complex $m$ the same proof works with $\abs{r^m}=r^{\Re m}$, $m$
replaced by $\Re m$ in all estimates. \emph{Convention:} for a function $G$ on
$\R^p\times\Sphere^{n-1}\times[0,1]$ put $G^\sharp(z,\omega,r)=G(z,\omega/\abs\omega,r)$ on
$\R^p\times\mathcal O\times[0,1]$, $\mathcal O=\{\frac14<\abs\omega<4\}\subset\R^n$; ``$G$ is
smooth with all derivatives bounded'' means that $G^\sharp$ is $\CI$, with one-sided
derivatives at $r=0,1$, and all its derivatives are bounded. This agrees with the
definition by charts on the sphere, since $\omega\mapsto\omega/\abs\omega$ is a smooth
submersion $\mathcal O\to\Sphere^{n-1}$ with bounded derivatives, and $G$ is the restriction of
$G^\sharp$ to $\abs\omega=1$.

Let $F(z,\theta,r)=r^ma(z,\theta/r)$. Then $F^\sharp(z,\omega,r)=r^ma(z,\Psi(\omega,r))$
with $\Psi(\omega,r)=\omega/(\abs\omega r)$; note $\abs{\Psi(\omega,r)}=1/r\ge1$.

\emph{Step 1.} Let $b\in S^\mu(\R^p;\R^n)$ and $B(z,\omega,r)=b(z,\Psi(\omega,r))$ on
$\R^p\times\mathcal O\times(0,1]$. We claim
\begin{equation}\label{soleuclidean:eq:polar-est}
  \abs{\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\gamma_\omega\pa^j_rB}\le C\,r^{-\mu-j}.
\end{equation}
We show by induction that $\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\gamma_\omega\pa^j_rB$ is $r^{-j}$ times a finite
sum of terms $c(\omega)(\xi^\kappa\pa^\nu_\xi\pa^\alpha_zb)(z,\Psi)$ with
$\abs\kappa\le\abs\nu$ and $c\in\CI(\mathcal O)$ bounded with all derivatives. This is
clear for $\gamma=0$, $j=0$. Since $\Psi\cdot\omega/\abs\omega=1/r$ we have
$r^{-1}=\sum_i\frac{\omega_i}{\abs\omega}\Psi_i$. Also
$\pa_{\omega_k}\Psi_l=r^{-1}e_{kl}(\omega)$ with $e_{kl}=\pa_{\omega_k}(\omega_l/\abs\omega)$, and
$\pa_r\Psi=-r^{-1}\Psi$. Hence for a smooth $h$,
\begin{equation*}
  \pa_{\omega_k}[h(\Psi)]=\sum_{l,i}e_{kl}(\omega)\frac{\omega_i}{\abs\omega}
  (\xi_i\pa_{\xi_l}h)(\Psi),\qquad
  \pa_r[h(\Psi)]=-r^{-1}\sum_l(\xi_l\pa_{\xi_l}h)(\Psi),
\end{equation*}
and $\xi_i\pa_{\xi_l}(\xi^\kappa\pa^\nu_\xi f)$ is a sum of terms $\xi^{\kappa'}\pa^{\nu'}_\xi f$
with $\abs{\kappa'}\le\abs{\nu'}$; the derivative of the prefactor $r^{-j}$ is
$-jr^{-j-1}$, and $\omega$-derivatives of the coefficients $c$ are again bounded. This
proves the structure. Each term is bounded by
$C\ang\xi^{\mu+\abs\kappa-\abs\nu}\le C\ang\xi^\mu$ at $\xi=\Psi$, where
$r^{-1}\le\ang\xi\le\sqrt2r^{-1}$, so by $C'r^{-\mu}$; this gives
\eqref{soleuclidean:eq:polar-est}.

\emph{Step 2: polyhomogeneous $\Rightarrow$ smooth.} Let $a\sim\sum_ja_{m-j}$ as in
\cref{euclidean:def:polyhomogeneous}. Every symbol satisfies
\eqref{euclidean:eq:uniform-regular}. Put $h_j^\sharp(z,\omega)=a_{m-j}(z,\omega/\abs\omega)$;
by \eqref{euclidean:eq:uniform-regular} for $a_{m-j}$ it is smooth with bounded
derivatives on $\R^p\times\mathcal O$. By homogeneity (with $t=1/r\ge1$),
$a_{m-j}(z,\Psi(\omega,r))=r^{-m+j}h_j^\sharp(z,\omega)$, so for every $N$
\begin{equation*}
  F^\sharp(z,\omega,r)=\sum_{j<N}r^jh_j^\sharp(z,\omega)+E_N,\qquad
  E_N=r^mR_N(z,\Psi(\omega,r)),\quad R_N=a-\sum_{j<N}a_{m-j}\in S^{m-N}.
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:polar-est} with $\mu=m-N$ and Leibniz' formula
($\pa_r^ir^m=m(m-1)\cdots(m-i+1)r^{m-i}$),
$\abs{\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\gamma_\omega\pa^j_rE_N}\le Cr^{N-j}$ on $\R^p\times\mathcal O\times(0,1]$.
Given $k$, take $N=k+1$: all derivatives of $E_N$ of order at most $k$ are bounded and
tend to $0$ uniformly as $r\to0$. A function on $(0,1]$ which is $\mathcal C^1$ and whose
derivative has a continuous extension to $[0,1]$ extends to a $\mathcal C^1$ function on
$[0,1]$ (mean value theorem); applying this repeatedly, $E_N$ extends to a
$\mathcal C^k$ function on $\R^p\times\mathcal O\times[0,1]$, with bounded derivatives of
order $\le k$, all vanishing at $r=0$. Hence $F^\sharp$ is $\mathcal C^k$ up to $r=0$ with
bounded derivatives of order at most $k$; as $k$ is arbitrary, $F$ extends to a smooth
function with all derivatives bounded. Moreover $\pa^j_rF^\sharp(z,\omega,0)=j!\,h_j^\sharp(z,\omega)$.

\emph{Step 3: smooth $\Rightarrow$ polyhomogeneous.} Suppose $a$ satisfies
\eqref{euclidean:eq:uniform-regular} and $F$ extends as stated. Put
$h_j^\sharp=\frac1{j!}\pa^j_rF^\sharp(\cdot,\cdot,0)$, smooth with bounded derivatives on
$\R^p\times\mathcal O$ and homogeneous of degree $0$ in $\omega$; extend it to
$\R^p\times(\R^n\setminus0)$ by homogeneity. Choose $\rho\in\CI(\R^n)$ vanishing for
$\abs\xi\le\frac12$ and equal to $1$ for $\abs\xi\ge1$, and set
$a_{m-j}(z,\xi)=\rho(\xi)\abs\xi^{m-j}h_j^\sharp(z,\xi)$. This is smooth, homogeneous of
degree $m-j$ in $\abs\xi\ge1$, and satisfies \eqref{euclidean:eq:uniform-regular}, so
$a_{m-j}\in S^{m-j}$ by the discussion before \cref{euclidean:def:polyhomogeneous}. By
Taylor's formula in $r$, for $N\ge1$,
\begin{equation*}
  F^\sharp(z,\omega,r)-\sum_{j<N}r^jh^\sharp_j(z,\omega)=r^NG_N(z,\omega,r),\quad
  G_N=\frac1{(N-1)!}\int_0^1(1-\tau)^{N-1}\pa^N_rF^\sharp(z,\omega,\tau r)\,d\tau,
\end{equation*}
and $G_N$ is smooth with all derivatives bounded (differentiate under the integral); put
$G_0=F^\sharp$. For $\abs\xi\ge1$ take $\omega=\xi/\abs\xi$, $r=1/\abs\xi$, so that
$\Psi(\omega,r)=\xi$; then $a(z,\xi)=\abs\xi^mF^\sharp(z,\omega,r)$ and
$a_{m-j}(z,\xi)=\abs\xi^{m-j}h_j^\sharp(z,\omega)$, whence
\begin{equation*}
  R_N(z,\xi)=a-\sum_{j<N}a_{m-j}=\abs\xi^{m-N}G_N\bigl(z,u(\xi)\bigr),\qquad
  u(\xi)=\bigl(\xi/\abs\xi,1/\abs\xi\bigr),\quad\abs\xi\ge1.
\end{equation*}
The components of $u$ are homogeneous of degree $0$ or $-1$, so
$\abs{\pa^\beta u_i}\le C_\beta\abs\xi^{-\abs\beta}$ for $\abs\xi\ge1$. By the chain rule,
$\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\beta_\xi[G_N(z,u(\xi))]$ is a finite sum of products of a bounded
derivative of $G_N$ with factors $\pa^{\beta_1}u_{i_1}\cdots\pa^{\beta_k}u_{i_k}$,
$\beta_1+\dots+\beta_k=\beta$, hence is $O(\abs\xi^{-\abs\beta})$. With Leibniz' formula
and $\abs{\pa^\beta\abs\xi^{m-N}}\le C\abs\xi^{m-N-\abs\beta}$ we obtain
$\abs{\pa^\alpha_z\pa^\beta_\xi R_N}\le C\abs\xi^{m-N-\abs\beta}\le C'\ang\xi^{m-N-\abs\beta}$
for $\abs\xi\ge1$. For $\abs\xi\le1$ all derivatives of $R_N$ are bounded by
\eqref{euclidean:eq:uniform-regular} for $a$ and the $a_{m-j}$. Hence
$R_N\in S^{m-N}$ for all $N$; $N=0$ gives $a\in S^m$, and $a\sim\sum_ja_{m-j}$, so
$a\in\Symph m(\R^p;\R^n)$.

\emph{Taylor series.} Steps 2 and 3 show: the Taylor coefficients of $F$ at $r=0$ are the
homogeneous terms restricted to the unit sphere,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac1{j!}\pa^j_rF(z,\theta,0)=a_{m-j}(z,\theta),\qquad\abs\theta=1,
\end{equation*}
so that $F(z,\theta,r)\sim\sum_jr^ja_{m-j}(z,\theta)$ as $r\to0$. The Taylor series need not
converge, and if it does, its sum need not be $F$; the difference is a residual symbol.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:ph-complete}}

\emph{Idea:} two choices of cut-off change the normalized terms only on the annulus
$\frac12\le\abs\xi\le1$, and there, by homogeneity, the terms are controlled by their
values on $1\le\abs\xi\le2$, which do not depend on the cut-off.

Let $\rho,\rho'$ be two admissible cut-offs, and denote by $a_{(m-j)}$ and $a'_{(m-j)}$ the
normalized terms of $a\in\Symph m$ formed with $\rho$ and $\rho'$. Let
$h_j(z,\xi)=\abs\xi^{m-j}a_{m-j}(z,\xi/\abs\xi)$, the homogeneous extension to
$\xi\ne0$. Then
\begin{equation*}
  d_j=a_{(m-j)}-a'_{(m-j)}=(\rho-\rho')h_j,
\end{equation*}
and $\rho-\rho'$ is smooth and supported in $\frac12\le\abs\xi\le1$. We compare the three
families of norms in \cref{euclidean:def:ph-topology}.

(1) The norms $\norm a_{N,\Re m}$ do not involve the cut-off.

(2) On $1\le\abs\xi\le2$, $\rho=\rho'=1$, so $a_{(m-j)}=a'_{(m-j)}$ there, and the second
family of norms is the same for both choices.

(3) For the third family,
\begin{equation*}
  \Bigl\|a-\sum_{j\le k}a'_{(m-j)}\Bigr\|_{N,\Re m-k-1}\le
  \Bigl\|a-\sum_{j\le k}a_{(m-j)}\Bigr\|_{N,\Re m-k-1}+\sum_{j\le k}\norm{d_j}_{N,\Re m-k-1}.
\end{equation*}
We estimate $\norm{d_j}_{N,\mu}$ for any $\mu\in\R$. On
$\frac12\le\abs\xi\le1$, $1\le\ang\xi\le\sqrt2$, so
$\norm{d_j}_{N,\mu}\le2^{(\abs\mu+N)/2}\max_{\abs\alpha+\abs\beta\le N}\sup\abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi d_j}$.
For $\frac12\le\abs\xi\le1$, homogeneity gives $h_j(z,\xi)=2^{-(m-j)}h_j(z,2\xi)$, and
$2\xi$ lies in $1\le\abs{2\xi}\le2$, where $h_j=a_{(m-j)}$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi h_j(z,\xi)}=2^{-\Re m+j+\abs\beta}
  \abs{(D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi a_{(m-j)})(z,2\xi)},
\end{equation*}
and by Leibniz' formula
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{d_j}_{N,\mu}\le C_{N,\mu,j}\max_{\abs\alpha+\abs\beta\le N}\
  \sup_{z\in\R^p,\ 1\le\abs\xi\le2}\abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi a_{(m-j)}(z,\xi)},
\end{equation*}
with $C_{N,\mu,j}$ depending only on $N,\mu,j,m$ and on finitely many derivatives of
$\rho-\rho'$. So each norm of the $\rho'$-family is bounded by a finite combination of
norms of the $\rho$-family; by symmetry the converse holds. The identity map is therefore
continuous in both directions, and the topology does not depend on the cut-off.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:ph-not-dense}}

\emph{$a\in S^m$.} Let $L(\xi)=\log\ang\xi$. Then $\pa_{\xi_j}L=\xi_j\ang\xi^{-2}\in S^{-1}(\R^n)$ by
\cref{euclidean:ex:japanese} and \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(ii). We claim that for
$f\in\CI(\R)$ with all derivatives bounded, $f(L)\in S^0(\R^n)$. By induction on
$\abs\beta\ge1$,
\begin{equation*}
  D^\beta_\xi[f(L)]=\sum_{k=1}^{\abs\beta}f^{(k)}(L)\,g_{\beta,k},\qquad g_{\beta,k}\in S^{-\abs\beta}(\R^n):
\end{equation*}
for $\abs\beta=1$ this is $D_{\xi_j}f(L)=f'(L)D_{\xi_j}L$, and
$D_{\xi_j}[f^{(k)}(L)g]=f^{(k+1)}(L)(D_{\xi_j}L)g+f^{(k)}(L)D_{\xi_j}g$ with
$(D_{\xi_j}L)g,D_{\xi_j}g\in S^{-\abs\beta-1}$. Hence
$\abs{D^\beta f(L)}\le C_\beta\ang\xi^{-\abs\beta}$. With $f=\cos$,
$\cos(\log\ang\xi)\in S^0$, and $a=\ang\xi^m\cos(\log\ang\xi)\in S^m$ by
\cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(i),(ii).

\emph{$a$ is not in the closure of $\Symph m$.} We show that
$\norm{a-b}_{0,m}=\sup\ang\xi^{-m}\abs{a-b}\ge\frac12$ for every $b\in\Symph m(\R^n)$;
since $\norm\cdot_{0,m}$ is one of the norms of $S^m$, $a$ is not in the closure. Let
$b\sim\sum_jb_{m-j}$. Since $b-b_m\in S^{m-1}$, for every $\theta\in\Sphere^{n-1}$ (which
exists as $n\ge1$)
\begin{equation*}
  \ang{r\theta}^{-m}b(r\theta)=\Bigl(\frac r{\ang r}\Bigr)^mb_m(\theta)+O(r^{-1})
  \longrightarrow b_m(\theta)\qquad(r\to\infty).
\end{equation*}
On the other hand $\ang{r\theta}^{-m}a(r\theta)=\cos(\log\ang r)$ equals $1$ at the
points $r_k$ with $\log\ang{r_k}=2\pi k$ and $-1$ at the points $r'_k$ with
$\log\ang{r'_k}=(2k+1)\pi$, $k\in\N$, and $r_k,r'_k\to\infty$. If
$\norm{a-b}_{0,m}<\frac12$, letting $r\to\infty$ along $r_k$ and along $r'_k$ gives
$\abs{1-b_m(\theta)}\le\frac12$ and $\abs{-1-b_m(\theta)}\le\frac12$, hence
$2\le1$, a contradiction. Thus $\Symph m$ is not dense in $S^m$.

\emph{Density for the topology of $S^{m'}$, $m'>m$.} Let $a\in S^m(\R^p;\R^n)$. By
\cref{euclidean:lem:density}, $a_\eps=\chi(\eps\xi)a\in S^{-\infty}(\R^p;\R^n)$ and
$a_\eps\to a$ in $S^{m'}$ as $\eps\to0$. Since
$S^{-\infty}\subset\Symph m$ (take all homogeneous terms equal to $0$),
$\Symph m(\R^p;\R^n)$ is dense in $S^m(\R^p;\R^n)$ for the topology of $S^{m'}$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:hol-asymptotic}}

\emph{Idea:} run Borel's construction with $\eps_j$ chosen uniformly on the $j$-th set of
an exhaustion. The cut-off does not change the homogeneous terms, so the polyhomogeneous
norms of the tail reduce to ordinary symbol norms, which are small. Holomorphy then passes
to the locally uniform limit.

We use two general facts.

\begin{lemma}\label{soleuclidean:lem:hol-limit}
  Let $E$ be a Fr\'echet space, $V\subset\C$ open, and $u_k:V\to E$ holomorphic maps
  converging to $u$ uniformly on compact subsets of $V$ \textup{(}in every continuous
  seminorm of $E$\textup{)}. Then $u$ is holomorphic.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  Let $v:V\to E$ be holomorphic, $p$ a continuous seminorm on $E$, and
  $\{\abs{s-s_0}\le2r\}\subset V$. For $\abs{s-s_0}<r$ and $0<\abs h,\abs{h'}<r/2$ let
  $Q_hv(s)=h^{-1}(v(s+h)-v(s))$. For $\lambda\in E'$ with $\abs{\lambda}\le p$, the scalar
  function $\lambda\circ v$ is holomorphic, and Cauchy's integral formula over
  $\abs{t-s_0}=2r$ gives
  \begin{equation*}
    \lambda\bigl(Q_hv(s)-Q_{h'}v(s)\bigr)=\frac{h-h'}{2\pi i}\oint
    \frac{\lambda(v(t))\,dt}{(t-s-h)(t-s-h')(t-s)},
  \end{equation*}
  so $\abs{\lambda(Q_hv(s)-Q_{h'}v(s))}\le16r^{-2}\abs{h-h'}\sup_{\abs{t-s_0}=2r}p(v(t))$. By
  the Hahn--Banach theorem, $p(x)=\sup\{\abs{\lambda(x)};\lambda\in E',\abs\lambda\le p\}$,
  so the same bound holds for $p(Q_hv(s)-Q_{h'}v(s))$. Apply this to $v=u_k$ and let
  $k\to\infty$ (for fixed $h,h'$, $Q_hu_k(s)\to Q_hu(s)$ in $E$):
  $p(Q_hu(s)-Q_{h'}u(s))\le16r^{-2}\abs{h-h'}\sup_{\abs{t-s_0}=2r}p(u(t))$. Thus
  $(Q_hu(s))$ is Cauchy as $h\to0$ in every seminorm, and converges since $E$ is
  complete.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{soleuclidean:lem:ph-hol-criterion}
  Let $\mu\in\C$, $V\subset\C$ open and $c:V\to\Symph\mu(\R^p;\R^n)$. If the maps
  $s\mapsto c(s)\in S^{\Re\mu}$, $s\mapsto c(s)_{(\mu-j)}\in S^{\Re\mu-j}$ and
  $s\mapsto c(s)-\sum_{j\le k}c(s)_{(\mu-j)}\in S^{\Re\mu-k-1}$ are holomorphic for all
  $j,k$, then $c$ is holomorphic into $\Symph\mu(\R^p;\R^n)$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
  The normalized terms depend linearly on $c$, so the difference quotients satisfy
  $(Q_hc)_{(\mu-j)}=Q_h(c_{(\mu-j)})$. Each norm of \cref{euclidean:def:ph-topology} is a
  norm of $S^{\Re\mu}$, $S^{\Re\mu-k-1}$, or (for the second family) is bounded by a norm
  $\norm\cdot_{N,\Re\mu-j}$ of $S^{\Re\mu-j}$, applied to one of the three maps. Hence the
  difference quotients of $c$ are Cauchy in every norm of $\Symph\mu$ as $h\to0$, and
  converge because $\Symph\mu$ is complete (\cref{euclidean:lem:ph-frechet}(ii)).
\end{proof}

Now fix $\phi\in\CI(\R^n)$ with $\phi=0$ for $\abs\xi\le1$, $\phi=1$ for $\abs\xi\ge2$,
and write $\phi=1-\chi$ with $\chi\in\CIc(\R^n)$. As in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:prop:borel} there are constants $C_N$ with
\begin{equation}\label{soleuclidean:eq:borel-gain}
  \norm{\phi(\eps\xi)b}_{N,\nu+1}\le C_N\,\eps\,\norm b_{N,\nu}\qquad
  (b\in S^\nu,\ \nu\in\R,\ 0<\eps\le1).
\end{equation}

\emph{The cut-off is continuous on $\Symph{-j}$.} Let $0<\eps\le1$ and
$b\in\Symph{-j}$, $b\sim\sum_lb_{-j-l}$. The function $\chi(\eps\xi)b$ has all derivatives
bounded and vanishes for $\abs\xi\ge2/\eps$, so it is residual and
$\norm{\chi(\eps\xi)b}_{N,\nu}\le C_{N,\nu,\eps}\norm b_{N,-j}$ for all $N$, $\nu$. Hence
$\phi(\eps\xi)b=b-\chi(\eps\xi)b\in\Symph{-j}$ has the same normalized terms as $b$, and
every norm of \cref{euclidean:def:ph-topology} of $\phi(\eps\xi)b$ is bounded by the same
norm of $b$ plus $C\norm b_{N,-j}$. So $b\mapsto\phi(\eps\xi)b$ is a continuous linear map
of $\Symph{-j}$, and $b\mapsto\chi(\eps\xi)b$ is continuous from $\Symph{-j}$ to
$S^{-\infty}$; by \cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule}, $s\mapsto\phi(\eps\xi)a_j(s)$ is
holomorphic into $\Symph{-j}$ and $s\mapsto\chi(\eps\xi)a_j(s)$ into $S^{-\infty}$. Note
also that $S^{-\infty}\hookrightarrow\Symph{-M}$ is continuous for every $M$ (all normalized
terms vanish, and the norms are symbol norms).

\emph{Choice of $\eps_j$.} Let $K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots$ be compact subsets of $U$ with
$K_l\subset\operatorname{int}K_{l+1}$ and $\bigcup_lK_l=U$. A holomorphic map is continuous,
so $B_{N,j}=\sup_{s\in K_j}\norm{a_j(s)}_{N,-j}<\infty$. Choose $\eps_j\in(0,1]$
decreasing to $0$ with $C_N\eps_jB_{N,j}\le2^{-j}$ for all $N\le j$. Put
$b_j(s)=\phi(\eps_j\xi)a_j(s)$. By \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:borel-gain},
\begin{equation}\label{soleuclidean:eq:tail}
  \norm{b_j(s)}_{N,-j+1}\le2^{-j}\qquad(s\in K_j,\ N\le j).
\end{equation}

\emph{Convergence of the tails in $\Symph{-M}$.} Fix $M\in\N_0$. For $j\ge M$,
$b_j(s)\in\Symph{-j}\subset\Symph{-M}$ (\cref{euclidean:lem:ph-complex}(iii)); as an element of
$\Symph{-M}$ its normalized term of degree $-M-l$ is $a_j(s)_{(-M-l)}$ if $j\le M+l$ and
$0$ if $j>M+l$. Consider the norms of $\Symph{-M}$ applied to $b_j(s)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $\norm{b_j(s)}_{N,-M}\le\norm{b_j(s)}_{N,-j+1}\le2^{-j}$ for $j\ge\max(M+1,N)$, $s\in K_j$;
\item the normalized term of degree $-M-l$ vanishes unless $j\le M+l$, so for each such
  norm only finitely many $j$ contribute;
\item for $j\ge\max(M+k+2,N)$ the terms of degree $-M-l$, $l\le k$, vanish, and the
  remainder norm is
  $\norm{b_j(s)}_{N,-M-k-1}\le\norm{b_j(s)}_{N,-j+1}\le2^{-j}$ for $s\in K_j$.
\end{itemize}
Let $K\subset U$ be compact; then $K\subset K_J$ for some $J$, hence $K\subset K_j$ for
$j\ge J$. Thus for every norm $q$ of $\Symph{-M}$ we have $q(b_j(s))\le2^{-j}$ for
$s\in K$ and all large $j$. Hence the partial sums of $\sum_{j\ge M}b_j(s)$ are uniformly
Cauchy on $K$ in every norm, and converge uniformly on compact sets to a map
$T_M:U\to\Symph{-M}$ ($\Symph{-M}$ is complete). The partial sums are holomorphic into
$\Symph{-M}$ (finite sums of holomorphic maps composed with the continuous inclusions
$\Symph{-j}\hookrightarrow\Symph{-M}$, \cref{euclidean:lem:ph-frechet}(iii)), so $T_M$ is
holomorphic by \cref{soleuclidean:lem:hol-limit}.

\emph{Conclusion.} Let $a=T_0=\sum_{j\ge0}b_j$, holomorphic into $\Symph0$. Since
$\Symph{-M}\hookrightarrow\Symph0$ is continuous, $T_M(s)$ is also the limit in $\Symph0$ of
$\sum_{M\le j<J}b_j(s)$, so
\begin{equation*}
  a(s)-\sum_{j<M}a_j(s)=-\sum_{j<M}\chi(\eps_j\xi)a_j(s)+T_M(s).
\end{equation*}
The first sum is holomorphic into $S^{-\infty}\hookrightarrow\Symph{-M}$, and $T_M$ is
holomorphic into $\Symph{-M}$. Hence $a-\sum_{j<M}a_j$ is holomorphic from $U$ into
$\Symph{-M}(\R^p;\R^n)$ for every $M$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:hol-adjoint}}

\emph{Idea:} as in \cref{euclidean:prop:hol-composition}, expand the adjoint formula to
finite order; the finitely many explicit terms are holomorphic in the polyhomogeneous
topology, and the remainder is holomorphic in a space of arbitrarily low order. The map
$t\mapsto A(t)^*$ is anti-holomorphic, and the substitution $t=\bar s$ makes it
holomorphic.

\emph{Step 1: the adjoint remainder.} For $m\in\R$ and $N\in\N_0$ let
\begin{equation*}
  r^*_N(p)=\sigma_L\bigl(\Op(p)^*\bigr)-\sum_{\abs\alpha<N}\frac{i^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}
  D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_x\bar p,\qquad p\in S^m(\R^n;\R^n).
\end{equation*}
By \eqref{euclidean:eq:adjoint-symbol}, $r^*_N$ takes values in $S^{m-N}$. It is
conjugate-linear and continuous into $S^m$, by \cref{euclidean:prop:continuity} (continuity
of the adjoint) and \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(iii). By
\cref{euclidean:lem:auto-continuity}(i), applied to $p\mapsto\overline{r^*_N(p)}$ (which is
linear) with $G=S^m$, $r^*_N:S^m\to S^{m-N}$ is continuous.

\emph{Step 2: holomorphy of the conjugated data.} Write
$\sigma_L(A(t))=p(t)=\ang\xi^{f(t)}b(t)$ with $b:U\to\Symph0$ holomorphic. Put
$b^*(s)=\overline{b(\bar s)}$ for $s\in U^*$. Complex conjugation is a continuous
conjugate-linear map of $\Symph0$ (\cref{euclidean:lem:ph-frechet}(iii)), so
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{b^*(s+h)-b^*(s)}h=\overline{\Bigl(\frac{b(\bar s+\bar h)-b(\bar s)}{\bar h}\Bigr)}
  \longrightarrow\overline{b'(\bar s)}\quad(h\to0),
\end{equation*}
and $b^*:U^*\to\Symph0$ is holomorphic. Likewise $f^*$ is holomorphic on $U^*$. Since
$\overline{\ang\xi^w}=\ang\xi^{\bar w}$, we have
$\overline{p(\bar s)}=\ang\xi^{f^*(s)}b^*(s)$.

\emph{Step 3: expansion.} Let
$c(s)=\ang\xi^{-f^*(s)}\sigma_L(A(\bar s)^*)$, $s\in U^*$. By Leibniz' formula and
\cref{euclidean:lem:japanese-holomorphic}(ii), for each $N$,
\begin{gather*}
  c(s)=\sum_{\abs\alpha<N}c_\alpha(s)+\ang\xi^{-f^*(s)}\,r^*_N\bigl(p(\bar s)\bigr),\\
  c_\alpha(s)=\frac{i^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}\sum_{\nu\le\alpha}\binom\alpha\nu
  \Bigl(\sum_{k\le\abs\nu}f^*(s)^kq_{\nu,k}\Bigr)D^{\alpha-\nu}_\xi D^\alpha_xb^*(s).
\end{gather*}
By \cref{euclidean:lem:ph-frechet}(iii) and \cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule}, each $c_\alpha$
is holomorphic from $U^*$ into $\Symph{-\abs\alpha}(\R^n;\R^n)$.

\emph{Step 4: the remainder.} Fix $s_0\in U^*$, $t_0=\bar s_0$, and $\eps=\frac18$. By
\cref{euclidean:lem:japanese-holomorphic}(i) there is a disc $V\ni t_0$ on which
$t\mapsto\ang\xi^{f(t)}$ is holomorphic into $S^{\mu}$, $\mu=\Re f(t_0)+\eps$, and a disc
$V'\ni s_0$ on which $s\mapsto\ang\xi^{-f^*(s)}$ is holomorphic into
$S^{-\Re f(t_0)+\eps}$ (note $\Re f^*(s_0)=\Re f(t_0)$). As in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:prop:hol-composition}, $p$ is holomorphic from $V$ into $S^\mu$. Since
$r^*_N$ is continuous and conjugate-linear, on $V^*=\{\bar t;t\in V\}$
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{r^*_N(p(\overline{s+h}))-r^*_N(p(\bar s))}{h}
  =r^*_N\Bigl(\frac{p(\bar s+\bar h)-p(\bar s)}{\bar h}\Bigr)\longrightarrow r^*_N(p'(\bar s)),
\end{equation*}
so $s\mapsto r^*_N(p(\bar s))$ is holomorphic into $S^{\mu-N}$. With
\cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule}, the remainder term is holomorphic on $V^*\cap V'$ into
$S^{-N+2\eps}\subset S^{-N+\frac14}$.

\emph{Step 5: conclusion.} For $s\in V^*\cap V'$,
$c(s)-\sum_{\abs\alpha<N}c_\alpha(s)\in S^{-N+\frac14}$ for every $N$, so
$c(s)\sim\sum_l\sum_{\abs\alpha=l}c_\alpha(s)$; by \cref{euclidean:lem:ph-complex}(i) and
\cref{euclidean:lem:asymptotic-manip}(iii), $c(s)\in\Symph0$ with normalized terms
$c(s)_{(-j)}=\sum_{\abs\alpha\le j}c_\alpha(s)_{(-j)}$. We verify the hypotheses of
\cref{soleuclidean:lem:ph-hol-criterion} on $V^*\cap V'$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $c=c_0+\ang\xi^{-f^*}r^*_1(p(\bar s))$ is holomorphic into $S^0$, since $c_0$ is
  holomorphic into $\Symph0\hookrightarrow S^0$ and the second term into $S^{-3/4}\subset S^0$;
\item $c_{(-j)}=\sum_{\abs\alpha\le j}(c_\alpha)_{(-j)}$ is holomorphic into $S^{-j}$ by
  \cref{euclidean:lem:ph-frechet}(i);
\item with $N=k+2$,
  \begin{equation*}
    c-\sum_{j\le k}c_{(-j)}=\ang\xi^{-f^*}r^*_N(p(\bar s))
    +\sum_{\abs\alpha\le k}\Bigl(c_\alpha-\sum_{j=\abs\alpha}^k(c_\alpha)_{(-j)}\Bigr)
    +\sum_{\abs\alpha=k+1}c_\alpha,
  \end{equation*}
  where the first term is holomorphic into $S^{-k-7/4}$, the second into $S^{-k-1}$ by the
  definition of the topology of $\Symph{-\abs\alpha}$, and the third into
  $\Symph{-k-1}\hookrightarrow S^{-k-1}$.
\end{itemize}
Hence $c$ is holomorphic near every $s_0$, i.e.\ $c:U^*\to\Symph0(\R^n;\R^n)$ is
holomorphic, and
$A(\bar s)^*=\Op\bigl(\ang\xi^{f^*(s)}c(s)\bigr)$ shows that
$(A(\bar s)^*)_{s\in U^*}\in\Psido{f^*}_{\mathrm{hol}}(\R^n)$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:smooth-parametrix}}

\emph{Idea:} build the symbol $\psi/a(t)$ on compact sets of parameters, glue with a
partition of unity in $t$, and run the Neumann series argument of
\cref{euclidean:lem:neumann} with a parameter-dependent Borel lemma. All operations used
(products, adjoints, remainders) are continuous, so they preserve smooth dependence.

A family $G(t)$ in $\Psido{-\infty}(\R^n)$ is called smooth if
$t\mapsto\sigma_L(G(t))\in S^{-\infty}(\R^n;\R^n)$ is smooth. Since the topology of
$S^{-\infty}$ is given by all the norms of all the $S^{-M}$, and limits in the spaces
$S^{-M}$ are compatible (the inclusions are continuous and injective), a map is smooth into
$S^{-\infty}$ if and only if it is smooth into $S^{-M}$ for every $M$. Write
$a(t)=\sigma_L(A(t))$.

\emph{Step 1: symbol of an approximate inverse.} Let $K\subset U$ be compact, with
$\eps_K,C_K$ as in the hypothesis, and $\Omega_K=\operatorname{int}K\times\R^n\subset\R^{k+n}$.
By \cref{euclidean:lem:smooth-symbols}, $a$ satisfies the symbol estimates of order $m$ in
the base variables $(t,x)\in\Omega_K$, i.e.\ $a\in S^m(\Omega_K;\R^n)$ in the sense of
\cref{euclidean:rem:symbols-open}, and $\abs a\ge\eps_K\ang\xi^m$ on
$\Omega_K\times\{\abs\xi>C_K\}$. The proof of \cref{euclidean:lem:powers} uses only the
symbol estimates of $a$ and the lower bound on the set in question, so it applies without
change to $a\in S^m(\Omega_K;\R^n)$ and the open set $\Omega_K\times\{\abs\xi>C_K\}$. Choose
$\psi_K\in\CI(\R^n)$ with $\psi_K=0$ for $\abs\xi\le C_K+1$ and $\psi_K=1$ for
$\abs\xi\ge C_K+2$, and let $b_K=\psi_K/a$ (zero where $\psi_K=0$). As in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:lem:elliptic-symbol}, $b_K\in S^{-m}(\Omega_K;\R^n)$, and
$ab_K-1=\psi_K-1$.

The sets $\operatorname{int}K$, $K$ compact, cover $U$. Choose a locally finite smooth
partition of unity $(g_i)$ on $U$ with $\supp g_i\subset\operatorname{int}K_i$, $K_i$
compact, and put
\begin{equation*}
  b_0(t,x,\xi)=\sum_ig_i(t)\,b_{K_i}(t,x,\xi).
\end{equation*}
On a compact $L\subset U$ only finitely many $g_i$ are non-zero, and each term satisfies
\eqref{euclidean:eq:smooth-symbol-est} of order $-m$ on $L$. By
\cref{euclidean:lem:smooth-symbols}, $b_0\in\CI(U;S^{-m}(\R^n;\R^n))$. Moreover
\begin{equation*}
  a(t)b_0(t)-1=\sum_ig_i(t)\bigl(\psi_{K_i}(\xi)-1\bigr);
\end{equation*}
on $L$ this is a finite sum of smooth functions of $(t,\xi)$ vanishing for $\abs\xi$
large, so it satisfies \eqref{euclidean:eq:smooth-symbol-est} for every order, and
$ab_0-1$ is a smooth family in $S^{-\infty}$.

\emph{Step 2: a parameter-dependent Borel lemma.} Let $\mu\in\R$ and
$c_j\in\CI(U;S^{\mu-j}(\R^n;\R^n))$, $j\in\N_0$. We claim there is
$c\in\CI(U;S^\mu)$ with $c-\sum_{j<N}c_j\in\CI(U;S^{\mu-N})$ for all $N$. With $\phi$,
$\chi=1-\phi$ and $C_N$ as in \eqref{soleuclidean:eq:borel-gain}, and an exhaustion
$K_1\subset K_2\subset\cdots$ of $U$ as in the previous solution, choose $\eps_j\in(0,1]$
decreasing to $0$ with
\begin{equation*}
  C_N\eps_j\sup_{t\in K_j}\norm{\pa^\gamma_tc_j(t)}_{N,\mu-j}\le2^{-j}\qquad
  (N\le j,\ \abs\gamma\le j),
\end{equation*}
which is possible since the suprema are finite by \cref{euclidean:lem:smooth-symbols}. Let
$c=\sum_j\phi(\eps_j\xi)c_j$. On $\{\abs\xi\le R\}$ only finitely many terms are non-zero,
so $c$ is smooth in $(t,x,\xi)$ and may be differentiated termwise, and
$\pa^\gamma_t(\phi(\eps_j\xi)c_j)=\phi(\eps_j\xi)\pa^\gamma_tc_j$. Fix $M$, a compact
$K\subset U$ (so $K\subset K_j$ for $j\ge J$) and $\gamma,\alpha,\beta$; let
$N=\abs\alpha+\abs\beta$. For $j\ge\max(M+1,N,\abs\gamma,J)$, by
\eqref{soleuclidean:eq:borel-gain},
\begin{equation*}
  \sup_{t\in K}\ang\xi^{-(\mu-M)+\abs\beta}\abs{D^\alpha_xD^\beta_\xi\pa^\gamma_t(\phi(\eps_j\xi)c_j)}
  \le\sup_{t\in K}\norm{\phi(\eps_j\xi)\pa^\gamma_tc_j(t)}_{N,\mu-j+1}\le2^{-j},
\end{equation*}
and each of the finitely many remaining terms with $j\ge M$ satisfies
\eqref{euclidean:eq:smooth-symbol-est} of order $\mu-j\le\mu-M$. Hence
$\sum_{j\ge M}\phi(\eps_j\xi)c_j$ satisfies \eqref{euclidean:eq:smooth-symbol-est} of order
$\mu-M$, and is in $\CI(U;S^{\mu-M})$ by \cref{euclidean:lem:smooth-symbols}. Finally
$c-\sum_{j<M}c_j=-\sum_{j<M}\chi(\eps_j\xi)c_j+\sum_{j\ge M}\phi(\eps_j\xi)c_j$, and the
first sum is a smooth family in $S^{-\infty}$ (it vanishes for $\abs\xi\ge2/\eps_{M-1}$ and
has locally uniformly bounded derivatives).

\emph{Step 3: the Neumann series.} Let $B_1(t)=\Op(b_0(t))$, a smooth family in
$\Psido{-m}$. By \cref{euclidean:prop:smooth-families}(ii), $E(t)=A(t)B_1(t)-\Id$ is a
smooth family in $\Psido0$ ($\Id$ being a constant family), and by
\cref{euclidean:cor:remainder-continuous} with $N=1$,
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_L(E(t))=\bigl(a(t)b_0(t)-1\bigr)+r_1\bigl(a(t),b_0(t)\bigr),
\end{equation*}
where $r_1:S^m\times S^{-m}\to S^{-1}$ is continuous bilinear; by
\cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule} and Step~1, $E$ is a smooth family in $\Psido{-1}$. By
\cref{euclidean:prop:continuity} and \cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule}, $(-E(t))^j$ is a
smooth family in $\Psido{-j}$. By Step~2 there is a smooth family $F(t)$ in $\Psido{-1}$
with $\sigma_L(F)-\sum_{j=1}^N\sigma_L((-E)^j)\in\CI(U;S^{-N-1})$ for every $N$. As in the
proof of \cref{euclidean:lem:neumann},
\begin{equation*}
  (\Id+E)(\Id+F)-\Id=-(-E)^{N+1}+(\Id+E)\Bigl(F-\sum_{j=1}^N(-E)^j\Bigr),
\end{equation*}
which is a smooth family in $\Psido{-N-1}$ for every $N$ (again by
\cref{euclidean:prop:continuity} and \cref{euclidean:lem:product-rule}), hence a smooth
family in $\Psido{-\infty}$. Put $B(t)=B_1(t)(\Id+F(t))$, a smooth family in $\Psido{-m}$;
then $G(t)=A(t)B(t)-\Id=(\Id+E)(\Id+F)-\Id$ is a smooth family in $\Psido{-\infty}$.

The same construction with $B_1A$ in place of $AB_1$ (now
$\sigma_L(B_1A-\Id)=(b_0a-1)+r_1(b_0,a)$) gives a smooth family $B'(t)$ in $\Psido{-m}$
with $G'(t)=B'(t)A(t)-\Id$ a smooth family in $\Psido{-\infty}$. As in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:lem:left-parametrix}, $B'(AB)=(B'A)B$ gives $B-B'=B'G-G'B$, a smooth family
in $\Psido{-\infty}$ (products of a smooth family in $\Psido{-m}$ with a smooth family in
$\Psido{-\infty}$ are smooth into every $\Psido{-M}$, by \cref{euclidean:prop:continuity}).
Hence
\begin{equation*}
  B(t)A(t)-\Id=(B-B')A+G'=(B'G-G'B)A+G'
\end{equation*}
is a smooth family in $\Psido{-\infty}$ as well.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:rho-delta}}

\emph{Idea:} every argument of \cref{euclidean:sec:symbols,euclidean:sec:reduction} is a
bookkeeping of orders. A $\xi$-derivative now gains only $\rho$, and an $x$- or
$y$-derivative loses $\delta$. The proofs survive as long as $\delta<1$ (for the
oscillatory integrals) and $\rho>\delta$ (for Borel's lemma and the Taylor remainder).

We use the norms
$\norm a_{N,m;\rho,\delta}=\max_{\abs\alpha+\abs\beta\le N}\sup\ang\xi^{-m+\rho\abs\beta-\delta\abs\alpha}
\abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi a}$. Exactly as in \cref{euclidean:lem:symbol-basic}(iv),
$S^m_{\rho,\delta}(\R^p;\R^n)$ is a Fr\'echet space. Since $\rho\le1$ and $\delta\ge0$,
$\ang\xi^{m-\abs\beta}\le\ang\xi^{m-\rho\abs\beta+\delta\abs\alpha}$, so
$S^m=S^m_{1,0}\subset S^m_{\rho,\delta}$ continuously; and
$\bigcap_mS^m_{\rho,\delta}=S^{-\infty}$, since in both cases every derivative is
$O(\ang\xi^{-N})$ for all $N$.

\emph{Products and derivatives.} By Leibniz' formula, for $a\in S^m_{\rho,\delta}$,
$b\in S^{m'}_{\rho,\delta}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{D^\alpha_zD^\beta_\xi(ab)}\le\sum_{\mu\le\alpha,\gamma\le\beta}\binom\alpha\mu\binom\beta\gamma
  C\ang\xi^{m-\rho\abs\gamma+\delta\abs\mu}\ang\xi^{m'-\rho\abs{\beta-\gamma}+\delta\abs{\alpha-\mu}}
  =C'\ang\xi^{m+m'-\rho\abs\beta+\delta\abs\alpha},
\end{equation*}
so $ab\in S^{m+m'}_{\rho,\delta}$ (with
$\norm{ab}_{N,m+m';\rho,\delta}\le C_N\norm a_{N,m;\rho,\delta}\norm b_{N,m';\rho,\delta}$). And
$D^{\alpha'}_zD^{\beta'}_\xi(D^\beta_\xi D^\alpha_za)=D^{\alpha+\alpha'}_zD^{\beta+\beta'}_\xi a$ is
bounded by
$C\ang\xi^{(m-\rho\abs\beta+\delta\abs\alpha)-\rho\abs{\beta'}+\delta\abs{\alpha'}}$, so
$D^\beta_\xi D^\alpha_z$ maps $S^m_{\rho,\delta}$ continuously into
$S^{m-\rho\abs\beta+\delta\abs\alpha}_{\rho,\delta}$.

\emph{Asymptotic sums.} Let $m_j=m-j(\rho-\delta)$, which decreases to $-\infty$ because
$\rho>\delta$, and $a_j\in S^{m_j}_{\rho,\delta}$. With $\phi$ as in the proof of
\cref{euclidean:prop:borel}, $\phi(\eps\xi)$ is bounded in $S^0=S^0_{1,0}\subset
S^0_{\rho,\delta}$ for $0<\eps\le1$, so
$\norm{\phi(\eps\xi)a_j}_{N,m_j;\rho,\delta}\le C_{N,j}$ uniformly in $\eps$. Since
$\phi(\eps\xi)a_j$ vanishes where $\ang\xi^{-1}>\eps$, raising the order by $\rho-\delta>0$
gains $\eps^{\rho-\delta}$:
$\norm{\phi(\eps\xi)a_j}_{N,m_j+\rho-\delta;\rho,\delta}\le C_{N,j}\eps^{\rho-\delta}$.
Choose $\eps_j\in(0,1]$ decreasing to $0$ with $C_{N,j}\eps_j^{\rho-\delta}\le2^{-j}$ for
$N\le j$, and let $a=\sum_j\phi(\eps_j\xi)a_j$, a locally finite sum. For fixed $M$ and
$j\ge M+1$ we have $m_j+\rho-\delta=m_{j-1}\le m_M$, so
$\norm{\phi(\eps_j\xi)a_j}_{N,m_M;\rho,\delta}\le2^{-j}$ for $j\ge N$. The series
$\sum_{j\ge M}\phi(\eps_j\xi)a_j$ therefore converges in the Fr\'echet space
$S^{m_M}_{\rho,\delta}$, and
\begin{equation*}
  a-\sum_{j<M}a_j=\sum_{j<M}(\phi(\eps_j\xi)-1)a_j+\sum_{j\ge M}\phi(\eps_j\xi)a_j
  \in S^{-\infty}+S^{m_M}_{\rho,\delta}\subset S^{m_M}_{\rho,\delta},
\end{equation*}
the first sum being smooth with bounded derivatives and compactly supported in $\xi$. The
asymptotic sum is unique modulo $S^{-\infty}$, as in \cref{euclidean:prop:borel}.

\emph{The reduction theorem.} Define amplitudes $\Amp m{w;\rho,\delta}(\R^n)$ by
$\abs{D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yD^\gamma_\xi a}\le C\ang{x-y}^w\ang\xi^{m-\rho\abs\gamma+\delta(\abs\alpha+\abs\beta)}$.
The statement to be proved is: for $a\in\Amp m{w;\rho,\delta}$ there is a unique
$\sigma_L\in S^m_{\rho,\delta}(\R^n;\R^n)$ with $\Op(a)=\Op(\sigma_L)$, and
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_L\sim\sum_\alpha\frac{i^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_ya\big|_{y=x},
\end{equation*}
the terms with $\abs\alpha=j$ lying in $S^{m-j(\rho-\delta)}_{\rho,\delta}$. We go through
the ingredients of the proof of \cref{euclidean:thm:reduction}, saying which use $\rho=1$,
$\delta=0$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
\item \emph{Oscillatory integral (\cref{euclidean:prop:osc-int}).} Each $x$-derivative of
  $a$ now costs $\ang\xi^\delta$, so $(1+\Delta_x)^q[a\phi]$ is bounded by
  $C\ang\xi^{m+2q\delta}\ang{(x,y)}^{\abs w}\sum_{\abs\nu\le2q}\abs{D^\nu\phi}$, and the
  integrand of \eqref{euclidean:eq:I-def} by $C\ang\xi^{m-2q(1-\delta)}(\dots)$. The
  condition $m-2q<-n$ must be replaced by $m-2q(1-\delta)<-n$, which can be met because
  $\delta<1$. The rest of the proof (independence of $q$, parts (i)--(iii)) is unchanged;
  in (ii) one uses that $\chi(\eps\xi)$ is bounded in $S^0\subset S^0_{\rho,\delta}$, so the
  analogue of \cref{euclidean:lem:density} holds with the same proof. The same change
  applies to \eqref{euclidean:eq:Op-formula} in \cref{euclidean:prop:action-S}.
\item \emph{Kernel identities (\cref{euclidean:lem:kernel-identities}) and
  \cref{euclidean:prop:action-S}.} These are unchanged, except for orders: $\xi_ja$ has
  order $m+1$, $D_{x_j}a$ and $D_{y_j}a$ have order $m+\delta\le m+1$, and $D_{\xi_j}a$ has
  order $m-\rho$. Only finiteness of orders is used.
\item \emph{Taylor expansion (\cref{euclidean:lem:taylor}).} This is the step where
  $\rho=1$, $\delta=0$ were used in an essential way. The term
  $\frac{i^{\abs\alpha}}{\alpha!}D^\alpha_\xi D^\alpha_ya|_{y=x}$ has order
  $m-\rho\abs\alpha+\delta\abs\alpha=m-\abs\alpha(\rho-\delta)$, not $m-\abs\alpha$
  (restriction to $y=x$ keeps the type, since $D_x[c(x,x,\xi)]=((D_x+D_y)c)(x,x,\xi)$). In
  the remainder
  $r_\alpha=\frac N{\alpha!}\int_0^1(1-t)^{N-1}(\pa^\alpha_ya)(x,x+t(y-x),\xi)\,dt$, the $N$
  derivatives in $y$ cost $\ang\xi^{\delta N}$, and every further $x$- or $y$-derivative
  costs $\ang\xi^\delta$, so $r_\alpha\in\Amp{m+\delta N}{w_+;\rho,\delta}$. The identity
  $I((y-x)^\alpha r_\alpha)=I(D^\alpha_\xi r_\alpha)$ then gains only $\rho N$, so the
  remainder operator is $\Op(D^\alpha_\xi r_\alpha)$ with
  $D^\alpha_\xi r_\alpha\in\Amp{m-N(\rho-\delta)}{w_+;\rho,\delta}$. Its order tends to
  $-\infty$ with $N$ precisely because $\rho>\delta$.
\item \emph{Borel's lemma.} It is needed for the orders $m-j(\rho-\delta)$, and was
  proved above; it uses $\rho>\delta$ in the gain $\eps^{\rho-\delta}$, which for $(1,0)$
  is the gain $\eps$ of \eqref{euclidean:eq:borel-gain}.
\item \emph{Residual operators (\cref{euclidean:prop:residual}).} An operator $\Op(a)$
  with $a\in\Amp{m-N(\rho-\delta)}{w_N;\rho,\delta}$ for every $N$ is residual: in the
  proof of (a)$\Rightarrow$(b), $(-D_\xi)^\gamma(\xi+D_x)^\alpha(D_y-\xi)^\beta a_M$ now has
  order at most $-M+\abs\alpha+\abs\beta-\rho\abs\gamma\le-M+\abs\alpha+\abs\beta$, which is
  $<-n$ for $M$ large, and the argument is otherwise unchanged. The implication
  (b)$\Rightarrow$(c) and the uniqueness argument do not involve the type at all.
\end{enumerate}
With these modifications the proof of \cref{euclidean:thm:reduction} goes through: if
$b\in S^m_{\rho,\delta}$ is an asymptotic sum of the Taylor terms, then
$\Op(a)-\Op(b)$ has amplitudes of order $m-N(\rho-\delta)$ for all $N$, so equals
$\Op(c)$ with $c\in S^{-\infty}$, and $\sigma_L=b+c$. (For invariance under changes of
variables one needs in addition $\rho+\delta\ge1$; see \cite[Section~18.1]{Hormander-III}.
This is not used here.)

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:not-compact}}

\emph{Idea:} translation preserves all Sobolev norms but moves mass off to infinity.

Let $f\in\CIc(\R^n)$, $f\ne0$, with $\supp f\subset\{\abs x\le R\}$; normalize so that
$\norm f_{H^s}=1$ ($f\in\cS\subset H^s$). Let $f_j(x)=f(x-3jRe_1)$, $j\in\N$. Since
$\widehat{f_j}(\xi)=e^{-3ijR\xi_1}\hat f(\xi)$,
$\norm{f_j}_{H^s}^2=\int\ang\xi^{2s}\abs{\hat f}^2\,\dbar\xi=1$. The supports of the $f_j$
are pairwise disjoint, so for $j\ne k$
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{f_j-f_k}_{L^2}^2=\norm{f_j}_{L^2}^2+\norm{f_k}_{L^2}^2=2\norm f_{L^2}^2>0.
\end{equation*}
No subsequence is Cauchy in $L^2(\R^n)$, so none converges. Hence the unit ball of
$H^s(\R^n)$ is not precompact in $L^2(\R^n)$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:rellich}}

\emph{Idea:} compact support makes the Fourier transforms uniformly Lipschitz, so they
converge uniformly on compact sets along a subsequence; the $H^N$ bound controls high
frequencies uniformly.

Let $u_k\in H^N(\R^n)$, $\norm{u_k}_{H^N}\le1$, $u_k=0$ in $\abs x>R$; we find a
subsequence convergent in $L^2$. Since $N>0$, $\norm{u_k}_{L^2}\le\norm{u_k}_{H^N}\le1$.
Let $\phi\in\CIc(\R^n)$ with $\phi=1$ on $\abs x\le R$; then $u_k=\phi u_k$ almost
everywhere. As $\phi u_k\in L^1\cap L^2$, its Fourier transform is the continuous
function (by Fubini, $\hat u_k(\psi)=u_k(\hat\psi)=\int\psi(\xi)\int e^{-ix\cdot\xi}u_k(x)\,dx\,d\xi$)
\begin{equation*}
  \hat u_k(\xi)=\int e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\phi(x)u_k(x)\,dx .
\end{equation*}
By Cauchy--Schwarz, $\abs{\hat u_k(\xi)}\le\norm\phi_{L^2}$ and, using
$\abs{e^{-ix\cdot\xi}-e^{-ix\cdot\eta}}\le\abs x\abs{\xi-\eta}$,
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{\hat u_k(\xi)-\hat u_k(\eta)}\le\norm{\abs x\phi}_{L^2}\abs{\xi-\eta}.
\end{equation*}
(This is the equicontinuity in the hint; the convolution formula
$\hat u=(2\pi)^{-n}\hat\phi*\hat u$ leads to the same bound.) The family $(\hat u_k)$ is
uniformly bounded and equicontinuous on $\R^n$. By the Arzel\`a--Ascoli theorem on the
balls $\{\abs\xi\le l\}$, $l\in\N$, and a diagonal argument, there is a subsequence, still
denoted $(u_k)$, such that $\hat u_k$ converges uniformly on every compact set.

We show that $(u_k)$ is Cauchy in $L^2$. Let $\eta>0$. By Plancherel,
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{u_k-u_l}_{L^2}^2=\int_{\abs\xi\le\varrho}\abs{\hat u_k-\hat u_l}^2\,\dbar\xi
  +\int_{\abs\xi>\varrho}\abs{\hat u_k-\hat u_l}^2\,\dbar\xi .
\end{equation*}
The second integral is at most
$\ang\varrho^{-2N}\int\ang\xi^{2N}\abs{\hat u_k-\hat u_l}^2\,\dbar\xi
=\ang\varrho^{-2N}\norm{u_k-u_l}_{H^N}^2\le4\ang\varrho^{-2N}$, which is $<\eta/2$ for
$\varrho$ large, as $N>0$. For this $\varrho$ the first integral tends to $0$ as
$k,l\to\infty$ by uniform convergence on $\abs\xi\le\varrho$. Hence
$\norm{u_k-u_l}^2<\eta$ for $k,l$ large, and $(u_k)$ converges in $L^2(\R^n)$. This proves
the compactness of the inclusion, for every real $N>0$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:weighted-compact}}

\emph{Idea:} the weight makes $f_j$ uniformly small outside large balls; inside a ball,
\cref{euclidean:ex:rellich} applies; a diagonal argument combines the two.

Let $\norm{\ang x^\eps f_j}_{H^\eps}\le1$. Since $H^\eps\subset L^2$ with
$\norm\cdot_{L^2}\le\norm\cdot_{H^\eps}$, we have $\norm{\ang x^\eps f_j}_{L^2}\le1$, and
$f_j=\ang x^{-\eps}(\ang x^\eps f_j)\in L^2$. Choose $\phi\in\CIc(\R^n)$ with
$0\le\phi\le1$, $\phi=1$ on $\abs x\le1$ and $\supp\phi\subset\{\abs x\le2\}$, and let
$\phi_k(x)=\phi(x/k)$, $k\in\N$.

\emph{Tails.} $1-\phi_k$ vanishes on $\abs x<k$, and for $\abs x\ge k$,
$\ang x^{-\eps}\le\ang k^{-\eps}$. Hence
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{(1-\phi_k)f_j}_{L^2}\le\sup_{\abs x\ge k}\ang x^{-\eps}\,\norm{\ang x^\eps f_j}_{L^2}
  \le\ang k^{-\eps}.
\end{equation*}

\emph{Compactly supported parts.} Fix $k$. The function
$\psi_k=\phi_k\ang x^{-\eps}$ is in $\CIc(\R^n)\subset\CIb(\R^n)$, so multiplication by it
is in $\Psido0(\R^n)$ (\cref{euclidean:ex:diff-ops}) and is bounded on $H^\eps$ by
\cref{euclidean:thm:sobolev}. Hence $\phi_kf_j=\psi_k(\ang x^\eps f_j)$ satisfies
$\norm{\phi_kf_j}_{H^\eps}\le C_k$ and vanishes in $\abs x>2k$. By
\cref{euclidean:ex:rellich} (with $N=\eps$, $R=2k$, after dividing by $C_k$), every
subsequence of $(\phi_kf_j)_j$ has a further subsequence convergent in $L^2$.

\emph{Diagonalization.} Choose infinite sets $\N\supset J_1\supset J_2\supset\cdots$ such
that $(\phi_kf_j)_{j\in J_k}$ converges in $L^2$, and let $j_l$ be the $l$-th element of
$J_l$. For each $k$, $(j_l)_{l\ge k}$ is a subsequence of $J_k$, so $(\phi_kf_{j_l})_l$
converges in $L^2$. Given $\eta>0$ choose $k$ with $2\ang k^{-\eps}<\eta/2$; then
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{f_{j_l}-f_{j_{l'}}}_{L^2}\le\norm{\phi_k(f_{j_l}-f_{j_{l'}})}_{L^2}+2\ang k^{-\eps}<\eta
\end{equation*}
for $l,l'$ large. Thus $(f_{j_l})$ is Cauchy, hence convergent in $L^2(\R^n)$, and the
inclusion $\ang x^{-\eps}H^\eps(\R^n)\hookrightarrow L^2(\R^n)$ is compact.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:affine}}

\emph{Idea:} the affine substitution $x'=Tx$, $y'=Ty$, $\xi=(L^t)^{-1}\eta$ preserves the
phase $(x'-y')\cdot\xi=(x-y)\cdot\eta$ and has total Jacobian $1$; it therefore transforms
any amplitude by composition. This handles left and right symbols simultaneously.

\emph{The transformed amplitude.} For $c\in\Amp mw(\R^n)$ let
\begin{equation*}
  c_T(x,y,\eta)=c\bigl(Tx,Ty,(L^t)^{-1}\eta\bigr).
\end{equation*}
Put $M=(L^t)^{-1}$. Since $\abs{M\eta}\le\norm M\abs\eta$ and $\abs\eta\le\norm{M^{-1}}\abs{M\eta}$,
$\ang{M\eta}$ and $\ang\eta$ are comparable, and likewise $\ang{T x-Ty}=\ang{L(x-y)}$ and
$\ang{x-y}$; this holds for negative powers as well. By the chain rule,
$D^\alpha_xD^\beta_yD^\gamma_\eta c_T$ is a linear combination, with coefficients
polynomial in the entries of $L$ and $M$, of derivatives
$(D^{\alpha'}_{x'}D^{\beta'}_{y'}D^{\gamma'}_\xi c)(Tx,Ty,M\eta)$ with $\abs{\gamma'}=\abs\gamma$.
Hence $c_T\in\Amp mw(\R^n)$.

\emph{Residual amplitudes.} Let $c\in\Amp{-\infty}w$, $f\in\cS(\R^n)$ and
$g=f\circ T^{-1}$. By \cref{euclidean:prop:action-S} (the case $m<-n$), the integral
$\Op(c)g(x')=\int e^{i(x'-y')\cdot\xi}c(x',y',\xi)g(y')\,dy'\,\dbar\xi$ converges
absolutely. Put $x'=Tx$, substitute $y'=Ty$ ($dy'=\abs{\det L}\,dy$) and $\xi=M\eta$
($\dbar\xi=\abs{\det L}^{-1}\dbar\eta$). As $(Tx-Ty)\cdot M\eta=L(x-y)\cdot(L^t)^{-1}\eta=(x-y)\cdot\eta$,
\begin{equation*}
  \bigl(T^*\Op(c)(T^*)^{-1}f\bigr)(x)=\Op(c)g(Tx)
  =\int e^{i(x-y)\cdot\eta}c_T(x,y,\eta)f(y)\,dy\,\dbar\eta=\Op(c_T)f(x).
\end{equation*}

\emph{General amplitudes.} Let $c\in\Amp mw$, and with $\chi$ as in
\cref{euclidean:lem:density} let $c_\eps=\chi(\eps\xi)c$. Then
$(c_\eps)_T=\chi(\eps M\eta)c_T$. The function $\chi_M=\chi\circ M$ is in $\CIc(\R^n)$
and $D^\beta[\chi_M(\eps\eta)]=\eps^{\abs\beta}(D^\beta\chi_M)(\eps\eta)$ is supported where
$1\le\abs{M\eps\eta}\le2$, i.e.\ where $c_1\le\eps\abs\eta\le c_2$ with
$c_1=\norm{M}^{-1}$, $c_2=2\norm{M^{-1}}$. There $\ang\eta\le(1+c_2)/\eps$, so
$\abs{D^\beta[\chi_M(\eps\eta)]}\le C_\beta\ang\eta^{-\abs\beta}$ uniformly in
$\eps\in(0,1]$: the family $\chi_M(\eps\eta)$ is bounded in $S^0(\R^n)$. Hence
$(c_\eps)_T$ is bounded in $\Amp mw$; and $c_T-(c_\eps)_T$ vanishes for
$\abs\eta<c_1/\eps$, so, as in the proof of \cref{euclidean:lem:density},
$(c_\eps)_T\to c_T$ in $\Amp{m'}w$ for every $m'>m$. By
\cref{euclidean:prop:osc-int}(ii) and \eqref{euclidean:eq:Op-def},
$\Op((c_\eps)_T)f\to\Op(c_T)f$ and $\Op(c_\eps)g\to\Op(c)g$ in $\cSp(\R^n)$. The pull-back
$T^*$ acts on $\cSp$ by $(T^*u)(v)=\abs{\det L}^{-1}u(v\circ T^{-1})$, which extends
$u\mapsto u\circ T$ on functions and is weakly continuous. Applying $T^*$ to the second
limit and using the residual case,
\begin{equation*}
  T^*\Op(c)(T^*)^{-1}f=\lim_{\eps\to0}T^*\Op(c_\eps)g=\lim_{\eps\to0}\Op((c_\eps)_T)f=\Op(c_T)f .
\end{equation*}
Thus, for every amplitude, $\Op(c)_T=\Op(c_T)$.

\emph{Left and right symbols.} If $A=\Op(a)$ with $a=\sigma_L(A)$, i.e.\ $c(x,y,\xi)=a(x,\xi)$,
then $c_T(x,y,\eta)=a(Tx,M\eta)$ does not depend on $y$, so $A_T=\Op(a(Tx,M\eta))$ and by
uniqueness of the left symbol (\cref{euclidean:thm:reduction})
$\sigma_L(A_T)(x,\xi)=\sigma_L(A)(Tx,(L^t)^{-1}\xi)$, which is
\eqref{euclidean:eq:affine-symbol}. If instead $A=\Op_R(b)$ with $b=\sigma_R(A)$, the right
symbol of the proof of \cref{euclidean:thm:composition}, i.e.\ $c(x,y,\xi)=b(y,\xi)$, then
$c_T(x,y,\eta)=b(Ty,M\eta)$ and $A_T=\Op_R(b(Ty,M\eta))$. Since
$\Op_R:S^m\to\Psido m$ is a bijection with inverse $\sigma_R$ (see after
\eqref{euclidean:eq:left-right}),
\begin{equation*}
  \sigma_R(A_T)(y,\xi)=\sigma_R(A)\bigl(Ty,(L^t)^{-1}\xi\bigr).
\end{equation*}

\emph{The hint.} One can also argue as suggested. For $f,h\in\cS$, substituting
$x=T^{-1}y$ twice,
\begin{equation*}
  \inner{A_Tf}h=\abs{\det L}^{-1}\inner{A(f\circ T^{-1})}{h\circ T^{-1}}
  =\abs{\det L}^{-1}\inner{f\circ T^{-1}}{A^*(h\circ T^{-1})}=\inner f{(A^*)_Th},
\end{equation*}
so $(A_T)^*=(A^*)_T$ by the uniqueness in \cref{euclidean:prop:adjoint}. Then
$\sigma_R(A_T)=\overline{\sigma_L((A_T)^*)}=\overline{\sigma_L((A^*)_T)}$, and
\eqref{euclidean:eq:affine-symbol} applied to $A^*$ gives the same formula.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:density-L2}}

\emph{Idea:} approximate in turn by simple functions, by indicators of finite unions of
cubes, and by smooth bumps; only the definition of the integral and the regularity of
Lebesgue measure are used. (The proof of Plancherel's theorem in \cref{ch:distributions}
quotes this fact, so we must not use Fourier analysis here.)

Since $\CIc(\R^n)\subset\cS(\R^n)\subset L^2(\R^n)$, it suffices to show that $\CIc$ is
dense. Let $u\in L^2$ and $\eta>0$. All approximations below are in the $L^2$ norm, and we
combine them with the triangle inequality.

\emph{Step 1: simple functions.} Write $u=u_1-u_2+i(u_3-u_4)$ with
$u_1=(\Re u)^+$, etc.; each $u_i\ge0$ lies in $L^2$. For $v\ge0$ in $L^2$ there are simple
functions $0\le s_k\uparrow v$ pointwise. Then $\abs{v-s_k}^2\le v^2$ is integrable and
tends to $0$ pointwise, so $\norm{v-s_k}_{L^2}\to0$ by dominated convergence. Writing
$s_k=\sum_ic_i\mathbf 1_{E_i}$ with $c_i>0$ and $E_i$ disjoint,
$c_i^2\abs{E_i}\le\int s_k^2\le\int v^2$, so each $E_i$ has finite measure. Thus finite
linear combinations of indicators $\mathbf 1_E$ with $\abs E<\infty$ are dense.

\emph{Step 2: cubes.} Let $\abs E<\infty$ and $\delta>0$. By outer regularity of Lebesgue
measure there is an open $O\supset E$ with $\abs O<\abs E+\delta$. Every open subset of
$\R^n$ is a countable disjoint union of half-open dyadic cubes $Q_k$; since
$\sum_k\abs{Q_k}=\abs O<\infty$, there is $K$ with $\sum_{k>K}\abs{Q_k}<\delta$. Let
$F=\bigcup_{k\le K}Q_k$. As $E\setminus F\subset O\setminus F$ and $F\setminus E\subset O\setminus E$,
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{\mathbf 1_E-\mathbf 1_F}_{L^2}^2=\abs{E\,\triangle\,F}\le\abs{O\setminus F}+\abs{O\setminus E}<2\delta.
\end{equation*}

\emph{Step 3: smoothing a cube.} Let $Q=\prod_l[a_l,b_l)$ and $0<\tau\le1$. Choose
$\phi_l\in\CIc(\R)$ with $0\le\phi_l\le1$, $\phi_l=1$ on $[a_l,b_l]$ and
$\supp\phi_l\subset(a_l-\tau,b_l+\tau)$, and let $\Phi(x)=\prod_l\phi_l(x_l)\in\CIc(\R^n)$.
Then $0\le\Phi\le1$, $\Phi=1$ on $Q$, and $\Phi=0$ outside
$Q_\tau=\prod_l(a_l-\tau,b_l+\tau)$, so
\begin{equation*}
  \norm{\Phi-\mathbf 1_Q}_{L^2}^2\le\abs{Q_\tau\setminus Q}
  =\prod_l(b_l-a_l+2\tau)-\prod_l(b_l-a_l)\longrightarrow0\quad(\tau\to0).
\end{equation*}
Combining the three steps, there is $\Phi\in\CIc(\R^n)$, a finite linear combination of
such products, with $\norm{u-\Phi}_{L^2}<\eta$. Hence $\CIc(\R^n)$, and a fortiori
$\cS(\R^n)$, is dense in $L^2(\R^n)$.

\subsection*{\Cref{euclidean:ex:S'-to-S}}

We use the topology of $\cSp(\R^n)$ fixed in \cref{ch:distributions}, the weak topology
(a basis of neighbourhoods of $0$ is given by the sets
$\{u;\abs{u(\phi_j)}<\eps,\ j=1,\dots,p\}$). \emph{Idea:} for this topology continuity
into $\cS$ is so strong that the operator has finite rank. A remark at the end treats the
strong topology, where the result is still true but has more content.

Let $T:\cSp(\R^n)\to\cS(\R^n)$ be linear and continuous. The sup norm $\norm\cdot_0$ is a
continuous norm on $\cS(\R^n)$, so $\{v;\norm v_0<1\}$ is a neighbourhood of $0$ and its
preimage under $T$ contains a weak neighbourhood
$\{u;\abs{u(\phi_j)}<\eps,\ j=1,\dots,p\}$ with $\phi_j\in\cS(\R^n)$. If $u(\phi_j)=0$ for
all $j$, then $tu$ lies in this neighbourhood for every $t>0$, so $\norm{Tu}_0<1/t$ for all
$t$, and $Tu=0$. Thus $T$ vanishes on the joint null space of the functionals
$\ev_j:u\mapsto u(\phi_j)$, and therefore factors through the linear map
$\ev=(\ev_1,\dots,\ev_p):\cSp\to\C^p$: there is a linear map $\Lambda:\ev(\cSp)\to\cS$
with $T=\Lambda\circ\ev$. Extend $\Lambda$ linearly to $\C^p$ and put
$\psi_j=\Lambda(e_j)\in\cS(\R^n)$. Then
\begin{equation*}
  Tu=\sum_{j=1}^pu(\phi_j)\,\psi_j,\qquad u\in\cSp(\R^n).
\end{equation*}
For $\psi\in\cS(\R^n)$ this gives $T\psi(x)=\int K(x,y)\psi(y)\,dy$ with
$K(x,y)=\sum_j\psi_j(x)\phi_j(y)$, and $K\in\cS(\R^{2n})$ since
$\norm{\psi_j\boxtimes\phi_j}_k\le\norm{\psi_j}_k\norm{\phi_j}_k$ (\cref{ch:distributions}).
As $T$ restricted to $\cS$ is a continuous map $\cS\to\cSp$, its Schwartz kernel is unique
(\cref{distributions:thm:kernel}), so the Schwartz kernel of $T$ is
$K\in\cS(\R^{2n})$.

\emph{Remark (strong topology).} If $\cSp$ carries the strong topology, there are
operators of infinite rank, e.g.\ $Tu=\sum_k2^{-k}c_k^{-1}u(\phi_k)\psi_k$ for suitable
normalizing constants. The statement remains true, by the following argument. A
continuous linear map from $\cSp$ with the strong topology is bounded on weakly bounded
sets (these are strongly bounded, by the uniform boundedness principle for the Fr\'echet
space $\cS$) and maps weakly convergent sequences to convergent sequences (weakly
convergent sequences in $\cSp$ converge strongly, as $\cS$ is a Montel space). Put
$K(x,y)=(T\delta_y)(x)$. Since $(\delta_{y+he_l}-\delta_y)/h\to(\phi\mapsto\pa_l\phi(y))$
weakly, and so on, $y\mapsto T\delta_y$ is a smooth map into $\cS$, and $K$ is smooth with
$\pa^\beta_yK(x,y)=T(\phi\mapsto\pa^\beta\phi(y))(x)$. The sets
$\{y^\gamma(\phi\mapsto\pa^\beta\phi(y));y\in\R^n\}$ are weakly bounded
($\abs{y^\gamma\pa^\beta\phi(y)}\le\norm\phi_{\abs\gamma+\abs\beta}$), so their images are
bounded in $\cS$, which gives
$\sup_{x,y}\abs{x^\alpha y^\gamma\pa^{\alpha'}_x\pa^\beta_yK}<\infty$, i.e.\
$K\in\cS(\R^{2n})$. Finally, Riemann sums $\sum_{\abs k\le j^2}j^{-n}\psi(k/j)\delta_{k/j}$
converge weakly to $\psi\in\cS$, and their images converge pointwise to
$\int K(\cdot,y)\psi(y)\,dy$, so $K$ is the kernel of $T$.
