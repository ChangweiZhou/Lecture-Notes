sections on the b-heat kernel, the eta invariant, and the heat proof of

closed-manifold Fredholm theory on a cylinder.  We then construct the

full parametrix.  Only after those tools are available do we return to the
APS spectral realization and the two index proofs.

\section{Elliptic Fredholm theory on closed manifolds}

The b-calculus is a boundary refinement of ordinary elliptic theory, so we
first record the closed-manifold argument that will later fail at the
cylindrical end. Let \(M\) be closed and let
\[
 P:C^\infty(M;E)\longrightarrow C^\infty(M;F)
\]
be a differential or classical pseudodifferential operator of order \(m\).
Its principal symbol
\[
 \sigma_m(P)(x,\xi):E_x\longrightarrow F_x
\]
is obtained by retaining the terms homogeneous of degree \(m\) in
\(\xi\). The operator is elliptic when this map is invertible for every
\(\xi\ne0\).

For every \(s\in\mathbb R\), an operator of order \(m\) extends boundedly
as
\[
 P:H^s(M;E)\longrightarrow H^{s-m}(M;F).
\]
Ellipticity permits the inverse symbol to be quantized and corrected
recursively.

\begin{theorem}[Elliptic parametrix]
If \(P\) is elliptic, there is \(Q\in\Psi^{-m}(M;F,E)\) such that
\[
 QP=I-R_L,\qquad PQ=I-R_R,
 \qquad R_L,R_R\in\Psi^{-\infty}(M).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(p\sim p_m+p_{m-1}+\cdots\) be the complete symbol of \(P\).
Choose \(q_{-m}=p_m^{-1}\) away from the zero section. If
\(q_{-m},\ldots,q_{-m-j+1}\) are known, the degree \(-j\) part of
\(q\# p-I\) is affine in the unknown \(q_{-m-j}\), with coefficient
\(p_m\). Multiplication by \(p_m^{-1}\) therefore determines
\(q_{-m-j}\) and removes that term. Borel summation produces a classical
symbol \(q\) for which \(q\# p-I\) has order \(-\infty\). Repeating on
the other side and applying a smoothing correction gives one \(Q\) with
both displayed identities.
\end{proof}

The logic of the construction is:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 P
 \arrow[r,"\sigma_m"]
 &p_m
 \arrow[r,"\text{invert}"]
 &p_m^{-1}
 \arrow[d,"\text{symbol recursion}"]\\
 \text{Fredholm}
 &I-\text{compact}\arrow[l,"\text{Atkinson}"']
 &Q\arrow[l,"\text{compose}"']
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\begin{theorem}[Closed elliptic Fredholm theorem]
For every \(s\), the map
\[
 P:H^s(M;E)\longrightarrow H^{s-m}(M;F)
\]
is Fredholm. Its kernel is smooth, its range is closed, and
\[
 \operatorname{coker}P\cong\ker P^*.
\]
If \(Pu\in H^{r-m}\), then \(u\in H^r\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The parametrix gives
\[
 u=QPu+R_Lu.
\]
This proves elliptic regularity because \(Q\) gains \(m\) derivatives and
\(R_L\) is smoothing. On a closed manifold, a smoothing operator factors
through \(H^{s+N}\hookrightarrow H^s\), which is compact by Rellich's
lemma. Thus \(P\) is invertible modulo compact operators and is Fredholm
by Atkinson's theorem. The orthogonal complement of its closed range is
\(\ker P^*\), giving the cokernel statement.
\end{proof}

The analytic index
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P=\dim\ker P-\dim\ker P^*
\]
is independent of \(s\) and constant in continuous families of elliptic
operators. A lower-order perturbation preserves the principal symbol and
hence the index.

\begin{lemma}[Fedosov trace formula]
Suppose \(Q\) is a parametrix for the Fredholm operator \(P\) and
\[
 S_L=I-QP,\qquad S_R=I-PQ
\]
are trace class. Then
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P=\Tr(S_L)-\Tr(S_R).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(G\) be the generalized inverse of \(P\), equal to the inverse from
\((\ker P)^\perp\) onto \(\operatorname{ran}P\) and zero on
\(\ker P^*\). If \(\Pi_0,\Pi_1\) are the orthogonal projections onto
\(\ker P,\ker P^*\), then
\[
 GP=I-\Pi_0,\qquad PG=I-\Pi_1.
\]
Hence
\(\operatorname{ind}P=\Tr(\Pi_0)-\Tr(\Pi_1)\).
Subtracting these identities from the identities for \(Q\) gives
\[
 (Q-G)P=\Pi_0-S_L,\qquad P(Q-G)=\Pi_1-S_R.
\]
Before using cyclicity we check that \(Q-G\) itself is trace class.
Since \(S_LG=G-QPG=G-Q(I-\Pi_1)\),
\[
 Q-G=Q\Pi_1-S_LG,
\]
a finite-rank operator plus a trace-class operator times a bounded one.
As \(P\) is bounded, \(\Tr((Q-G)P)=\Tr(P(Q-G))\), so
\[
 \Tr(S_L)-\Tr(S_R)
 =\Tr(\Pi_0)-\Tr(\Pi_1).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{discussion}
Both hypotheses in the closed theorem matter. Ellipticity produces
smoothing remainders; compactness of the base turns the Sobolev gain into
a compact map. On a cylinder, translations of a fixed bump remain bounded
but have no convergent subsequence. The b-calculus must therefore control
not only high frequency, through the principal symbol, but also escape to
infinity, through the normal operator.
\end{discussion}

\section{From the closed index theorem to the APS formula}
\label{sec:closed-to-APS}

Let $X$ be a compact Riemannian manifold with boundary $Y$, of even
dimension \(n=\dim X\); this \(n\) is fixed throughout. Assume that the metric and all bundle data are of product type on a
collar $[0,1)_u\times Y$. Let $E=E^+\oplus E^-$ be a graded Hermitian
Clifford module. Write the chiral operator as
\[
 P=D^+:C^\infty(X;E^+)\longrightarrow C^\infty(X;E^-)
\]
and the total odd self-adjoint operator as
\[
 \mathcal D=\begin{pmatrix}0&P^*\\P&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
On the collar we use the convention
\[
 P=c(du)(\partial_u+A^+),
 \qquad
 \mathcal D=c(du)(\partial_u+\mathcal A),
\]
where \(A^+\) is self-adjoint on \(E^+|_Y\) and
\[
 \mathcal A=\begin{pmatrix}A^+&0\\0&A^-\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 A^-=-c(du)A^+c(du)^{-1}.
\]
Thus \(c(du)\) carries the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(A^+\) to the
\(-\lambda\)-eigenspace of \(A^-\). The eta invariant in the APS formula
is always that of \(A^+\), not that of the total block operator
\(\mathcal A\), whose two spectra cancel.

\subsection{Conventions}\label{subsec:conventions}

\emph{Cylinder coordinate.} The collar coordinate \(u\geq0\) is extended
to \(u\leq0\) on the attached cylinder \((-\infty,0]_u\times Y\), and the
compactifying boundary defining function is
\[
 x=e^{u},\qquad u=\log x,\qquad x\partial_x=\partial_u,\qquad
 \frac{dx}x=du .
\]
The cylindrical end is \(u\to-\infty\), i.e.\ \(x\to0\). This is the only
cylinder convention used below; the letter \(t\) is reserved for heat time.

\emph{Characteristic forms.} Let \(R\) be the Riemannian curvature of
\(\nabla^{TX}\). The Clifford connection on \(E\) has curvature
\[
 F^E=R^S+F^{E/S},\qquad
 R^S(V,W)=\frac14\sum_{j,k}\langle R(V,W)e_j,e_k\rangle c(e_j)c(e_k),
\]
where \(R^S\) is the action of \(R\) through the spin representation and
the \emph{twisting curvature} \(F^{E/S}\) commutes with Clifford
multiplication. Locally \(E=S\otimes W\) with \(S\) the graded spinor
bundle, \(W=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{Cl}}(S,E)\), and
\(F^{E/S}=1\otimes F^W\); the \emph{relative supertrace}
\(\operatorname{Str}_{E/S}\) on \(\operatorname{End}_{\mathrm{Cl}}(E)\) is
the supertrace on \(W\), and is globally defined. We normalize
\[
 \widehat A(TX,\nabla^{TX})
 ={\det}^{1/2}\!\left(\frac{R/4\pi i}{\sinh(R/4\pi i)}\right),
 \qquad
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S,\nabla^{E/S})
 =\operatorname{Str}_{E/S}\exp\!\left(-\frac{F^{E/S}}{2\pi i}\right),
\]
so that the factors of \(2\pi i\) are already included; with the
normalization of Berline--Getzler--Vergne, which omits them, every
index formula below acquires the factor \((2\pi i)^{-n/2}\). For the spin
Dirac operator twisted by \(W\), \(\operatorname{ch}(E/S)=\operatorname{ch}(W)\).
We write \(\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\) when the connections are
understood, and \([\,\cdot\,]_{[n]}\) for the degree-\(n\) component.

\emph{Calculus vocabulary.} The small calculus \(\Psi_b^m\) is defined in
Section~\ref{sec:small-calculus}; a \emph{suspended} operator on \(Y\) is
a translation-invariant operator on \(\mathbb R\times Y\), equivalently,
after Fourier transform in \(\mathbb R\), a parameter-dependent family on
\(Y\) (Section~\ref{sec:normal-op}). An \emph{index set} is a discrete set
\(\mathcal E\subset\mathbb C\times\mathbb N_0\) with
\(\operatorname{Re}z\to\infty\) along it, closed under
\((z,k)\mapsto(z,k-1),(z+1,k)\); an \emph{index family} assigns an index
set to each boundary hypersurface, and a function is polyhomogeneous with
that index family if it has expansions
\(\sum_{(z,k)\in\mathcal E}\rho^z(\log\rho)^ka_{z,k}\) at each face. The
\emph{full calculus} consists of the sums of small-calculus operators and
operators whose kernels are polyhomogeneous on \(X_b^2\) with prescribed
index families; an operator is \emph{residual} if its kernel is
polyhomogeneous with positive index sets at \(\mathrm{lb}\), \(\mathrm{rb}\)
and vanishes at \(\mathrm{ff}\), so that its normal operator is zero
\cite[Chapters~4--5]{Melrose}. A \emph{generalized b-Laplacian} is a
second-order b-differential operator \(L\) with
\({}^b\sigma_2(L)(\zeta)=|\zeta|^2_g\operatorname{Id}\) for a b-metric
\(g\); it is of \emph{product type} if near \(\partial X\) it equals
\(-(x\partial_x)^2+L_\partial\) with \(L_\partial\) independent of \(x\),
as \(\mathcal D^2\) is under the present assumptions.

\subsection{Closed manifolds}

When $\partial X=\varnothing$, the heat operators are smoothing and
trace class. Define
\[
 Q_t=P^*\int_0^t e^{-sPP^*}\,ds.
\]
Functional calculus and the intertwining relation
$P^*e^{-sPP^*}=e^{-sP^*P}P^*$ give
\[
 PQ_t=I-e^{-tPP^*},
 \qquad
 Q_tP=I-e^{-tP^*P}.
\]
Fedosov's trace formula therefore yields the McKean--Singer identity
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P
 =\Tr(e^{-tP^*P})-\Tr(e^{-tPP^*})
 =\operatorname{Str}(e^{-t\mathcal D^2})
\]
for every $t>0$. The local heat expansion then gives
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P
 =\int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX,\nabla^{TX})
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S,\nabla^{E/S})
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]

\subsection{The cylindrical end}

Attach $(-\infty,0]_u\times Y$ to $X$ and extend \(P\) and
\(\mathcal D\) by their product formulas. The resulting complete manifold
is denoted $X_\infty$. The
operator
\[
 D^+:
 H^1(X_\infty,E^+)
 \longrightarrow
 L^2(X_\infty,E^-)
\]
is Fredholm precisely when $A^+$ is invertible. Expand a section in an
eigenbasis of \(A^+\). On the mode with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the operator
on the end reduces to \(\partial_u+\lambda\). A spectral gap gives uniform
exponential estimates.
If \(0\in\operatorname{spec}(A^+)\), translates of a \emph{fixed} cutoff
in a zero mode do not suffice: \(D^+\) applied to each translate has the
same nonzero norm. Instead let \(\phi_0\in\ker A^+\) be a unit
vector, fix \(0\neq\chi\in C_c^\infty((-1,-\tfrac12))\), and put
\[
 w_R(u,y)=R^{-1/2}\chi(u/R)\phi_0(y),\qquad R\geq1 .
\]
Then \(\lVert w_R\rVert_{L^2}=\lVert\chi\rVert_{L^2}\),
\(\lVert w_R\rVert_{H^1}\) is bounded,
\(D^+w_R=R^{-3/2}c(du)\chi'(u/R)\phi_0\) has norm
\(R^{-1}\lVert\chi'\rVert_{L^2}\to0\), and \(w_R\rightharpoonup0\)
because the supports \(\{-R<u<-R/2\}\) escape. If \(D^+\) were Fredholm,
a parametrix would give \(w=QD^+w+Kw\) with \(Q\) bounded and \(K\)
compact, so \(\lVert w_R\rVert\leq C\lVert D^+w_R\rVert+\lVert Kw_R\rVert\to0\),
a contradiction. Thus \(\{w_R\}\) is a Weyl sequence at zero and
\(D^+\) is not Fredholm.

Although $D^+$ is Fredholm when $A^+$ is invertible, the two heat
operators are not separately trace class: their kernels are
translation invariant to leading order on the infinite cylinder. Their
graded difference is handled by the $b$-trace introduced below.

For $\Re s$ large, define the eta function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta-def}
 \eta_{A^+}(s)=\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A^+)\setminus\{0\}}
   \operatorname{sgn}(\lambda)|\lambda|^{-s}.
\end{equation}
Since \(\int_0^\infty t^{(s-1)/2}\lambda e^{-t\lambda^2}dt
=\Gamma(\tfrac{s+1}2)\operatorname{sgn}(\lambda)|\lambda|^{-s}\), it has the
Mellin representation
\begin{equation}\label{eq:eta-mellin}
 \eta_{A^+}(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)}
 \int_0^\infty t^{(s-1)/2}\Tr(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2})\,dt.
\end{equation}
The right side extends meromorphically and is regular at $s=0$; this is
proved in Section~\ref{sec:eta} (Theorem~\ref{thm:eta-regular}). With
$h=\dim\ker A^+$, the Atiyah--Patodi--Singer formula \cite{APS} is
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D_{\mathrm{APS}}^+
 =\int_X
  \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
  -\frac{\eta_{A^+}(0)+h}{2}.
\]
Here \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) is \(D^+\) acting on sections \(\phi\) that
satisfy the APS spectral boundary condition
\(\Pi_{\geq0}(A^+)(\phi|_Y)=0\); this realization is defined precisely in
Section~\ref{sec:APS-realization}.
When $A^+$ is invertible, $h=0$, and this equals the $L^2$ index on
$X_\infty$.

\begin{question}
Why does attaching a cylinder help if the resulting manifold is
noncompact?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The cylinder does not remove the analytic difficulty; it puts that
difficulty into a translation-invariant form. On the compact manifold
with boundary, the APS condition is the nonlocal projection determined by
the spectrum of \(A^+\). On the cylinder, the same condition is encoded by
square integrability. Indeed, on an \(A^+\)-eigenvector with eigenvalue
\(\lambda\), the equation \((\partial_u+A^+)\phi=0\) has the solution
\(e^{-\lambda u}\phi(0)\), which decays as \(u\to-\infty\) exactly when
\(\lambda<0\): one spectral half gives decaying solutions on the end.
Thus the APS projection and the \(L^2\) condition select the same boundary
data. The remaining translation-invariant part is what the normal operator
and the \(b\)-trace measure.
\end{answer}

\section{Real blow-up and the b-double space}

A smooth $n$-manifold with corners is locally modelled on
\[
 \mathbb R^{n,k}=[0,\infty)^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k},
 \qquad 0\leq k\leq n,
\]
with smooth transition maps in the usual extension sense. A boundary
hypersurface is a connected codimension-one boundary face; following
\cite[Lecture~2, \S1]{MelroseBlowup}, every boundary hypersurface \(H\) is
required to be embedded, i.e.\ \(H=\{\rho_H=0\}\) for some
\(\rho_H\in C^\infty(X)\) with \(\rho_H\geq0\) and \(d\rho_H\neq0\) on
\(H\). At a point
$p$, the codimension is the number of independent boundary defining
functions that vanish at $p$.

Let $S\subset X$ be an embedded boundary face. Its real blow-up
$[X;S]$ replaces $S$ by the inward-pointing spherical normal bundle
$S^+NS$. The blow-down map
\[
 \beta:[X;S]\longrightarrow X
\]
is a diffeomorphism away from the new boundary hypersurface and
collapses that hypersurface onto $S$.

For a compact manifold $X$ with connected boundary, the $b$-double space is
\[
 X_b^2=[X^2;\partial X\times\partial X].
\]
If \(\partial X\) has components \(H_1,\ldots,H_N\), one blows up only the
mutually disjoint products \(H_j\times H_j\), so that
\(X_b^2=[X^2;H_1\times H_1;\ldots;H_N\times H_N]\), independently of the
order \cite[(2.28), (2.29)]{MelroseBlowup}. Its boundary hypersurfaces are the front face $\mathrm{ff}$ and the
lifts $\mathrm{lb}$ and $\mathrm{rb}$ of
$\partial X\times X$ and $X\times\partial X$. The lifted diagonal
$\Delta_b$ is the closure of the interior diagonal.

The geometry is summarized by the blow-down square
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 \Delta_b \arrow[r,hook] \arrow[d,"\beta|_{\Delta_b}"']
   & X_b^2 \arrow[d,"\beta"] \\
 \Delta \arrow[r,hook]
   & X^2 .
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\beta(\mathrm{ff})=\partial X\times\partial X.
\]
The blow-down is an isomorphism away from
\(\partial X\times\partial X\). Over that corner, the front face records
the ratio of the two normal variables and
therefore remembers how the corner is approached.

Near $\partial X$, choose product coordinates $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ on
the two factors. Two useful projective coordinate systems are
\[
 (x,s=x'/x,y,y')\quad\text{and}\quad
 (s'=x/x',x',y,y').
\]
In the first chart, $\mathrm{ff}=\{x=0\}$ and
$\mathrm{rb}=\{s=0\}$; in the second,
$\mathrm{ff}=\{x'=0\}$ and $\mathrm{lb}=\{s'=0\}$.
Near the interior of the front face one may instead use
\[
 r=x,\qquad z=\log(x/x'),\qquad y,\qquad Y=y-y',
\]
so that $\Delta_b=\{z=0,\;Y=0\}$.

\begin{question}
Why not work directly on \(X^2\)?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The ratio \(x/x'\) has no limit at \((x,x')=(0,0)\) on \(X^2\). Kernels of
translation-invariant operators on the cylinder depend on
\(\log(x/x')\), so they cannot be smooth functions of \(x,x'\) at that
corner. Blow-up replaces the corner by the set of limiting ratios. In the
projective chart \(x'=sx\), the same kernel is a function of
\(-\log s\), which is smooth in the interior of the front face and has
controlled asymptotics as \(s\to0\) or \(s\to\infty\). The singularity
has not been discarded; it has been separated into geometrically distinct
boundary faces.
\end{answer}

\subsection{A one-dimensional model}

On the full line \(\mathbb R_u\), consider
\[
 P=-\partial_u^2+\epsilon^2,\qquad \epsilon>0.
\]
Near the end \(u=-\infty\), use the compactifying coordinate \(x=e^u\)
of Section~\ref{subsec:conventions}.
Then
\[
 \partial_u=x\partial_x,\qquad du=\frac{dx}{x}.
\]
With respect to \(du'=dx'/x'\), the inverse of \(P\) has kernel
\[
 K_{P^{-1}}(x,x')
 =\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
   e^{-\epsilon|\log(x/x')|}
 =\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
 \begin{cases}
  (x'/x)^\epsilon,&x'\leq x,\\
  (x/x')^\epsilon,&x\leq x'.
 \end{cases}
\]
On $X_b^2$ this kernel is conormal to $\Delta_b$, smooth across the
front face away from $\Delta_b$, and vanishes to order $\epsilon$ at
$\mathrm{lb}$ and $\mathrm{rb}$. This model explains why the
$b$-calculus resolves the simultaneous limit $x,x'\to0$ while retaining
the translation-invariant variable $\log(x/x')$; see
Figure~\ref{fig:model-kernel}.

\subsection{General and iterated blow-ups}

The construction is not limited to a corner of a double space. A
\emph{p-submanifold} \(S\) of a manifold with corners \(Z\) is a
submanifold that, in suitable product coordinates
\[
 (x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_\ell),
\]
is given by the vanishing of a subset of the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates.
Its inward spherical normal bundle is therefore well defined, and the real
blow-up
\[
 [Z;S]=(Z\setminus S)\sqcup S^+NS
\]
has a canonical smooth structure.

In the model \(Z=[0,\infty)^2\), \(S=\{(0,0)\}\), the two projective charts
are
\[
 U_x:(x,s=x'/x),\qquad
 U_{x'}:(s'=x/x',x').
\]
On their overlap,
\[
 s'=s^{-1},\qquad x'=sx.
\]
These transition functions are smooth where \(s,s'>0\), and the front
face is \(x=0\) in the first chart and \(x'=0\) in the second; see
Figure~\ref{fig:proj-charts}.

\begin{proposition}[Coordinate invariance]
The blow-up \([Z;S]\), its front face, and its blow-down map are
independent up to canonical diffeomorphism of the product coordinates
used in the construction.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A product-coordinate change preserving \(S\) has, in the normal
variables, the form
\[
 \widetilde z=A(w)z+O(|z|^2),
\]
where \(A(w)\) is invertible and preserves the inward normal cone.
Writing \(z=r\omega\) gives
\[
 \widetilde r
 =r\,|A(w)\omega+O(r)|,\qquad
 \widetilde\omega
 =\frac{A(w)\omega+O(r)}
 {|A(w)\omega+O(r)|}.
\]
Both expressions are smooth down to \(r=0\); the second restricts there
to the induced map on the spherical normal bundle. Hence every allowed
coordinate change lifts smoothly, and the lifted changes satisfy the
same cocycle identities.
\end{proof}

Iterated blow-ups are used for \(X_b^3\) and \(X_{b,h}^2\). The
commutation results below are those of
\cite[Lecture~2, \S\S3, 5]{MelroseBlowup}. They concern \emph{joint}
p-submanifolds \(S,T\): near each point of \(S\cap T\) there is a single
adapted coordinate system in which both are coordinate subspaces. For such
a pair the lift of \(T\) to \([Z;S]\) (the preimage \(\beta^{-1}(T)\) if
\(T\subset S\), otherwise the closure of \(\beta^{-1}(T\setminus S)\)) is
again a p-submanifold, so both iterated blow-ups \([Z;S;T]\) and
\([Z;T;S]\) are defined. They are canonically diffeomorphic when \(S\) and
\(T\) are disjoint, transversal, or nested (\(S\subset T\) or
\(T\subset S\)), and not otherwise. The case that requires care is two
centers that intersect but are neither transversal nor nested: then
\([Z;S;T]\) and \([Z;T;S]\) are in general different manifolds. The remedy
used below is to blow up the intersection first: after the blow-up of
\(S\cap T\) the lifts of \(S\) and \(T\) are disjoint, so
\([Z;S\cap T;S;T]=[Z;S\cap T;T;S]\) \cite[(2.15)]{MelroseBlowup}.

\begin{discussion}
The order is geometric, not cosmetic. In \(X^3\) the three codimension-two
corners \(\partial X\times\partial X\times X\),
\(\partial X\times X\times\partial X\), and
\(X\times\partial X\times\partial X\) meet pairwise in the triple corner
\((\partial X)^3\), which has codimension three rather than four, so they
do not meet transversally, and none contains another. Blowing up
\((\partial X)^3\) first makes their lifts disjoint; they can then be
blown up in any order, which gives \(X_b^3\)
\cite[(2.31)]{MelroseBlowup}. The result is symmetric under permutation of
the three factors, so the three lifted projections to \(X_b^2\) are on an
equal footing.
\end{discussion}

\section{The b-tangent and b-cotangent bundles}

Let $\mathcal V_b(X)$ be the Lie algebra of smooth vector fields tangent
to every boundary face. In local coordinates
\[
 (x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_{n-k})
 \in[0,\infty)^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k},
\]
it is the locally free $C^\infty(X)$-module generated by
\[
 x_1\partial_{x_1},\ldots,x_k\partial_{x_k},
 \partial_{y_1},\ldots,\partial_{y_{n-k}}.
\]
Consequently there is a vector bundle \({}^bTX\to X\) with
\[
 C^\infty(X;{}^bTX)=\mathcal V_b(X).
\]
The natural anchor \({}^bTX\to TX\) is an isomorphism in the interior
but drops rank at the boundary.

The dual bundle \({}^bT^*X\) has local frame
\[
 \frac{dx_1}{x_1},\ldots,\frac{dx_k}{x_k},
 dy_1,\ldots,dy_{n-k}.
\]
A positive $b$-density is locally
\[
 a(x,y)\left|
 \frac{dx_1}{x_1}\cdots\frac{dx_k}{x_k}
 dy_1\cdots dy_{n-k}\right|,
 \qquad a>0.
\]
Under the cylindrical variables $x_j=e^{u_j}$, this is the ordinary
product density $a\,|du\,dy|$.

\section{b-differential operators and their symbols}

The algebra $\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)$ consists of differential
operators of order at most $m$ generated over $C^\infty(X)$ by
$\mathcal V_b(X)$. Locally,
\[
 P=\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq m}
 a_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)
 (x\partial_x)^\alpha\partial_y^\beta.
\]
For manifolds with corners, $(x\partial_x)^\alpha$ denotes
$\prod_j(x_j\partial_{x_j})^{\alpha_j}$.

The filtration by order satisfies
\[
 [\operatorname{Diff}_b^m,\operatorname{Diff}_b^\ell]
 \subseteq\operatorname{Diff}_b^{m+\ell-1}.
\]
It follows that the associated graded algebra is canonically
$\operatorname{Sym}({}^bTX)$. Thus the principal symbol is the
coordinate-independent homogeneous bundle map
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P):{}^bT^*X\setminus0
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(\pi^*E,\pi^*F).
\]
In the coordinates above,
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P)(x,y;\xi,\eta)
 =\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=m}
   a_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)(i\xi)^\alpha(i\eta)^\beta.
\]

The symbol is multiplicative:
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{m+\ell}(PQ)
 ={}^b\sigma_m(P)\,{}^b\sigma_\ell(Q),
\]
and fits into the exact sequence
\[
 0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_b^{m-1}(X;E,F)
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)
 \xrightarrow{{}^b\sigma_m}
 \mathcal P^m
 ({}^bT^*X;\operatorname{Hom}(\pi^*E,\pi^*F))
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Here \(\mathcal P^m\) denotes smooth fibrewise homogeneous polynomials of
degree \(m\).

\begin{definition}
An operator $P\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)$ is $b$-elliptic if
\({}^b\sigma_m(P)(\zeta)\) is invertible for every nonzero
$\zeta\in{}^bT^*X$.
\end{definition}

Fix a positive $b$-density and Hermitian metrics on the bundles. If
$V\in\mathcal V_b(X)$, integration by parts gives
\[
 V^*=-V-\operatorname{div}_b(V).
\]
Because $\operatorname{div}_b(V)\in C^\infty(X)$, formal adjoints
preserve the calculus:
\[
 P\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 P^*\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;F,E).
\]
This also gives
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P^*)={}^b\sigma_m(P)^*.
\]
\section{Conormal functions and boundary weights}

Let \(X\) be a compact manifold with corners, with boundary hypersurfaces
\(H_1,\ldots,H_N\) and defining functions
\(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_N\).

\begin{definition}
The space of bounded conormal functions is
\[
 \mathcal A^0(X)
 =
 \{u\in L^\infty(X^\circ):V_1\cdots V_k u\in L^\infty(X^\circ)
 \text{ for all }V_j\in\mathcal V_b(X)\}.
\]
For a multiweight \(\gamma=(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_N)\), set
\[
 \rho^\gamma\mathcal A^0(X)
 =
 \rho_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots\rho_N^{\gamma_N}\mathcal A^0(X).
\]
\end{definition}

This definition is independent of the chosen defining functions. Indeed, a
second defining function has the form \(\widetilde\rho_j=a_j\rho_j\), where
\(a_j\) is smooth and positive, and multiplication by \(a_j^{\gamma_j}\)
preserves \(\mathcal A^0(X)\).

Conormality records uniform control under vector fields tangent to every
boundary face. It is weaker than smoothness. Powers of defining functions
belong to the corresponding weighted conormal classes. In contrast,
\(\log\rho_j\notin\mathcal A^0(X)\) because it is unbounded; logarithms
belong only to broader logarithmic or suitably weighted conormal classes.

\begin{definition}
For \(\epsilon>0\), write
\[
 \mathcal A^{0,\epsilon}(X)
 =
 \mathcal C^\infty(X)+\rho^\epsilon\mathcal A^0(X),
 \qquad
 \rho^\epsilon=\prod_{j=1}^N\rho_j^\epsilon.
\]
Thus \(u\in\mathcal A^{0,\epsilon}(X)\) has a smooth boundary value and a
conormal remainder that vanishes at every boundary hypersurface.
\end{definition}

The same notation applies to sections of a smooth vector bundle after
choosing local trivializations; the resulting space is independent of those
trivializations.

\section{Conormal distributions and the small b-calculus}
\label{sec:small-calculus}

Let \(Z\subset M\) be an embedded submanifold of codimension \(k\). In local
coordinates \((x,y)\), with \(Z=\{x=0\}\), a classical conormal distribution
has the form
\[
 u(x,y)
 =
 (2\pi)^{-k}\int_{\mathbb R^k}e^{ix\cdot\xi}a(y,\xi)\,d\xi,
\]
modulo a smooth term, where \(a\) is a classical symbol. The symbol order,
with the standard dimension shift, defines the conormal order \(m\) and the
space \(I^m(M,Z)\). This description is invariant under coordinate changes.

For vector bundles \(E,F\to X\), let
\({}^b\Omega_R\) denote the lifted right b-density bundle on \(X_b^2\).
Set
\[
 \mathcal H_{E,F}
 =
 \operatorname{Hom}(E,F)\otimes{}^b\Omega_R.
\]

\begin{definition}
The small b-pseudodifferential calculus is
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F)
 =
 \left\{
 A:
 K_A\in
 I^m\!\left(
 X_b^2,\Delta_b;
 \mathcal H_{E,F}
 \right),
 \quad
 K_A\text{ vanishes to infinite order at }\mathrm{lb}\cup\mathrm{rb}
 \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The convention relating \(m\) to the conormal order depends on the density
normalization; throughout these notes, \(m\) is the operator order. The
lifted diagonal \(\Delta_b\) meets the front face transversely, so restricting
the kernel to the front face gives another conormal distribution.

Every b-differential operator of order \(m\) belongs to
\(\Psi_b^m(X)\): its Schwartz kernel is a finite sum of derivatives of the
delta distribution on \(\Delta_b\). Operators in the small calculus act
continuously
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F):
 \mathcal C^\infty(X;E)\longrightarrow\mathcal C^\infty(X;F)
\]
and preserve the space \(\dot{\mathcal C}^\infty(X)\) of sections vanishing
to infinite order at the boundary.

\section{Composition in the b-calculus}

Composition is most transparent on the b-triple space. Start with \(X^3\)
and blow up the triple corner and the lifted pairwise corners in increasing
order of dimension \cite[(2.31)]{MelroseBlowup}. The resulting manifold
with corners \(X_b^3\) has
b-fibrations
\[
 \pi_{12},\pi_{23},\pi_{13}:X_b^3\longrightarrow X_b^2
\]
lifting the three coordinate projections.

The simplest instance of this phenomenon is already visible on the double
space.  The left projection \(X^2\to X\), \((x,x')\mapsto x\), lifts to a
smooth map \(\pi_L:X_b^2\to X\) with \(\pi_L^*x=\rho_{\mathrm{lb}}
\rho_{\mathrm{ff}}\) for suitable defining functions.  Its fibre over
\(x>0\) is a copy of \(X\), while its fibre over \(x=0\) is
\(\mathrm{lb}\cup\mathrm{ff}\), which has a corner; so \(\pi_L\) is not a
fibration.  It is, however, a b-fibration: it is a b-submersion, and no
boundary hypersurface is mapped into a corner of the base; see
Figure~\ref{fig:bfibration}.

Their roles in composition are displayed by

\begin{theorem}[Small b-composition]
If \(A\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) and
\(B\in\Psi_b^{m'}(X;G,E)\), then
\[
 K_{A\circ B}
 =
 (\pi_{13})_*
 \bigl(\pi_{12}^*K_A\;\pi_{23}^*K_B\bigr).
\]
The lifted conormal sets meet cleanly, and the vanishing at the left and
right faces makes the push-forward integrable. The pull-back, product, and
push-forward theorems for conormal distributions therefore give
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\circ\Psi_b^{m'}(X;G,E)
 \subseteq
 \Psi_b^{m+m'}(X;G,F).
\]
The leading oscillatory amplitudes multiply, so
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{m+m'}(A\circ B)
 =
 {}^b\sigma_m(A)\,{}^b\sigma_{m'}(B).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof architecture; triple-space theorem quoted]
The displayed push-forward formula follows from construction of the
b-triple space. Its proof requires that the three projections be
b-fibrations, clean pull-back and product of the lifted conormal
distributions, and the push-forward theorem with the full index-family
integrability conditions. These are the composition theorem of the
b-calculus \cite{Melrose}; they are not consequences of ordinary Fubini
alone.

To see the operator order once that theorem is available, work near the lifted diagonal and
write the two kernels as oscillatory integrals. Their product has amplitude
of order \(m+m'\). Stationary phase in the intermediate variable identifies
the two covectors and produces the usual symbol product. Away from the
lifted diagonal, at least one factor is smooth; the push-forward is then
smooth. At every boundary face, the exponent contributed by the product is
the sum of the pulled-back exponents minus the fibre-density exponent. The
infinite-order side-face vanishing in the small calculus places that sum
strictly above the integrability threshold.
\end{proof}

The residual part of the full calculus allows polyhomogeneous expansions at
the boundary faces. In the positive-weight case used below, products remain
integrable and the index sets combine under the same pull-back and
push-forward formula. Precise index-set bookkeeping is needed only when a
leading exponent reaches an integrability threshold.

\section{Parametrices in the small calculus}

Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic. Choose
\(Q_0\in\Psi_b^{-m}(X;F,E)\) with
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{-m}(Q_0)={}^b\sigma_m(P)^{-1}.
\]
Then
\[
 P Q_0=I-R_1,\qquad R_1\in\Psi_b^{-1}(X;F).
\]
The symbolic Neumann construction successively removes the homogeneous
terms of \(R_1\). Borel summation produces
\(Q\in\Psi_b^{-m}(X;F,E)\) such that
\[
 P Q=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L,
 \qquad R_R,R_L\in\Psi_b^{-\infty}(X).
\]

This is a symbolic parametrix, not yet a Fredholm inverse. A residual kernel
in the small b-calculus need not be compact on an unweighted b-Sobolev
space: after passage to the cylindrical coordinate \(u=\log x\), it may
retain translation-invariant behavior as \(u\to-\infty\). The normal operator,
introduced next, detects precisely this missing boundary condition.

\begin{question}
Why is a smooth residual kernel not automatically compact?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
Compactness on a compact manifold follows from smoothing plus Rellich's
lemma. The \(b\)-metric turns a boundary collar into an infinite cylinder,
so translations can escape every compact set. If a residual operator has a
nonzero translation-invariant limit \(K_\infty(u-u',y,y')\), choose a fixed
compactly supported section \(\phi\) on the cylinder and translate it to
\(\phi_j(u,y)=\phi(u+j,y)\). The sequence \(\phi_j\) is bounded, tends weakly
to zero, and has no convergent subsequence, while \(K\phi_j\) is
asymptotic to the corresponding translate of \(K_\infty\phi\). Hence
\(K\) is not compact unless its boundary model vanishes.
\end{answer}


\section{The normal operator and indicial family}
\label{sec:normal-op}

The principal b-symbol controls high-frequency behavior, but it does not
control escape along the cylindrical end. The missing invariant is the
restriction of the lifted kernel to the front face.

\begin{definition}
For \(A\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\), the normal operator \(N(A)\) is the
translation-invariant operator on the inward normal cylinder of
\(\partial X\) obtained by restricting the lifted kernel \(K_A\) to the
front face.
\end{definition}

After choosing a boundary defining function \(x\) and writing
\(u=\log x\), the interior of the front face is modeled by
\(\partial X\times\partial X\times\mathbb R_z\), \(z=\log(x/x')=u-u'\).
Thus \(N(A)\) is a \emph{suspended} pseudodifferential operator on
\(\partial X\): an operator on \(\mathbb R_u\times\partial X\) that commutes
with translations in \(u\), is pseudodifferential of order \(m\), and has
a kernel that, away from \(z=0\), is smooth and rapidly decreasing as
\(|z|\to\infty\). The space of these operators is denoted
\(\Psi_{\mathrm{sus}}^m(\partial X)\). The normal map is
multiplicative:
\[
 N(AB)=N(A)N(B).
\]
It follows either from restriction of the composition formula to the front
face or from Fourier transformation in \(u\).  Figure~\ref{fig:normal-op}
shows the front face opened out to the line \(\mathbb R_z\).

For a b-differential operator
\[
 P=\sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(x,y)(x\partial_x)^j\partial_y^\alpha,
\]
freezing the coefficients at \(x=0\) gives
\[
 N(P)=
 \sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(0,y)\,\partial_u^j\partial_y^\alpha.
\]

\begin{definition}
The indicial family of \(P\) is
\[
 I(P,\lambda)
 =
 \left.x^{-i\lambda}P x^{i\lambda}\right|_{\partial X}
 =
 \sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(0,y)(i\lambda)^j\partial_y^\alpha.
\]
For a b-pseudodifferential operator, \(I(A,\lambda)\) is the Fourier
transform of the convolution kernel \(k_A(z)\) of \(N(A)\) in the
translation variable \(z=\log(x/x')\), with
\[
 \widehat f(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-iz\lambda}f(z)\,dz,
 \qquad
 f(z)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{iz\lambda}\widehat f(\lambda)\,d\lambda .
\]
This sign makes \(N(A)x^{i\lambda}=I(A,\lambda)x^{i\lambda}\), consistent
with the differential case.
\end{definition}

For \(A\in\Psi_b^m\) in the small calculus, \(I(A,\lambda)\) is an
\emph{entire} family of pseudodifferential operators of order \(m\) on
\(\partial X\). Indeed, infinite-order vanishing of \(K_A\) at
\(\mathrm{lb}\) and \(\mathrm{rb}\) means that \(k_A(z)\) is
\(O(e^{-N|z|})\) for every \(N\) away from \(z=0\) (in the projective
chart, \(s=x'/x=e^{-z}\) and \(\mathrm{rb}=\{s=0\}\)), so the Fourier
integral converges for all complex \(\lambda\). On every strip
\(|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\leq C\) the restricted family is a suspended
(parameter-dependent) family of order \(m\). For differential operators it
is polynomial in \(\lambda\). Holomorphy only in a strip, or
meromorphy, occurs for operators of the full calculus, whose kernels have
finite-order expansions at the side faces.  For instance, if the kernel is
\(O(\rho_{\mathrm{lb}}^\alpha)\) and \(O(\rho_{\mathrm{rb}}^\alpha)\) with
\(\alpha>0\), then near the front face \(\rho_{\mathrm{lb}}=x/x'=e^{z}\) as
\(z\to-\infty\) and \(\rho_{\mathrm{rb}}=x'/x=e^{-z}\) as \(z\to+\infty\), so
\(k_A(z)=O(e^{-\alpha|z|})\) away from \(z=0\).  Since
\(|e^{-iz\lambda}|=e^{z\operatorname{Im}\lambda}\), the Fourier integral
then converges, and \(I(A,\lambda)\) is holomorphic, at least in the strip
\(|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|<\alpha\); see Figure~\ref{fig:strip}.

If \(x'=e^\phi x\), then
\[
 I_{x'}(A,\lambda)
 =e^{-i\lambda\phi_0}I_x(A,\lambda)e^{i\lambda\phi_0},
 \qquad \phi_0=\phi|_{\partial X}.
\]
Thus the change is a \(\lambda\)-dependent conjugation by boundary
multiplication operators, not a scalar unless \(\phi_0\) is constant.
Invertibility is nevertheless intrinsic. Multiplicativity gives
\[
 I(AB,\lambda)=I(A,\lambda)I(B,\lambda).
\]

The two symbol maps control complementary regimes:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 \Psi_b^m(X)
   \arrow[r,"{}^b\!\sigma_m"]
   \arrow[d,"N"']
 & S^{[m]}({}^bT^*X) \\
 \Psi_{\mathrm{sus}}^m(\partial X)
   \arrow[r,"\mathcal F_t"']
 & \{I(A,\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb R} .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
The principal symbol sees large covectors at a fixed point. The indicial
family sees bounded frequency while the base point escapes down the
cylinder. Full ellipticity requires invertibility in both regimes.

\begin{discussion}
This is why the normal operator is often called a second symbol. It is not
lower-order information: a smoothing \(b\)-operator has zero principal
symbol but may have a nonzero normal operator, and that nonzero boundary
model can destroy compactness.
\end{discussion}

For a product b-Dirac operator
\[
 D=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+A_{\partial}),
\]
one has
\[
 I(D,\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A_{\partial}).
\]
Consequently \(D\) is invertible at the boundary on the unweighted line
precisely when the tangential operator \(A_{\partial}\) is invertible.

\section{Weighted b-Sobolev spaces}

Fix a positive b-density and a positive b-Laplacian. For
\(s\in\mathbb R\), the b-Sobolev space \(H_b^s(X;E)\) is defined by
b-derivatives for integral \(s\), and by the b-pseudodifferential calculus
for arbitrary \(s\). Near the boundary this is the ordinary Sobolev space
in the variables \((u,y)=(\log x,y)\).

For \(\delta\in\mathbb R\), set
\[
 x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 =
 \{x^\delta v:v\in H_b^s(X;E)\}.
\]
A b-operator of order \(m\) acts continuously as
\[
 P:x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 \longrightarrow
 x^\delta H_b^{s-m}(X;F).
\]
Conjugation reduces this mapping to the unweighted one:
\[
 x^{-\delta}Px^\delta:
 H_b^s(X;E)\longrightarrow H_b^{s-m}(X;F).
\]

With the convention \(x^{i\lambda}\) used above, the weight \(x^\delta\)
corresponds to the Mellin line
\[
 \operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta.
\]

Indeed, write \(\lambda=\tau-i\delta\). Then
\(x^{i\lambda}=x^{\delta}x^{i\tau}\). Thus conjugating by \(x^\delta\)
moves the real Fourier line for the cylindrical variable to
\(\operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta\); no sign convention is hidden.

\begin{definition}
A b-elliptic operator \(P\) is fully elliptic at weight \(\delta\) if
\[
 I(P,\lambda)\text{ is invertible whenever }
 \operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta.
\]
\end{definition}

No additional uniformity hypothesis is needed. If \(P\) is b-elliptic,
then on the line \(\operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta\) the family
\(I(P,\lambda)\) is an elliptic suspended family, whose joint symbol in
\((\eta,\operatorname{Re}\lambda)\) is the restriction of
\({}^b\sigma_m(P)\) to the boundary. Hence it is invertible for
\(|\operatorname{Re}\lambda|\) large, and pointwise invertibility on the
whole line implies that \(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\) is a suspended family of
order \(-m\), with the corresponding uniform estimates.

For several boundary hypersurfaces, impose the corresponding condition at
each face with a multiweight. Below, \(X\) has one boundary hypersurface.

\section{Meromorphic inversion of the indicial family}

Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic, for instance a b-elliptic
b-differential operator. Its indicial family is an entire family of
elliptic operators of order \(m\) on the closed manifold \(\partial X\)
(polynomial in the differential case). On each strip
\(|\operatorname{Im}\lambda|\leq C\) it is an elliptic suspended family,
hence invertible for \(|\operatorname{Re}\lambda|\) large in the strip.
The Fredholm index of \(I(P,\lambda)\) is constant in \(\lambda\), so it is
zero. Hence the analytic Fredholm theorem below applies on each strip,
and the boundary spectrum meets each strip in a finite set.

\begin{theorem}[Analytic Fredholm theorem]
Let \(T(\lambda)\) be a holomorphic family of Fredholm operators of index
zero on a connected domain. If \(T(\lambda_0)\) is invertible at one point,
then \(T(\lambda)^{-1}\) is meromorphic. Every coefficient in the principal
part of a Laurent expansion at a pole has finite rank.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix \(\lambda_*\). Choose closed complements
\[
 X=\ker T(\lambda_*)\oplus X_1,
 \qquad
 Y=C\oplus\operatorname{ran}T(\lambda_*),
\]
where \(C\) is finite-dimensional. Since the index is zero,
\(\dim C=\dim\ker T(\lambda_*)\). Relative to these decompositions, write
\[
 T(\lambda)=
 \begin{pmatrix}a(\lambda)&b(\lambda)\\
                 c(\lambda)&d(\lambda)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The block \(d(\lambda_*):X_1\to\operatorname{ran}T(\lambda_*)\) is an
isomorphism, so \(d(\lambda)^{-1}\) is holomorphic near \(\lambda_*\). Block
Gaussian elimination reduces invertibility of \(T(\lambda)\) to that of
the finite-dimensional Schur complement
\[
 S(\lambda)=a(\lambda)-b(\lambda)d(\lambda)^{-1}c(\lambda):
 \ker T(\lambda_*)\longrightarrow C.
\]
Where \(S\) is invertible, the block inverse formula expresses
\(T(\lambda)^{-1}\) using \(d(\lambda)^{-1}\) and \(S(\lambda)^{-1}\).
Cramer's rule gives
\[
 S(\lambda)^{-1}
 =\frac{\operatorname{adj}S(\lambda)}{\det S(\lambda)},
\]
so this inverse is meromorphic. Its negative Laurent coefficients have
range contained in a finite-dimensional space obtained from \(C\) and
\(\ker T(\lambda_*)\).

Join \(\lambda_0\) to \(\lambda_*\) by a path and cover the path by finitely
many such block neighborhoods \(U_0,\ldots,U_N\), with connected nonempty
successive overlaps and \(\lambda_0\in U_0\).  On \(U_0\), the determinant
is not identically zero because \(T(\lambda_0)\) is invertible.  Inductively,
suppose a meromorphic inverse has been constructed on \(U_j\).  Its poles
are discrete, so \(U_j\cap U_{j+1}\) contains a point where \(T\) is
invertible.  The Schur determinant on \(U_{j+1}\) is therefore not
identically zero, and Cramer's rule constructs the next local meromorphic
inverse.  The two inverses agree on the nonempty open subset of the overlap
where \(T\) is invertible, hence on the whole overlap by the identity
theorem.  This continues the inverse to \(U_N\).  Continuations along two
paths agree by the same overlap argument (equivalently, by uniqueness in the
meromorphic sheaf), so the local inverses assemble to one meromorphic inverse
on the domain; see Figure~\ref{fig:continuation}.
\end{proof}

Applied to \(I(P,\lambda)\), this theorem yields a discrete set
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}_b(P)
 =
 \{\lambda\in\mathbb C:I(P,\lambda)\text{ is not invertible}\},
\]
called the boundary spectrum or set of indicial roots. The inverse
\(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\) is meromorphic with finite-rank residues.

A weight is admissible exactly when its Mellin line avoids
\(\operatorname{Spec}_b(P)\). Moving the line without crossing an indicial
root does not change the Fredholm index. When a root is crossed, the index
jump is determined by its algebraic multiplicity; the sign depends on the
direction in which the weight is moved.

\section{The full b-calculus and the Fredholm parametrix}

The symbolic parametrix constructed in the small calculus has smooth
remainders, but their normal operators need not vanish. Full ellipticity
allows one to remove this boundary error.

\begin{theorem}[Full b-parametrix; quoted]
Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic and fully elliptic at the
admissible weight \(\delta\). Then the full b-calculus contains a two-sided parametrix
\(Q\) such that
\[
 PQ=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L.
\]
The remainders are residual, have zero normal operators, gain arbitrarily
many b-derivatives, and have strictly positive side-face orders after
conjugation by \(x^\delta\). Their complete polyhomogeneous index families
are obtained from the poles of \(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\).
\end{theorem}

This is the full parametrix theorem of the b-calculus
\cite[Chapters~4--5]{Melrose}. Its proof uses the extended union and
composition of complete index families, rapid parameter estimates for
inverse Mellin transformation, and Borel summation at every boundary
face. The following calculation records its mechanism without replacing
those theorems.

Let \(Q_0\in\Psi_b^{-m}\) be a symbolic parametrix for \(P\):
\[
 PQ_0=I-R_0,\qquad Q_0P=I-R_0',
 \qquad R_0,R_0'\in\Psi_b^{-\infty}.
\]
On an admissible Mellin line, define the boundary correction by
\[
 I(Q_1,\lambda)
 =
 I(Q_0,\lambda)
 +I(P,\lambda)^{-1}I(R_0,\lambda).
\]
Then \(I(PQ_1,\lambda)=I\). The inverse Mellin transform produces a kernel
with polyhomogeneous expansions at the boundary faces. Its exponents are
governed by the poles of \(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\). This is the reason the
full b-calculus, rather than only the small calculus, is needed.

For the leading front-face correction, choose a horizontal line
\(\Gamma_\delta=\{\operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta\}\) avoiding the boundary
spectrum. With cutoffs \(\chi(x)\chi(x')\), the inverse Mellin formula
\[
 K_C(x,y;x',y')
 =
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_\delta}
 \left(\frac{x}{x'}\right)^{i\lambda}
 I(P,\lambda)^{-1}I(R_0,\lambda)(y,y')\,d\lambda
\]
defines a residual correction \(C\) near the front face. Parameter-elliptic
estimates make the integral rapidly convergent in
\(\operatorname{Re}\lambda\). Applying \(P\), using Mellin
multiplicativity, and restricting to the front face gives
\[
 N(P(Q_0+C)-I)=0.
\]
If the contour is shifted, the residue theorem adds terms
\[
 \left(\frac{x}{x'}\right)^{i\lambda_0}
 \left(\log\frac{x}{x'}\right)^j a_{\lambda_0,j}(y,y'),
 \qquad 0\leq j\leq k-1,
\]
where \(\lambda_0\) is an indicial root and \(k\) is the order of the
corresponding pole: the residue of \((x/x')^{i\lambda}F(\lambda)\) at a pole
of order \(k\) involves the first \(k-1\) derivatives of
\((x/x')^{i\lambda}\) in \(\lambda\). These are precisely the
polyhomogeneous terms allowed in the full calculus.

At the level of complete index families, the construction is performed on
both sides. Any mismatch between the two corrections is residual and has
zero normal operator. A Neumann iteration
in the residual ideal removes its successive boundary coefficients; Borel
summation removes all of them at once. An interior symbolic correction then
restores infinite-order vanishing at the lifted diagonal. The quoted
full-calculus theorem justifies these iterations and yields the two-sided
parametrix below.

Iterating at the interior diagonal and at the boundary faces gives
\(Q\in\Psi_b^{-m}\) in the full calculus with
\[
 PQ=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L.
\]
At an admissible weight, \(R_R\) and \(R_L\) are residual operators whose
normal operators vanish and whose side-face exponents have the decay
required by that weight.

\begin{lemma}
The remainders \(R_R\) and \(R_L\) act compactly on weighted b-Sobolev
spaces.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A residual kernel gains arbitrarily many b-derivatives. Vanishing of its
normal operator removes the translation-invariant limit on the cylindrical
end, while its positive side-face weights give uniform decay there. Cut
the cylinder at \(u=-T\). On the compact part, Rellich's lemma makes the
operator compact; on the tail, the operator norm tends to zero as
\(T\to\infty\). Hence the full operator is a norm limit of compact
operators.

More explicitly, let \(\chi_T\) equal one on \(\{u\geq-T\}\), which
contains the compact part, and vanish on \(\{u\leq-T-1\}\). The operator
\(\chi_TR\chi_T\) factors as
\[
 H_b^s\xrightarrow{\,\chi_T\,}H_b^s\xrightarrow{\,R\,}H_b^{s+N}
 \xrightarrow{\,\chi_T\,}H_b^s
\]
for any \(N>0\); the second arrow is compact by Rellich on the truncated
cylinder. The full-calculus residual mapping theorem, using both
\(N(R)=0\) and the strictly positive side-face orders stated above, gives
\[
 \|(1-\chi_T)R\|+\|R(1-\chi_T)\|\longrightarrow0.
\]
Therefore
\[
 R-\chi_TR\chi_T
 =(1-\chi_T)R+\chi_TR(1-\chi_T)
\]
tends to zero in operator norm. This proves compactness without assuming
that the noncompact \(b\)-manifold itself has a compact Sobolev embedding.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fredholm theorem for b-operators]
Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic and fully elliptic at weight
\(\delta\). Then, for every \(s\in\mathbb R\),
\[
 P:x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 \longrightarrow
 x^\delta H_b^{s-m}(X;F)
\]
is Fredholm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The full b-parametrix \(Q\) is bounded between the reverse weighted Sobolev
spaces, and
\[
 PQ=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L
\]
with compact remainders. Atkinson's theorem therefore implies that \(P\)
is Fredholm.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Elliptic and polyhomogeneous regularity]
If \(Pv=f\), where \(v\in x^\delta H_b^{s_0}\) and
\(f\in x^\delta H_b^{s-m}\), then \(v\in x^\delta H_b^s\). If, in
addition, the coefficients of \(P\) and \(f\) are polyhomogeneous with
compatible index families, then \(v\) is polyhomogeneous. Its exponents
are generated by those index families and the indicial roots; higher-order
poles produce logarithms. In particular, a null-solution of a
polyhomogeneous fully elliptic operator has such an expansion.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply the parametrix:
\[
 v=Qf+R_Lv.
\]
The first term gains \(m\) derivatives, and the residual term gains
arbitrarily many. Iteration gives the Sobolev regularity. The additional
polyhomogeneous statement is the full-calculus regularity theorem: Mellin
inversion and contour shifting apply because the coefficients,
right-hand side, and parametrix have compatible complete index families.
Residues at indicial poles then produce powers of \(x\) and, at
higher-order poles, logarithmic terms. Sobolev iteration alone would not
imply this expansion.
\end{proof}

\section{The Dirac case}

For the chiral operator
\[
 P=D^+=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+A^+),
\]
the b-principal symbol is Clifford multiplication and is invertible away
from the zero section. Thus \(P\) is b-elliptic. Its indicial family is
invertible on the unweighted line if and only if
\(A^+\) is invertible.

Since \(I(P,\lambda)=c(dx/x)(i\lambda+A^+)\), the boundary spectrum is
\(\operatorname{Spec}_b(P)=\{\lambda=ia:a\in\operatorname{spec}(A^+)\}\),
and the root \(\lambda=0\) has multiplicity \(h=\dim\ker A^+\) (a simple
pole of \(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\) with residue of rank \(h\)). If \(A^+\) has
kernel, choose a weight \(\pm\epsilon\) with
\(0<\epsilon<\min\{|a|:0\neq a\in\operatorname{spec}(A^+)\}\); both Mellin
lines \(\operatorname{Im}\lambda=\mp\epsilon\) avoid the boundary spectrum
and the realizations
\[
 P_{\pm\epsilon}=D^+:x^{\pm\epsilon}H_b^1(X;E^+)\longrightarrow
 x^{\pm\epsilon}L_b^2(X;E^-)
\]
are Fredholm, on the manifold \(X_\infty\) compactified at
\(x=0\).

\begin{proposition}[APS index as a weighted index]\label{prop:weighted-APS}
For \(\epsilon\) as above,
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}=\operatorname{ind}P_{+\epsilon},
 \qquad
 \operatorname{ind}P_{-\epsilon}-\operatorname{ind}P_{+\epsilon}=h .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the cylinder a null solution is a sum of modes
\(e^{-\lambda u}\phi_\lambda\), \(\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A^+)\).
Such a mode lies in \(x^{\epsilon}L_b^2=e^{\epsilon u}L^2(du\,dy)\) iff
\(\lambda<-\epsilon\), i.e.\ iff \(\lambda<0\); so restriction to \(X\) and
extension identify \(\ker P_{+\epsilon}\) with the kernel of the APS
realization of Section~\ref{sec:APS-realization}, whose boundary data are
exactly the negative modes. The cokernel of \(P_{+\epsilon}\) is the kernel
of \(D^-\) on the dual space \(x^{-\epsilon}L_b^2\); a mode of \(A^-\) with
eigenvalue \(\mu\) lies there iff \(\mu<\epsilon\), i.e.\ \(\mu\leq0\), which
is the adjoint APS condition \(\Pi_{>0}(A^-)\gamma\psi=0\) of
Section~\ref{sec:APS-realization}. The same argument for \(-\epsilon\)
identifies \(P_{-\epsilon}\) with the realization defined by the condition
\(\Pi_{>0}(A^+)\gamma\phi=0\), whose kernel may also contain zero modes and
whose adjoint condition excludes them. The APS domain is a closed
subspace of codimension \(h\) in this larger domain (the boundary values
in \(\ker A^+\) are removed), and restricting a Fredholm operator to a
closed subspace of codimension \(h\) lowers its index by \(h\).
\end{proof}

Consequently
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}
 =\frac{\operatorname{ind}P_{+\epsilon}+\operatorname{ind}P_{-\epsilon}}2
 -\frac h2 .
\]
The term \(-h/2\) in the APS formula is exactly half the weight jump across
the zero indicial root; by the APS theorem proved below, the symmetric
average of the two weighted indices is \(\int_X[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)]_{[n]}-\eta(A^+)/2\).
Combining the two corrections gives the reduced eta invariant
\[
 \bar\eta(A^+)
 =\frac{\eta(A^+)+\dim\ker A^+}2 ,
\]
in which the strict/non-strict choice at the zero eigenspace appears as
the choice of the weight \(+\epsilon\).

\section{The APS boundary realization}
\label{sec:APS-realization}

The preceding b-Fredholm theorem explains the cylindrical realization.
We now return to the compact manifold with boundary and prove that its
spectral boundary condition selects exactly the same modes.  Placing this
section here avoids using the indicial family before it has been defined.

Let
\[
 \gamma:H^1(X;E^\pm)\longrightarrow H^{1/2}(Y;E^\pm|_Y)
\]
be the trace maps. Since \(A^+\) is self-adjoint and elliptic on the closed
manifold \(Y\), it has discrete real spectrum, and its spectral projections
are order-zero pseudodifferential operators. Define
\[
 \Pi_{\geq0}(A^+)=\sum_{\lambda\geq0}\Pi_\lambda
\]
and
\[
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)
 =\left\{\phi\in H^1(X;E^+):
          \Pi_{\geq0}(A^+)\gamma\phi=0\right\}.
\]
Thus the boundary value is allowed to lie only in the negative spectral
subspace of \(A^+\). Since
\(\Pi_{\geq0}(A^+):H^{1/2}(Y)\to H^{1/2}(Y)\) and the trace map are bounded,
this domain is the kernel of a bounded map from \(H^1(X;E^+)\) to
\(H^{1/2}(Y;E^+|_Y)\). It is therefore closed in \(H^1\).
We write \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) for \(D^+\) with this domain, viewed as an
unbounded operator \(L^2(X;E^+)\to L^2(X;E^-)\). Throughout this section
\(u\in[0,1)\) is the inward collar coordinate of
Section~\ref{subsec:conventions}, sections of \(E^+\) are denoted
\(\phi\), and sections of \(E^-\) are denoted \(\psi\).

Green's formula determines the adjoint domain.  On the collar,
\(D^-=c(du)(\partial_u+A^-)\), and
\[
 \langle D^+\phi,\psi\rangle_X-\langle \phi,D^-\psi\rangle_X
 =-\langle c(du)\gamma\phi,\gamma\psi\rangle_Y,
 \qquad \phi\in H^1(X;E^+),\ \psi\in H^1(X;E^-).
\]
Recall that \(A^-=-c(du)A^+c(du)^{-1}\) is the tangential operator on
\(E^-|_Y\), so Clifford multiplication sends the \(\lambda\)-space
of \(A^+\) to the \(-\lambda\)-space of \(A^-\).

\begin{lemma}[Adjoint domain]\label{lem:APS-adjoint}
The Hilbert-space adjoint of \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) is \(D^-\) with domain
\[
 \mathcal D((D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})^*)
 =\{\psi\in H^1(X;E^-):\Pi_{>0}(A^-)\gamma\psi=0\}.
\]
In particular every element of the adjoint domain lies in \(H^1\).
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If \(\psi\in H^1\) satisfies the displayed condition, then \(\gamma\psi\)
lies in the nonpositive spectral subspace of \(A^-\), which is orthogonal
to \(c(du)\gamma\phi\) for every allowed \(\phi\), since \(c(du)\gamma\phi\)
lies in the positive subspace. Green's formula shows that \(\psi\) is in
the adjoint domain.

Conversely, let \(\psi\in L^2\) be in the adjoint domain, so
\(\langle D^+\phi,\psi\rangle=\langle\phi,f\rangle\) for all
\(\phi\in\mathcal D_{\mathrm{APS}}\), with \(f\in L^2\). Testing with
\(\phi\in C_c^\infty(X^\circ)\) gives \(D^-\psi=f\) weakly, hence
\(\psi\in H^1_{\mathrm{loc}}(X^\circ)\) by interior elliptic regularity.
Fix \(\chi\in C_c^\infty([0,\varepsilon))\) equal to one near \(u=0\). Then
\(\chi\psi\) is still in the adjoint domain, with \(f\) replaced by
\(\chi f+c(du)\chi'\psi\in L^2\), so it suffices to treat \(\chi\psi\). Let
\(\{e_\mu\}\) be an orthonormal eigenbasis of \(A^-\) and write
\(\chi\psi=\sum_\mu b_\mu(u)e_\mu\), \(c(du)^{-1}(\chi f+c(du)\chi'\psi)
=\sum_\mu g_\mu(u)e_\mu\). Testing with \(\phi=\theta(u)c(du)^{-1}e_\mu\),
\(\theta\in C_c^\infty((0,\varepsilon))\), gives
\(b_\mu'+\mu b_\mu=g_\mu\) in \(\mathcal D'((0,\varepsilon))\), so
\(b_\mu\in H^1((0,\varepsilon))\), \(b_\mu(\varepsilon)=0\), and
\(\sum_\mu\lVert g_\mu\rVert^2<\infty\). For \(\mu>0\), the section
\(c(du)^{-1}e_\mu\) lies in the \((-\mu)\)-eigenspace of \(A^+\), so
\(\phi=\theta(u)c(du)^{-1}e_\mu\) with \(\theta(0)=1\) is an allowed test
section; one-dimensional integration by parts in the adjoint identity
leaves only the boundary term, which is a nonzero multiple of
\(\overline{b_\mu(0)}\). Hence \(b_\mu(0)=0\) for \(\mu>0\). Now
\[
 \int_0^\varepsilon|b_\mu'+\mu b_\mu|^2du
 =\int_0^\varepsilon(|b_\mu'|^2+\mu^2|b_\mu|^2)du-\mu|b_\mu(0)|^2,
\]
and the last term is \(\geq0\) for every \(\mu\): it vanishes for \(\mu>0\)
and has the favorable sign for \(\mu\leq0\). Summing over \(\mu\) and using
the elliptic graph-norm equivalence for \(A^-\) on \(Y\) gives
\(\chi\psi\in H^1\), with
\(\lVert\chi\psi\rVert_{H^1}\leq C\bigl(\sum_\mu\lVert g_\mu\rVert^2\bigr)^{1/2}
+C\lVert\psi\rVert_{L^2}\). Thus \(\psi\in H^1\), and its boundary values
are \(\gamma\psi=\sum_\mu b_\mu(0)e_\mu\), which satisfy
\(\Pi_{>0}(A^-)\gamma\psi=0\).
\end{proof}

The strict projection at zero in the adjoint condition is the source of
the kernel-dimension correction.

\begin{theorem}[Fredholmness of the APS realization]
The operator
\[
 D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}:
 \mathcal D_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)
 \longrightarrow L^2(X;E^-)
\]
is Fredholm, and its cokernel is the kernel of \(D^-\) on the domain of
Lemma~\ref{lem:APS-adjoint}. If \(A^+\) is invertible, restriction to \(X\)
and extension along the cylinder identify its kernel and cokernel with
those of the \(L^2\) realization on \(X_\infty\). Consequently
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}
 =\operatorname{ind}_{L^2}D^+_{X_\infty}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Choose an orthonormal eigenbasis \(A^+e_\lambda=\lambda e_\lambda\)
and expand on the collar,
\[
 \phi(u,y)=\sum_\lambda a_\lambda(u)e_\lambda(y).
\]
The product operator is the direct sum of the one-dimensional operators
\(\partial_u+\lambda\).  For a scalar function \(f\), integration by
parts gives
\[
 \int_0^\varepsilon|f'+\lambda f|^2du
 =\int_0^\varepsilon(|f'|^2+\lambda^2|f|^2)du
   +\lambda|f(\varepsilon)|^2-\lambda|f(0)|^2.
\]
For \(\lambda\geq0\), the APS condition sets \(f(0)=0\).  For
\(\lambda<0\), the last term has the favorable sign.  A cutoff at
\(u=\varepsilon\), followed by summation over \(\lambda\), yields the
collar estimate below.  Here one uses
\[
 \lVert g\rVert_{H^1(Y)}^2
 \asymp \lVert A^+g\rVert_{L^2(Y)}^2+\lVert g\rVert_{L^2(Y)}^2,
\]
the elliptic graph-norm equivalence for \(A^+\); the finitely many
eigenvalues in a fixed bounded interval are absorbed by the \(L^2\)-term.
Thus
\[
 \lVert \phi\rVert_{H^1([0,\varepsilon)\times Y)}
 \leq C\left(
 \lVert D^+\phi\rVert_{L^2}
 +\lVert \phi\rVert_{L^2}
 +\lVert \phi\rVert_{H^1(\{u>\varepsilon/2\})}
 \right).
\]
Choose a partition of unity with one function supported in the collar and
one in the interior. The commutators with these cutoffs are order zero.
The ordinary interior elliptic estimate controls the interior function,
and its compact lower-order remainder is absorbed into the \(L^2\)-term.
This gives
\[
 \lVert \phi\rVert_{H^1(X)}
 \leq C\bigl(\lVert D^+\phi\rVert_{L^2(X)}
              +\lVert \phi\rVert_{L^2(X)}\bigr).
\]
By Lemma~\ref{lem:APS-adjoint} and the same computation, the analogous
estimate holds for \(D^-\) on the adjoint domain. Rellich
compactness first makes each kernel finite-dimensional: the unit ball of a
kernel is precompact in \(L^2\), and the estimate upgrades \(L^2\)
convergence to \(H^1\) convergence.

For closed range, the estimate implies the sharper bound
\[
 \lVert \phi\rVert_{H^1}\leq C\lVert D^+\phi\rVert_{L^2},
 \qquad \phi\perp\ker D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}.
\]
Otherwise there would be \(\phi_j\perp\ker D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) with
\(\lVert \phi_j\rVert_{H^1}=1\) and \(D^+\phi_j\to0\). Rellich gives an
\(L^2\)-convergent subsequence; applying the original estimate to
differences makes it \(H^1\)-convergent to a unit vector in the kernel,
contradicting orthogonality. The sharper bound makes the range closed.
Since the range is closed, the cokernel is the kernel of the adjoint,
which by Lemma~\ref{lem:APS-adjoint} is the kernel of \(D^-\) on an
\(H^1\) domain satisfying the same estimate; it is therefore
finite-dimensional. Thus the APS realization is Fredholm.

Assume now that \(A^+\) is invertible and continue \(u\) to \(u\leq0\) on
the attached cylinder. A solution in the \(\lambda\)-eigenspace is
\[
 a_\lambda(u)=e^{-\lambda u}a_\lambda(0).
\]
It is square integrable on \(u\leq0\) exactly for \(\lambda<0\), precisely
the boundary data allowed by the APS projection.  Restriction and extension
identify the kernels.  The adjoint condition of
Lemma~\ref{lem:APS-adjoint} gives the same identification for the
adjoints, hence for the cokernels.
\end{proof}

\begin{question}
Why is an elliptic differential operator on a manifold with boundary not
automatically Fredholm?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The interior principal symbol controls high-frequency behavior away from
the boundary but does not select boundary data.  For example,
\(\bar\partial\) on a disk has the infinite-dimensional space of
holomorphic functions in its kernel if no boundary condition is imposed.
An elliptic boundary condition removes half of the Cauchy data.  The APS
condition makes that choice spectrally; the b-calculus encodes the same
choice as decay on the attached cylinder.
\end{answer}

\section{The K-theoretic proof architecture}
\label{sec:K-theory}

The closed index theorem has two layers: an elliptic symbol defines a
\(K\)-class, and the analytic index is the unique push-forward of that
class normalized by the Bott operator. On a manifold with boundary, the
interior symbol alone is insufficient. One must also record an invertible
model at the cylindrical end. The b-calculus packages exactly these two
pieces \cite{Melrose,MelrosePiazza}.

Fix the full order-zero b-calculus on \(L_b^2(X)\), stabilize by compact
matrices, and let \(\mathcal A_b\) be its operator-norm closure. Let
\(\Sigma_b\) be the norm closure of compatible pairs in the product of
the continuous b-cosphere symbols and the order-zero suspended normal
algebra.
An element of \(\Sigma_b\) is a compatible pair
\[
 (a,q)=
 \bigl({}^b\sigma_0(P),I(P,\lambda)\bigr),
\]
where \(a\) is the b-principal symbol and \(q\) is the suspended indicial
family. Compatibility means that the principal symbol of \(q\) is the
restriction of \(a\) to the boundary.

\begin{theorem}[Joint symbol extension; quoted]
With these closures, the joint symbol map is surjective and the sequence
\[
 0\longrightarrow\mathcal K
 \longrightarrow\mathcal A_b
 \xrightarrow{\,j\,}\Sigma_b
 \longrightarrow0
\]
is exact.
\end{theorem}

This is a C*-closure theorem, including surjectivity of the compatibility
map and identification of its kernel; it does not follow from the formal
parametrix alone. We use the standard result of Melrose and
Melrose--Nistor \cite{Melrose,MNCstar}. A b-operator with vanishing principal
and normal symbols lies in the compact kernel, while the theorem supplies
the converse and the closure assertions.

If \(P\) is fully elliptic, \(j(P)\) is invertible. After stabilization it
defines a class \([j(P)]\in K_1(\Sigma_b)\). The six-term exact sequence
has a connecting map
\[
 \partial:K_1(\Sigma_b)\longrightarrow
 K_0(\mathcal K)\cong\mathbb Z.
\]

\begin{lemma}[The connecting map is the Fredholm index]
With the usual sign convention for \(\partial\),
\[
 \partial[j(P)]=\operatorname{ind}P.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Lift \(j(P)^{-1}\) to a b-parametrix \(Q\). Then
\[
 I-QP\in\mathcal K,\qquad I-PQ\in\mathcal K.
\]
After a compact perturbation, the polar part \(V\) of \(P\) is a partial
isometry. The definition of the connecting map gives
\[
 \partial[j(P)]
 =[I-V^*V]-[I-VV^*].
\]
The first defect projection is the orthogonal projection onto
\(\ker P\), and the second is the projection onto \(\ker P^*\). Under
\(K_0(\mathcal K)\cong\mathbb Z\), their difference is
\(\dim\ker P-\dim\ker P^*=\operatorname{ind}P\).
\end{proof}

For the chiral product operator \(P=D^+\), the unbounded joint elliptic
data are
\[
 \left({}^b\sigma_1(P),\,
 I(P,\lambda)=
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A^+)\right).
\]
Composing on the right with the inverse of a fixed positive elliptic
family of order one normalizes this to an order-zero element of
\(\Sigma_b\) without changing invertibility or the index class.
Equivalently, one may use the bounded transform of \(P\), whose indicial
family includes the corresponding factor of order minus one.
If \(A^+\) is invertible, this pair is fully elliptic. If \(A^+\) has kernel,
changing only the displayed boundary family does not yet define an
operator on the cylinder. Put
\[
 V=\ker A^+,
 \qquad T=-I_V,
 \qquad \Pi_0^+=\Pi_V.
\]
The involution \(T=-I_V\) is Loya's ideal boundary condition corresponding
to the APS convention \(\Pi_{\geq0}(A^+)\gamma\phi=0\): it moves every zero
mode to the positive side. Put
\[
 \rho(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda^2}.
\]
It is positive and even on \(\mathbb R\) with \(\rho(0)=1\), and, unlike
a general Schwartz function, it is entire and Schwartz on every horizontal
line; its inverse Fourier transform is a Gaussian in \(z=\log(x/x')\), which
decays faster than every exponential. This is needed because the
indicial family of a b-smoothing operator in the small calculus is entire
(Section~\ref{sec:normal-op}). Surjectivity of the residual normal map
supplies global lifts of the suspended family below; choose the
Loya--Melrose--Piazza lift that realizes the ideal boundary condition.
Thus there is a global chiral b-smoothing operator
\(T_b^+:E^+\to E^-\)
with
\[
 I(T_b^+,\lambda)
 =c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)
   \varepsilon\rho(\lambda)\Pi_0^+.
\]
For sufficiently small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\(i\lambda+A^++\varepsilon\rho(\lambda)\Pi_0^+\) is invertible for every
real \(\lambda\): on \(V\) it is the scalar
\(i\lambda+\varepsilon\rho(\lambda)\), which cannot vanish, and on
\(V^\perp\) the nonzero spectrum retains its gap. Hence
\[
 P_T=P+T_b^+
\]
is fully elliptic. The associated total self-adjoint operator used in the
heat proof is
\[
 \mathcal D_T=
 \begin{pmatrix}0&P_T^*\\P_T&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The perturbation is smoothing in the b-calculus, but it is not a
finite-rank or compact operator on the infinite cylinder.

For this chosen lift, the ideal-boundary comparison theorem identifies the
perturbed \(L^2\) realization with the APS realization determined by
\(T=-I_V\): their kernels, adjoint kernels, and indices agree
\cite{Loya,MelrosePiazza}. A different lift with the same normal family
differs from this one by a b-smoothing operator with vanishing normal
operator, which is compact on \(L_b^2\); it therefore has the same index,
but need not have canonically identical kernel spaces.
Thus the precise stable joint-symbol class is
\[
 [j(P_T)]\in K_1(\Sigma_b).
\]

For the geometric push-forward it is safer to retain the relative symbol
group rather than claim a Thom map on every element of \(K_1(\Sigma_b)\).
Let \(\mathfrak S_b\) be the stabilized continuous b-cosphere-symbol
algebra, let \(\mathfrak S_\partial\) be its boundary restriction, and let
\(r:\mathfrak S_b\to\mathfrak S_\partial\) be restriction. Its mapping
cone is
\[
 C_r=\{(a,f)\in\mathfrak S_b\oplus
 C_0([0,1),\mathfrak S_\partial): f(0)=r(a)\}.
\]
Write
\[
 K^0_{\mathrm{APS}}({}^bT^*X):=K_1(C_r).
\]
The degree is one: an invertible symbol \(a\in\mathfrak S_b\) together with
a path \(f\) of invertible elements of \(\mathfrak S_\partial\) from
\(r(a)\) to the identity is an invertible element of the unitization of
\(C_r\). As in the closed case, where an invertible symbol defines a class
in \(K_1(C(S^*M))\) and the boundary map of the pair \((B^*M,S^*M)\) carries
it to \(K^0_c(T^*M)\), the superscript \(0\) refers to the compactly
supported topological group that this class represents. The invertible
indicial family of \(P_T\) is not itself such a path: it is a family of
operators on \(\partial X\) whose principal symbol, as a suspended family,
is \(r(a)\). The quoted comparison theorem converts it into a trivializing
path of \(r(a)\), up to homotopy, and hence into a class
\(\alpha_{\mathrm{APS}}(P_T)\in K^0_{\mathrm{APS}}({}^bT^*X)\).
The same comparison theorem for the joint-symbol pullback algebra supplies
a map
\[
 \kappa:K^0_{\mathrm{APS}}({}^bT^*X)\longrightarrow K_1(\Sigma_b),
 \qquad
 \kappa(\alpha_{\mathrm{APS}}(P_T))=[j(P_T)].
\]
This mapping-cone/joint-symbol comparison is a quoted part of the relative
index theorem \cite{MelrosePiazza}; it is not inferred from the formal
parametrix.

A neat embedding
\[
 i:(X,\partial X)\hookrightarrow
 (\mathbb R_+^N,\mathbb R^{N-1})
\]
defines a Thom push-forward of these relative symbol classes. Normal deformation
and collar doubling identify the target with the Bott group
\[
 K_c^0(\mathbb R^{2N})\cong\mathbb Z.
\]
Call the resulting integer
\(\operatorname{t\text{-}ind}_{\mathrm{APS}}\).

\begin{theorem}[K-theoretic APS index theorem; quoted]
For a product-type graded Dirac operator,
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \operatorname{t\text{-}ind}_{\mathrm{APS}}
 \bigl(\alpha_{\mathrm{APS}}(P_T)\bigr).
\]
\end{theorem}

The theorem asserts that the following diagram commutes; the left vertical
map is the connecting-map index after comparison with the joint symbol.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge,row sep=large]
 K^0_{\mathrm{APS}}({}^bT^*X)
 \arrow[r,"i_!"]
 \arrow[d,"{\partial\circ\kappa}"']
 &K_c^0(\mathbb R^{2N})
 \arrow[d,"\mathrm{Bott}"]\\
 \mathbb Z\arrow[r,equal]&\mathbb Z
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\begin{proof}[Proof architecture]
The proof follows the closed push-forward argument. In a tubular
neighborhood, the Thom class is represented analytically by the
fibrewise Bott operator
\[
 B_\nu=\sum_j c(e_j)\partial_{v_j}+\widehat c(v),
\]
on \(\Lambda^\bullet\nu_\mathbb C^*\), where
\(c(v)=\varepsilon(v^\flat)-\iota_v\) and
\(\widehat c(v)=\varepsilon(v^\flat)+\iota_v\).
Its even kernel is the Gaussian line and its odd kernel is zero.
Taking the graded product with \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) therefore preserves
the index. On the boundary collar the normal Bott factor is product type,
so it carries the chosen indicial trivialization to the pushed-forward
operator without changing which zero modes are selected. Excision removes
the complement of the tubular neighborhood.

The existence and homotopy invariance of this relative Thom map, its
compatibility with the joint-symbol extension, and the collar-doubling
excision step are the K-theoretic APS push-forward theorem of Melrose and
Melrose--Piazza \cite{Melrose,MelrosePiazza}. They are substantial inputs,
not consequences of the displayed Bott formula alone.

After the neat embedding and collar doubling, the class is an integer
multiple of the Bott generator. The Bott operator has index one, so both
the analytic index, computed by the connecting map above, and the
topological push-forward equal that integer. This proves the formula.
\end{proof}

This theorem proves the equality of two \emph{integer} indices. Ordinary
\(K\)-theory does not by itself determine the real number
\[
 \bar\eta(A^+)=\frac{\eta(A^+)+\dim\ker A^+}{2}.
\]
That number is a secondary, differential refinement of the boundary
\(K\)-class: it can vary continuously with the metric and connection even
when the underlying \(K\)-class is fixed. The heat-kernel and b-trace
argument below supplies its canonical real lift and proves the numerical
formula
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
 -\bar\eta(A^+).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 \bar\eta(A^+)\equiv
 \int_X
 \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
 \pmod{\mathbb Z}.
\]
Thus the exponentiated eta invariant is the boundary transgression of the
local index form. The \(K\)-theoretic
proof explains integrality and homotopy invariance, while the heat proof
identifies the precise spectral correction.

\begin{question}
How can \(\bar\eta(A^+)\) vary continuously while the APS index, an
integer, can change only by jumps?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
As the geometric data vary without a zero eigenvalue crossing, the local
integral and \(\bar\eta(A^+)\) vary by the same amount, so their difference
remains the fixed integer index. At a crossing, the spectral projection
changes by a finite-dimensional space; spectral flow changes the
\(K\)-theoretic boundary trivialization and accounts for the integer jump.
Ordinary \(K\)-theory records that jump, whereas the eta invariant records
the continuous real-valued refinement between jumps.
\end{answer}

\section{The b-trace defect}

We postpone regularization until this point because the b-density,
front-face restriction, and indicial family are now available.  As in
Section~\ref{subsec:conventions}, \(x=e^{u}\) on the cylindrical end.  If
\[
 f(x,y)=f_0(y)+O(x^\varepsilon)
\]
with the same estimate after b-differentiation, then for
\(\operatorname{Re}z>0\)
\[
 \int_Xx^zf\,d\mu_b
 =\frac1z\int_{\partial X}f_0\,d\mu_{\partial X}+G_f(z),
\]
where \(G_f\) is holomorphic for \(\operatorname{Re}z>-\varepsilon\).
Indeed, the leading term contributes
\(\int_0^1x^{z-1}dx=z^{-1}\), and the remainder is integrable in the
larger half-plane.

\begin{definition}
The b-integral is the finite part
\[
 {}^b\!\int_Xf\,d\mu_b
 :=\operatorname*{FP}_{z=0}\int_Xx^zf\,d\mu_b=G_f(0).
\]
Equivalently, if the cylinder is truncated at \(u=-T\), then
\[
 \int_{\{u\geq -T\}}f\,d\mu_b=cT+C+o(1),
 \qquad
 {}^b\!\int_Xf\,d\mu_b=C.
\]
\end{definition}

More generally, if the diagonal is a polyhomogeneous b-density
\[
 \omega\sim
 \sum_{(z,k)\in\mathcal E}x^z(\log x)^k\omega_{z,k}(y)\frac{dx}{x},
\]
with a discrete index set \(\mathcal E\) satisfying the usual finiteness
conditions, then \(\int x^w\omega\) is meromorphic in \(w\). The
b-integral is its finite part at \(w=0\). The one-term definition above is
the special case needed for the trace-defect calculation; the density and
the full diagonal index family must be specified in the general case.

The constant \(c\) is determined by the translation-invariant normal
operator.  Indeed, the leading term gives \(c=\int_{\partial X}f_0\,
d\mu_{\partial X}\); when \(f=\operatorname{tr}K_A(p,p)\) is the diagonal
value of a kernel, \(f_0(y)\) is the value of
\(\operatorname{tr}K_A\) at the point of \(\Delta_b\cap\mathrm{ff}\) over
\(y\), namely \(\operatorname{tr}k_A(0;y,y)\), the convolution kernel of
\(N(A)\) at \(z=0\); see Figure~\ref{fig:btrace}.  Thus the same boundary model that obstructs trace class also
determines the regularization.  The finite part depends on the defining
function, but the change is compensated by the trace-defect term below.

Let \(A\) be a residual b-operator whose lifted kernel has a full expansion
at the front face and sufficient decay at the side faces. Its b-trace is the
finite part of the diagonal integral,
\[
 {}^b\Tr(A)
 =
 \operatorname*{FP}_{z=0}
 \int_X x^z\operatorname{tr}_E K_A(p,p)\,d\mu_b(p).
\]
The ordinary trace property fails because the finite part retains a
boundary contribution.

Use the Fourier convention fixed for the indicial family in
Section~\ref{sec:normal-op}:
\[
 \widehat f(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-iz\lambda}f(z)\,dz,
 \qquad
 f(z)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{iz\lambda}
 \widehat f(\lambda)\,d\lambda.
\]
Only the inversion constant \(1/2\pi\) enters the proof below: the
diagonal value of a convolution kernel is
\(k(0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\widehat k(\lambda)\,d\lambda\) for either sign.

\begin{theorem}[b-trace defect formula]
Let \(A,B\) lie in the full b-calculus so that \(AB\) and \(BA\) are
residual with the side-face decay required for the b-trace, and suppose
\(\partial_\lambda I(A,\lambda)I(B,\lambda)\) is trace class on
\(\partial X\) and integrable in \(\lambda\). Then
\[
 {}^b\Tr([A,B])
 =
 \frac{i}{2\pi}
 \int_{\mathbb R}
 \Tr_{\partial X}
 \left(
 \partial_\lambda I(A,\lambda)\,I(B,\lambda)
 \right)\,d\lambda.
\]
For graded bundles, the same formula holds with the supercommutator and
supertrace.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(\operatorname{Re}z>0\), the stated side-face orders make
\(x^zAB\) and \(x^zBA\) trace class, so cyclicity of the ordinary trace is
legitimate and gives
\[
 \Tr(x^z[A,B])=\Tr([x^z,A]B).
\]
Set
\[
 C_z=z^{-1}x^{-z}[x^z,A].
\]
The full b-commutator theorem says that \(C_z\) extends holomorphically to
\(z=0\) in the full calculus, and its indicial family there is
\[
 I(C_0,\lambda)=i\,\partial_\lambda I(A,\lambda).
\]
The diagonal finite-part theorem says that the trace of \(x^zC_zB\) has a
simple pole at zero and identifies its residue with the Fourier integral of
\(I(C_0,\lambda)I(B,\lambda)\), divided by \(2\pi\).
Since \([x^z,A]B=z x^zC_zB\), that residue is the constant term of the
original trace. Finally \(i/(2\pi)=-1/(2\pi i)\).

Holomorphic extension of \(C_z\) and the diagonal residue identification
are the two analytic inputs in Melrose's b-trace theorem
\cite[Chapter~5]{Melrose}; neither follows from the displayed algebraic
commutator identity alone.
\end{proof}

This identity is independent of the defining function: changing \(x\)
changes both the finite part and the indicial family by compensating terms.

\section{Dirac operators of product type}

We use the product-type setup of Section~\ref{sec:closed-to-APS}:
\(X\) is even-dimensional, \(E=E^+\oplus E^-\) is a graded Hermitian
Clifford module with compatible connection, \(\mathcal D\) is the odd
total Dirac operator with chiral part \(P=D^+\), and near \(\partial X\)
\[
 \mathcal D=c(du)(\partial_u+\mathcal A),
 \qquad
 P=c(du)(\partial_u+A^+),
 \qquad
 \mathcal A=\operatorname{diag}(A^+,A^-).
\]
With the cylinder convention \(x=e^u\) of
Section~\ref{subsec:conventions}, \(du=dx/x\) and
\(\partial_u=x\partial_x\), so the product formula becomes
\[
 \mathcal D=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+\mathcal A),
 \qquad
 P=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+A^+),
\]
with the same tangential operators as above. This convention fixes the sign
of the APS correction to be
\(-(\eta_{A^+}(0)+\dim\ker A^+)/2\).

The Weitzenböck formula is
\[
 \mathcal D^2=\nabla^*\nabla+\mathcal R,
\]
where \(\mathcal R\) is the curvature endomorphism. For the spin Dirac
operator twisted by \(W\),
\[
 \mathcal R=\frac14\operatorname{scal}
 +\frac12\sum_{j,k}c(e_j)c(e_k)F^W(e_j,e_k).
\]

The heat kernel is constructed on the parabolic b-heat space

 \right]_{\mathrm{par}},
\]
where the final blow-up assigns weight one to spatial variables and weight
two to \(t\). The new temporal front face resolves the singularity of the
kernel as \(t\downarrow0\).

At the temporal front face, the model equation is the Euclidean heat
equation on each b-tangent fibre. At the boundary front face, the model is
the heat equation for the normal operator on the cylinder. This local heat
construction does not require full ellipticity. Full ellipticity and the
boundary spectral gap enter later, when the large-time limit is identified
with a Fredholm index.

\begin{proposition}[Compatibility of the heat models]
The Euclidean model at the temporal front face and the cylindrical normal
model at the boundary front face have the same restriction to their
intersection. They therefore determine a single leading kernel on
\(X_{b,h}^2\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Near a boundary point use the cylindrical coordinates
\[
 (u,y)=(\log x,y)
\]
of Section~\ref{subsec:conventions}.
After parabolic rescaling about the diagonal, the coefficients of
\(\mathcal D^2\)
freeze at the base point. The temporal model is therefore the Gaussian
heat kernel of the constant-coefficient operator on
\({}^bT_pX\). If the base point tends to the boundary, the same frozen
operator splits into the normal line in \(u\) and the tangential operator
on \(T_yY\). This is exactly the small-time restriction of the
translation-invariant normal heat kernel. Thus both models restrict to
\[
 (4\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|V|^2/4}
\]
times parallel transport at the corner of the two faces.
\end{proof}

\subsection{The semiclassical construction in square-root time}

Put
\[
 s=\sqrt t.
\]
The parabolic blow-up in the variable \(t\) is the ordinary radial blow-up
in the square-root-time smooth structure:
\[
 \widetilde X_{b,h}^2
 =
 \left[
 X_b^2\times[0,\infty)_s;
 \Delta_b\times\{0\}
 \right].
\]
Thus the semiclassical parameter \(s\downarrow0\) and the heat-space
description are two coordinate versions of the same construction.  This
observation is useful because it turns the heat equation into a pair of
explicit model equations.

Recall \(n=\dim X\).  Near the temporal front face, use the rescaled
difference variable
\[
 V=\frac{z-z'}s\in{}^bT_zX.
\]
After the density factor is removed, a heat kernel has a formal expansion
of the form
\[
 K(s;z,z')
 \sim
 s^{-n}\sum_{j\geq0}s^j k_j(z,V).
\]
Its leading temporal symbol is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semiclassical-temporal-symbol}
 k_0(z,V)
 =
 (4\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|V|_z^2/4}\operatorname{Id}_{E_z}.
\end{equation}
Indeed, if \(\Delta_V=-\sum_i\partial_{V_i}^2\) is the positive Euclidean
Laplacian, then the scalar factor \(G(V)\) in
\eqref{eq:semiclassical-temporal-symbol} is the uniquely normalized
Schwartz solution of
\[
 \left[
 \Delta_V-\frac12\bigl(V\mathbin{\cdot}\partial_V+n\bigr)
 \right]G=0,
 \qquad
 \int_{{}^bT_zX}G(V)\,dV=1.
\]
For the coefficient \(s^{-n+j}k_j\), the corresponding normal operator is
\[
 T_j
 =
 \Delta_V-\frac12\bigl(V\mathbin{\cdot}\partial_V+n-j\bigr).
\]
The equations \(T_jk_j=r_j\) are the usual heat transport equations in
semiclassical notation.

The second model is obtained by restricting to the boundary face and
Fourier transforming in the translation variable of the cylinder.  For
the product operator
\[
 \mathcal D
 =c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+\mathcal A),
\]
the indicial families satisfy
\begin{equation}\label{eq:semiclassical-boundary-model}
 I(\mathcal D,\lambda)
 =c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+\mathcal A),
 \qquad
 I(\mathcal D^2,\lambda)=\lambda^2+\mathcal A^2.
\end{equation}
Consequently the boundary heat model is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:boundary-heat-family}
 I(e^{-t\mathcal D^2},\lambda)
 =e^{-t(\lambda^2+\mathcal A^2)}
 =e^{-t\lambda^2}e^{-t\mathcal A^2}.
\end{equation}
After inverse Fourier transform, this is the one-dimensional Gaussian in
the cylindrical variable multiplied by the tangential heat kernel.  A
different Mellin convention replaces \(i\lambda\) by its signed variant;
the invariant statement is
\(I(\mathcal D^2,\lambda)=\lambda^2+\mathcal A^2\) on the real Fourier
line.

\begin{proposition}[Formal semiclassical heat-parametrix recursion]
Let \(\mathcal D^2\) be a product-type generalized b-Laplacian, and assume
the heat-space restriction and extension theorem for polyhomogeneous
kernels.  Starting with the compatible leading models above, for every
\(N\) one can choose a heat parametrix whose error vanishes to order \(N\)
at both model faces.  The compatible formal expansions can be
Borel-summed to a parametrix \(H_0\) whose error vanishes to infinite order
at every temporal face.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Assume that the lower-order jets have already been removed, and denote the
first remaining temporal and boundary error coefficients by
\(R_{\mathrm{tf}}\) and \(R_{\mathrm{bf}}(\lambda)\).  At the temporal
model, the correction with zero initial value is given by the Euclidean
Duhamel operator
\[
 Q_{\mathrm{tf}}(t)
 =-
 \int_0^t
 e^{-(t-r)\Delta_{{}^bT_zX}}R_{\mathrm{tf}}(r)\,dr.
\]
Thus
\[
 (\partial_t+\Delta_{{}^bT_zX})Q_{\mathrm{tf}}
 =-R_{\mathrm{tf}}.
\]
Expanding this identity in powers of \(s\) gives precisely the transport
equations \(T_jk_j=r_j\) displayed above.

At the boundary model, put
\(B(\lambda)=\lambda^2+\mathcal A^2\).  The corresponding correction is
\[
 Q_{\mathrm{bf}}(\lambda,t)
 =-
 \int_0^t
 e^{-(t-r)B(\lambda)}R_{\mathrm{bf}}(\lambda,r)\,dr,
 \qquad
 (\partial_t+B(\lambda))Q_{\mathrm{bf}}
 =-R_{\mathrm{bf}}.
\]
The two Duhamel constructions commute with restriction to the common
corner because the model operators agree there.  Hence the two new jets
can be chosen compatibly.  Alternating the corrections raises the order of
vanishing at each face without changing the jets already fixed at the
other face: the extension theorem realizes a new face correction with
zero lower-order jets at the other face.

Induction gives compatible jets to arbitrary order.  The Borel extension
lemma on manifolds with corners realizes those jets by a single kernel.
At this point the error is residual and vanishes to infinite order at the
temporal faces.  This last passage uses the heat-calculus extension,
composition, and push-forward results; it is the nonformal geometric
input in the recursion.
\end{proof}

\begin{question}
Does the semiclassical construction by itself prove the APS index formula?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
No.  It constructs the heat kernel and controls its asymptotic structure.
The index formula still requires the local Clifford-supertrace
calculation, the b-trace defect formula, regularity of the eta function at
the origin, and the large-time identification with the Fredholm index.
Those inputs are stated separately below.
\end{answer}

Matching the leading Gaussian is necessary but not sufficient: every
coefficient in the two polyhomogeneous index families must agree at the
corner.

\begin{theorem}[b-heat kernel theorem; quoted]
For a product-type generalized b-Laplacian, the temporal and boundary
model problems have compatible complete index families on
\(X_{b,h}^2\). There is a heat parametrix with the prescribed normal
operators whose error is residual and vanishes to infinite order at all
temporal faces. Its Volterra correction is the heat kernel and remains in
the b-heat calculus.
\end{theorem}

This is the b-heat theorem of \cite[Chapters~6--7]{Melrose}. It is
stated for product-type generalized b-Laplacians only; the perturbed
operator \(\mathcal D_T^2\) used in the heat proof is handled separately
in Section~\ref{sec:heat-proof}. Its proof
uses the heat-space composition and push-forward theorems at every face.
The factorial Volterra estimate below completes the final residual
correction; it does not construct the compatible index families by itself.

The preceding semiclassical recursion describes the formal part of this
theorem.  The quoted heat-space results justify the global extension and
the closure of the polyhomogeneous class under all the compositions used
in that recursion.

Starting with the Gaussian model, solve the transport equations
successively along the temporal front face. Borel summation gives a
parametrix \(H_0(t)\) satisfying the distributional initial condition
\(H_0(0)=I\) and
\[
 (\partial_t+\mathcal D^2)H_0(t)=R(t),
\]
where \(R\) vanishes to infinite order at \(t=0\). The Volterra series
\[
 H
 =
 H_0-H_0*R+H_0*R*R-\cdots
\]
converges for small \(t\), and the semigroup property extends it to all
positive times. Here
\[
 (K_1*K_2)(t)=\int_0^tK_1(t-\sigma)K_2(\sigma)\,d\sigma.
\]
The decisive boundary term in differentiating a Volterra convolution is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Volterra-boundary}
 (\partial_t+\mathcal D^2)(H_0*S)
 =S+R*S.
\end{equation}
Indeed, differentiating the upper integration limit contributes
\(H_0(0)S(t)=S(t)\); the remaining terms give \(R*S\).  Applying
\eqref{eq:Volterra-boundary} term by term makes the geometric cancellation
in the displayed series explicit. Thus \(H(t)=e^{-t\mathcal D^2}\).

Convergence comes from the volume of the ordered time simplex. On each
residual heat-calculus seminorm, \(H_0\) and \(R\) are uniformly bounded
for \(0<t\leq t_0\). If \(\|R(t)\|\leq C\), then
\[
 R^{*k}(t)
 =
 \int_{\substack{\sigma_j\geq0\\\sigma_1+\cdots+\sigma_k=t}}
 R(\sigma_1)\cdots R(\sigma_k)\,d\sigma_1\cdots d\sigma_{k-1}
\]
and the simplex has volume \(t^{k-1}/(k-1)!\). Hence
\[
 \|R^{*k}(t)\|
 \leq \frac{C^k t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}.
\]
The factorial proves absolute convergence; multiplication by the fixed
bounded left factor \(H_0\) gives the same conclusion for every term of the
heat series. The estimate after applying \(b\)-derivatives proves
convergence in the residual heat calculus. Since \(R\) vanishes to infinite
order at \(t=0\), so does every correction term, and the initial value
remains the identity.

The construction also proves Duhamel's formula. If \(L_r\) is a smooth
family of nonnegative self-adjoint elliptic operators with common domain
and is differentiable in the graph topology, then
\[
 \frac{d}{dr}e^{-tL_r}
 =
 -\int_0^t
 e^{-(t-\sigma)L_r}\dot L_r e^{-\sigma L_r}\,d\sigma.
\]
Both sides solve the same inhomogeneous heat equation with zero initial
value, which proves the identity by uniqueness.

\section{Small-time supertrace}

For an ordinary Laplace-type operator, the diagonal heat kernel has an
expansion
\[
 K(t;p,p)
 \sim
 (4\pi t)^{-n/2}\sum_{j=0}^\infty t^j a_j(p).
\]
For a Dirac operator, Clifford supertrace cancels all terms below total
degree \(n\). Getzler rescaling identifies the surviving term.

\begin{theorem}[Local index theorem]
For a graded Dirac operator, with the normalizations of
Section~\ref{subsec:conventions},
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}
 \operatorname{Str}K(t;p,p)\,d\operatorname{vol}(p)
 =
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX,\nabla^{TX})
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S,\nabla^{E/S})
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
For the spin Dirac operator twisted by \(W\), the second factor is
\(\operatorname{ch}(W,\nabla^W)\).
\end{theorem}

The rescaled heat-kernel convergence and Clifford-supertrace
normalization in this theorem are the standard local index theorem, used
here as an analytic input (see \cite{Melrose} and the closed-manifold
references cited there). The heat-space construction above supplies the
boundary-compatible kernel but does not, by itself, prove the Getzler
model calculation.

On a manifold with cylindrical end, diagonal integration is interpreted as
a b-integral. The heat-space expansion shows
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}{}^b\Str(e^{-t\mathcal D^2})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
Because the data are of product type, no additional local boundary density
appears: on the product cylinder the limiting integrand is pulled back
from \(Y\) and has degree \(n>\dim Y\), so it vanishes there, and the
b-integral reduces to the ordinary integral over \(X\).  This assertion is a corollary of the quoted b-heat/Getzler
theorem: the temporal-front-face supertrace is the interior Getzler model,
while the boundary-front-face model has zero independent constant term.
It does not follow from matching only the leading Gaussian.

\section{The eta invariant}\label{sec:eta}

The eta function \(\eta_{A^+}(s)\) and its Mellin representation were
defined in \eqref{eq:eta-def} and \eqref{eq:eta-mellin}. In
\eqref{eq:eta-mellin} the large-time integral is entire after omission of the zero eigenspace.
The heat expansion gives a meromorphic continuation of the small-time
integral.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:eta-regular}
The eta function of a self-adjoint elliptic differential operator \(B\) of
positive order on a closed manifold is regular at \(s=0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof using the residue theorem for projections]
Split the Mellin integral at \(t=1\). On the orthogonal complement of the
kernel, the large-time integrand is exponentially decreasing, so that
part is entire. For an operator of order \(r\) on a \(d\)-manifold, the
pseudodifferential heat calculus gives a polyhomogeneous expansion of
\(\Tr(Be^{-tB^2})\) as \(t\downarrow0\). Termwise Mellin integration gives
the meromorphic continuation.

The standard Wodzicki--Guillemin residue theorem identifies the possible residue at zero with a constant multiple of the
noncommutative residue of the order-zero operator
\[
 \operatorname{sgn}(B)=B|B|^{-1}
\]
on \(\ker B^\perp\). Modulo a finite-rank operator,
\(\operatorname{sgn}(B)=2\Pi_+-I\), where \(\Pi_+\) is the positive spectral
projection. The same theorem says that the noncommutative residue vanishes on the identity, on
finite-rank operators, and on pseudodifferential projections. The possible
residue is therefore zero. For a Dirac-type operator the same cancellation
is visible directly in the parity of its local Clifford heat coefficients;
for the boundary operator \(A^+\) it also follows from the heat proof below,
since \(F(t)\) has a finite limit as \(t\downarrow0\).
These residue facts are quoted analytic inputs, not consequences of the
preceding Mellin manipulation; see \cite{Melrose} for their use in the APS
setting.
\end{proof}

Write
\[
 \eta(A^+)=\eta_{A^+}(0),\qquad h(A^+)=\dim\ker A^+.
\]

\section{Heat proof of the APS index theorem}
\label{sec:heat-proof}

Assume first that \(A^+\) is invertible. Set
\[
 F(t)={}^b\Str(e^{-t\mathcal D^2})
 ={}^b\Tr(e^{-tP^*P})-{}^b\Tr(e^{-tPP^*}).
\]
Since \(\mathcal D\) commutes with \(e^{-t\mathcal D^2}\),
\[
 F'(t)
 =
 -\frac12\,{}^b\Str
 \left([\mathcal D,\mathcal D e^{-t\mathcal D^2}]_{\mathrm s}\right).
\]
Apply the graded b-trace defect formula and use
\[
 I(\mathcal D,\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+\mathcal A).
\]
Moreover,
\[
 I(\mathcal D e^{-t\mathcal D^2},\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+\mathcal A)
 e^{-t(\lambda^2+\mathcal A^2)},
 \qquad
 \partial_\lambda I(\mathcal D,\lambda)
 =i\,c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right).
\]
The Clifford relation \(c(dx/x)^2=-I\), together with the graded matrix
trace, transports the \(E^-\)-block through \(c(dx/x)\) and combines it
with the \(E^+\)-block. Writing \(c=c(dx/x)\), the relation
\(cA^+=-A^-c\) shows that the surviving contribution is the ordinary trace of
\(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2}\) on \(E^+|_Y\); the term odd in \(\lambda\) integrates
to zero. Finally,
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t\lambda^2}\,d\lambda
 =\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}.
\]
Substitution into the defect formula, including the factor \(1/2\) above,
gives
\[
 F'(t)
 =
 -\frac1{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\Tr(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2}).
\]
This is the only place where the b-supertrace varies with \(t\).

The large-time limit needs a separate input, because \({}^b\Tr\) is not
continuous in trace norm: strong or spectral convergence of
\(e^{-t\mathcal D^2}\) to the kernel projection does not by itself control
the b-trace.

\begin{proposition}[Large-time limit; quoted]\label{prop:large-time}
Let \(\mathcal D\) be fully elliptic at weight zero, e.g.\ \(A^+\)
invertible, or \(\mathcal D_T\) below. Then \(\ker_{L^2}\mathcal D\) is
finite-dimensional, its orthogonal projection \(\Pi_{\ker}\) is a
residual operator whose kernel decays exponentially along the
cylindrical end, and \(e^{-t\mathcal D^2}-\Pi_{\ker}\to0\) as \(t\to\infty\)
in the topology of the b-heat calculus, in which \({}^b\Tr\) is continuous.
Consequently
\[
 \lim_{t\to\infty}{}^b\Str(e^{-t\mathcal D^2})
 =\Str(\Pi_{\ker})
 =\dim\ker_{L^2}D^+-\dim\ker_{L^2}D^-
 =\operatorname{ind}_{L^2}D^+ .
\]
\end{proposition}

This is the large-time part of the b-heat calculus
\cite[Chapter~7]{Melrose}; see also \cite{MelrosePiazza} for operators
perturbed by b-smoothing terms such as \(\mathcal D_T\). Full ellipticity
gives a spectral gap \((0,c)\) for \(\mathcal D^2\) on \(L^2\), and the
convergence is \(O(e^{-ct})\). Since \(\Pi_{\ker}\) is trace class, its
b-trace is its ordinary trace. Combined with the Fredholm theorem of
Section~\ref{sec:APS-realization}, it gives
\[
 \lim_{t\to\infty}F(t)=\operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}).
\]
The local index theorem gives
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}F(t)
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
Integrating the trace-defect identity and using the Mellin formula at
\(s=0\) yields
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}
 -\frac12\eta(A^+).
\]

Indeed,
\[
 F(\infty)-F(0)
 =
 -\frac1{2\sqrt\pi}
 \int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\Tr(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2})\,dt
 =-\frac12\eta_{A^+}(0).
\]
At the upper endpoint the integrand decays exponentially. At the lower
endpoint the integral converges as an improper integral, because \(F\) has
a finite limit at \(t=0\); since \(\Tr(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2})\) has an
asymptotic expansion in powers of \(t^{1/2}\) without logarithms, the
convergent value agrees with the continuation of the Mellin integral
\eqref{eq:eta-mellin} to \(s=0\).

If \(A^+\) has kernel, a constant shift of its zero modes along the entire
cylinder is not a finite-rank perturbation of the cylindrical operator.
Use instead the global chiral b-smoothing perturbation \(T_b^+\) constructed
in Section~\ref{sec:K-theory}, with
\(\mathcal T=\begin{pmatrix}0&(T_b^+)^*\\T_b^+&0\end{pmatrix}\) and
\(\mathcal D_T=\mathcal D+\mathcal T\). Then
\[
 \mathcal D_T^2=\mathcal D^2+S_T,\qquad
 S_T=\mathcal D\mathcal T+\mathcal T\mathcal D+\mathcal T^2
 \in\Psi_b^{-\infty}(X;E),
\]
and \(N(S_T)\neq0\), so \(\mathcal D_T^2\) is \emph{not} a product-type
generalized b-Laplacian and the b-heat theorem above does not apply
directly. Its heat kernel is obtained by the same Volterra argument with
\(R\) replaced by \(S_T\):
\[
 e^{-t\mathcal D_T^2}
 =\sum_{k\geq0}(-1)^k\,e^{-t\mathcal D^2}*
   \bigl(S_Te^{-t\mathcal D^2}\bigr)^{*k}.
\]
Since \(S_T\) is bounded, the simplex estimate gives convergence with
bound \(C^kt^k/k!\); that each term lies in the b-heat calculus, with
the expected normal operators at the boundary front face, is the
composition theorem for the b-heat calculus with \(\Psi_b^{-\infty}\),
which we quote from Melrose--Piazza \cite{MelrosePiazza}. Every
correction term is \(O(t)\) at the temporal front face, so the
perturbation does not change the interior local index density.

\begin{theorem}[Ideal-boundary perturbation comparison; quoted]
Let \(V=\ker A^+\), choose the involution \(T=-I_V\), and let \(T_b^+\)
be the Loya--Melrose--Piazza lift chosen in Section~\ref{sec:K-theory},
a global b-smoothing realization of the ideal boundary condition with
suspended normal family
\[
 I(T_b^+,\lambda)
 =c(dx/x)\varepsilon\rho(\lambda)\Pi_V,
 \qquad \rho(\lambda)=e^{-\lambda^2}.
\]
Then \(P_T=P+T_b^+\) and \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) have naturally identified
kernels and adjoint kernels. Moreover the generalized boundary correction
in the b-trace-defect formula for \(\mathcal D_T\) is
\[
 \eta(A^+)-\operatorname{sign}(T)
 =\eta(A^+)+\dim V.
\]
\end{theorem}

This is Loya's ideal-boundary comparison theorem, equivalently the
Melrose--Piazza spectral-section comparison \cite{Loya,MelrosePiazza}.
The sign is now explicit:
\(\operatorname{sign}(-I_V)=-\dim V\).  Analytically, the suspended family
is homotoped through invertibles to the direct sum of the nonzero family
\(i\lambda+A^+\) and the finite-dimensional positive zero-mode model
\(i\lambda+\varepsilon\); the first contributes \(\eta(A^+)\) and the
second contributes \(\dim V\).  Applying the preceding trace-defect
calculation to \(\mathcal D_T\) therefore gives
\(-\bigl(\eta(A^+)+\dim V\bigr)/2\). This comparison step cannot be
replaced by perturbing the boundary family without constructing a global
b-operator.

\begin{theorem}[Atiyah--Patodi--Singer \cite{APS}]
Let \(D^+\) be a product-type graded Dirac operator on an
even-dimensional compact manifold with boundary. With APS boundary
condition
\[
 \Pi_{\geq0}(A^+)(\phi|_{\partial X})=0,
 \qquad \phi\in H^1(X;E^+),
\]
one has
\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}
 -
 \frac{\eta_{A^+}(0)+\dim\ker A^+}{2}.
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

The interior term is local. The eta invariant and the kernel dimension are
the global spectral correction forced by the cylindrical end.

\begin{question}
Why can the boundary correction not be another local curvature integral?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The derivative \(F'(t)\) is the full spectral trace
\(\Tr(A^+e^{-t(A^+)^2})\), not the integral of one fixed heat coefficient.
Integrating it from zero to infinity uses every eigenvalue of \(A^+\).
Small-time asymptotics control the meromorphic continuation, but the value
\(\eta_{A^+}(0)\) also contains large-time information and therefore depends on
the global spectral asymmetry. This is exactly the information lost when a
local index density is integrated on a manifold with boundary.
\end{answer}
