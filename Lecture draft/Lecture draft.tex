\section{Elliptic Fredholm theory on closed manifolds}

The b-calculus is a boundary refinement of ordinary elliptic theory, so we
first record the closed-manifold argument that will later fail at the
cylindrical end. Let \(M\) be closed and let
\[
 P:C^\infty(M;E)\longrightarrow C^\infty(M;F)
\]
be a differential or classical pseudodifferential operator of order \(m\).
Its principal symbol
\[
 \sigma_m(P)(x,\xi):E_x\longrightarrow F_x
\]
is obtained by retaining the terms homogeneous of degree \(m\) in
\(\xi\). The operator is elliptic when this map is invertible for every
\(\xi\ne0\).

For every \(s\in\mathbb R\), an operator of order \(m\) extends boundedly
as
\[
 P:H^s(M;E)\longrightarrow H^{s-m}(M;F).
\]
Ellipticity permits the inverse symbol to be quantized and corrected
recursively.

\begin{theorem}[Elliptic parametrix]
If \(P\) is elliptic, there is \(Q\in\Psi^{-m}(M;F,E)\) such that
\[
 QP=I-R_L,\qquad PQ=I-R_R,
 \qquad R_L,R_R\in\Psi^{-\infty}(M).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let \(p\sim p_m+p_{m-1}+\cdots\) be the complete symbol of \(P\).
Choose \(q_{-m}=p_m^{-1}\) away from the zero section. If
\(q_{-m},\ldots,q_{-m-j+1}\) are known, the degree \(-j\) part of
\(q\# p-I\) is affine in the unknown \(q_{-m-j}\), with coefficient
\(p_m\). Multiplication by \(p_m^{-1}\) therefore determines
\(q_{-m-j}\) and removes that term. Borel summation produces a classical
symbol \(q\) for which \(q\# p-I\) has order \(-\infty\). Repeating on
the other side and applying a smoothing correction gives one \(Q\) with
both displayed identities.
\end{proof}

The logic of the construction is:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 P
 \arrow[r,"\sigma_m"]
 &p_m
 \arrow[r,"\text{invert}"]
 &p_m^{-1}
 \arrow[d,"\text{symbol recursion}"]\\
 \text{Fredholm}
 &I-\text{compact}\arrow[l,"\text{Atkinson}"']
 &Q\arrow[l,"\text{compose}"']
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\begin{theorem}[Closed elliptic Fredholm theorem]
For every \(s\), the map
\[
 P:H^s(M;E)\longrightarrow H^{s-m}(M;F)
\]
is Fredholm. Its kernel is smooth, its range is closed, and
\[
 \operatorname{coker}P\cong\ker P^*.
\]
If \(Pu\in H^{r-m}\), then \(u\in H^r\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The parametrix gives
\[
 u=QPu+R_Lu.
\]
This proves elliptic regularity because \(Q\) gains \(m\) derivatives and
\(R_L\) is smoothing. On a closed manifold, a smoothing operator factors
through \(H^{s+N}\hookrightarrow H^s\), which is compact by Rellich's
lemma. Thus \(P\) is invertible modulo compact operators and is Fredholm
by Atkinson's theorem. The orthogonal complement of its closed range is
\(\ker P^*\), giving the cokernel statement.
\end{proof}

The analytic index
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P=\dim\ker P-\dim\ker P^*
\]
is independent of \(s\) and constant in continuous families of elliptic
operators. A lower-order perturbation preserves the principal symbol and
hence the index.

\begin{lemma}[Fedosov trace formula]
Suppose \(Q\) is a parametrix for the Fredholm operator \(P\) and
\[
 S_L=I-QP,\qquad S_R=I-PQ
\]
are trace class. Then
\[
 \operatorname{ind}P=\Tr(S_L)-\Tr(S_R).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let \(G\) be the generalized inverse of \(P\), equal to the inverse from
\((\ker P)^\perp\) onto \(\operatorname{ran}P\) and zero on
\(\ker P^*\). If \(\Pi_0,\Pi_1\) are the orthogonal projections onto
\(\ker P,\ker P^*\), then
\[
 GP=I-\Pi_0,\qquad PG=I-\Pi_1.
\]
Hence
\(\operatorname{ind}P=\Tr(\Pi_0)-\Tr(\Pi_1)\).
Subtracting these identities from the identities for \(Q\) shows that
\((Q-G)P\) and \(P(Q-G)\) are trace class. Cyclicity gives them equal
traces, so
\[
 \Tr(S_L)-\Tr(S_R)
 =\Tr(\Pi_0)-\Tr(\Pi_1).
\]
\end{proof}

\begin{discussion}
Both hypotheses in the closed theorem matter. Ellipticity produces
smoothing remainders; compactness of the base turns the Sobolev gain into
a compact map. On a cylinder, translations of a fixed bump remain bounded
but have no convergent subsequence. The b-calculus must therefore control
not only high frequency, through the principal symbol, but also escape to
infinity, through the normal operator.
\end{discussion}

\section{From the closed index theorem to the APS formula}

Let $X$ be a compact even-dimensional Riemannian manifold with boundary
$Y$. Assume that the metric and all bundle data are of product type on a
collar $[0,1)_u\times Y$. Let $E=E^+\oplus E^-$ be a graded Hermitian
Clifford module and let
\[
 D=\begin{pmatrix}0&D^-\\D^+&0\end{pmatrix}
\]
be its Dirac operator. On the collar we use the convention
\[
 D=c(du)(\partial_u+A),
\]
where $A$ is the self-adjoint tangential Dirac operator and
$c(du)A=-Ac(du)$.

\subsection{Closed manifolds}

When $\partial X=\varnothing$, the heat operators are smoothing and
trace class. Define
\[
 Q_t=D^-\int_0^t e^{-sD^+D^-}\,ds.
\]
Functional calculus and the intertwining relation
$D^-e^{-sD^+D^-}=e^{-sD^-D^+}D^-$ give
\[
 D^+Q_t=I-e^{-tD^+D^-},
 \qquad
 Q_tD^+=I-e^{-tD^-D^+}.
\]
Fedosov's trace formula therefore yields the McKean--Singer identity
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+
 =\Tr(e^{-tD^-D^+})-\Tr(e^{-tD^+D^-})
 =\operatorname{Str}(e^{-tD^2})
\]
for every $t>0$. The local heat expansion then gives
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+
 =\int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX,\nabla^{TX})
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S,\nabla^{E/S})
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]

\subsection{The cylindrical end}

Attach $(-\infty,0]\times Y$ to $X$ and extend $D$ by its product
formula. The resulting complete manifold is denoted $X_\infty$. The
operator
\[
 D^+:
 H^1(X_\infty,E^+)
 \longrightarrow
 L^2(X_\infty,E^-)
\]
is Fredholm precisely when $A$ is invertible. Expand a section in an
eigenbasis of \(A\). On the mode with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), the operator
on the end reduces to \(\partial_x+\lambda\). A spectral gap gives uniform
exponential estimates.
If \(0\in\operatorname{spec}(A)\), translates of a fixed cutoff in a zero
mode form a Weyl sequence at zero.

Although $D^+$ is Fredholm when $A$ is invertible, the two heat
operators are not separately trace class: their kernels are
translation invariant to leading order on the infinite cylinder. Their
graded difference is handled by the $b$-trace introduced below.

For $\Re s$ large, define the eta function
\[
 \eta_A(s)=\sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{spec}(A)\setminus\{0\}}
   \operatorname{sgn}(\lambda)|\lambda|^{-s}.
\]
Its Mellin representation is
\[
 \eta_A(s)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\!\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)}
 \int_0^\infty t^{(s-1)/2}\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2})\,dt.
\]
The right side extends meromorphically and is regular at $s=0$. With
$h=\dim\ker A$, the Atiyah--Patodi--Singer formula is
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D_{\mathrm{APS}}^+
 =\int_X
  \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
  -\frac{\eta_A(0)+h}{2}.
\]
When $A$ is invertible, $h=0$, and this equals the $L^2$ index on
$X_\infty$.

\begin{question}
Why does attaching a cylinder help if the resulting manifold is
noncompact?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The cylinder does not remove the analytic difficulty; it puts that
difficulty into a translation-invariant form. On the compact manifold
with boundary, the APS condition is the nonlocal projection determined by
the spectrum of \(A\). On the cylinder, the same condition is encoded by
square integrability. Indeed, on an \(A\)-eigenvector with eigenvalue
\(\lambda\), the equation \((\partial_x+A)u=0\) becomes an elementary ODE,
and exactly one spectral half gives decaying solutions on the outward end.
Thus the APS projection and the \(L^2\) condition select the same boundary
data. The remaining translation-invariant part is what the normal operator
and the \(b\)-trace measure.
\end{answer}

\section{The APS boundary problem}

Let
\[
 \gamma:H^1(X;E^+)\longrightarrow H^{1/2}(Y;E^+|_Y)
\]
be the trace map. Since \(A\) is self-adjoint and elliptic on the closed
manifold \(Y\), its spectral projections are order-zero
pseudodifferential operators. Define
\[
 \Pi_{\geq0}(A)
 =
 \sum_{\lambda\geq0}\Pi_\lambda
\]
and the APS domain
\[
 \mathcal D_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)
 =
 \left\{
 u\in H^1(X;E^+):
 \Pi_{\geq0}(A)\gamma u=0
 \right\}.
\]
Thus the boundary value is allowed to lie only in the negative spectral
subspace of \(A\).

Green's formula fixes the complementary adjoint condition. With the
collar convention above,
\[
 \langle D^+u,v\rangle_X-\langle u,D^-v\rangle_X
 =
 -\langle c(du)\gamma u,\gamma v\rangle_Y.
\]
Since \(c(du)\) interchanges the positive and negative spectral subspaces,
the adjoint of \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) is \(D^-\) with the complementary
strict projection. This distinction at zero is responsible for the
\(\dim\ker A\) term in the index formula.

\begin{theorem}[Fredholmness of the APS realization]
The operator
\[
 D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}:
 \mathcal D_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)
 \longrightarrow L^2(X;E^-)
\]
is Fredholm. If \(A\) is invertible, its kernel and cokernel agree with
those of the \(L^2\) realization on \(X_\infty\); in particular,
\[
 \operatorname{ind}D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}
 =\operatorname{ind}_{L^2}D^+_{X_\infty}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
On the collar, expand
\[
 u(u,y)=\sum_\lambda u_\lambda(u)\phi_\lambda(y),
 \qquad A\phi_\lambda=\lambda\phi_\lambda.
\]
The principal part of \(D^+\) is the direct sum of the one-dimensional
operators \(\partial_u+\lambda\). The APS condition sets
\(u_\lambda(0)=0\) for \(\lambda\geq0\). Integration by parts in each
mode gives the uniform collar estimate
\[
 \|u\|_{H^1([0,\varepsilon)\times Y)}
 \leq C\left(
 \|D^+u\|_{L^2}
 +\|u\|_{L^2}
 +\|u\|_{H^1(\{u>\varepsilon/2\})}
 \right).
\]
An interior elliptic estimate absorbs the last term and yields
\[
 \|u\|_{H^1(X)}
 \leq C\bigl(\|D^+u\|_{L^2(X)}+\|u\|_{L^2(X)}\bigr)
\]
on the APS domain. The same estimate holds for the adjoint boundary
problem. Since \(H^1(X)\hookrightarrow L^2(X)\) is compact, the standard
compactness argument gives finite-dimensional kernels and closed range;
the adjoint estimate gives finite-dimensional cokernel. Hence the
realization is Fredholm.

Assume now that \(A\) is invertible and write the outward cylinder
coordinate as \(t=-u\geq0\). A boundary eigenmode extends as a solution of
the product equation by
\[
 u_\lambda(t)=e^{\lambda t}u_\lambda(0).
\]
This extension is square integrable exactly when \(\lambda<0\), precisely
the modes allowed by the APS condition. Restriction and extension
therefore identify the kernels. Applying the same argument to the adjoint
identifies the cokernels, proving equality of indices.
\end{proof}

\begin{question}
Why is an elliptic differential operator on a manifold with boundary not
automatically Fredholm?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The interior principal symbol controls high-frequency behavior away from
the boundary but does not select boundary data. For example, the
\(\bar\partial\)-operator on a disk has the infinite-dimensional space of
holomorphic functions in its kernel if no boundary condition is imposed.
An elliptic boundary condition removes half of the Cauchy data. The APS
condition makes that choice spectrally; the b-calculus makes the same
choice through decay on the attached cylinder.
\end{answer}

\section{Regularized b-integrals and b-traces}

Put $\rho=e^u$ on the cylindrical end \(u\in(-\infty,0]\). Then \(\rho\)
is a boundary
defining function for the compactification and
$dx=d\rho/\rho$. Suppose that a smooth function has an expansion
\[
 f(\rho,y)=f_0(y)+O(\rho^\epsilon),\qquad \epsilon>0,
\]
with the same estimate after applying any number of $b$-derivatives.
For $\Re z>0$, set
\[
 F_f(z)=\int_X \rho^z f\,d\mu_b.
\]
The contribution of $f_0$ is
\[
 \int_0^1\rho^{z-1}\,d\rho\int_Y f_0\,d\mu_Y
 =\frac1z\int_Yf_0\,d\mu_Y.
\]
The remainder is holomorphic for $\Re z>-\epsilon$. Hence
\[
 F_f(z)=\frac{1}{z}\int_Yf_0\,d\mu_Y+G_f(z),
\]
where $G_f$ is holomorphic near zero.

\begin{definition}
The $b$-integral of $f$ is the finite part
\[
 {}^b\!\int_X f\,d\mu_b:=\operatorname*{FP}_{z=0}F_f(z)=G_f(0).
\]
\end{definition}

The definition depends on the chosen boundary defining function, but
the trace-defect formula below changes compatibly, and the final index
is invariant.

Let $K$ be a smoothing $b$-operator whose diagonal has an expansion of
the preceding form.

\begin{definition}
The $b$-trace is
\[
 {}^b\Tr(K):={}^b\!\int_X\operatorname{tr}_E K(p,p)\,d\mu_b(p).
\]
For a graded bundle, the analogous definition uses the fibrewise
supertrace.
\end{definition}

Unlike the ordinary trace, the $b$-trace need not vanish on commutators.
Its defect is a boundary term determined by the indicial family. The
precise formula and its application to the heat kernel appear below.

\begin{discussion}
This finite part is not an arbitrary subtraction. If the cylinder is cut
at \(u=-T\), then the diagonal integral has the form \(cT+C+o(1)\). The
\(b\)-integral is the constant term \(C\). The coefficient \(c\) depends
only on the translation-invariant kernel at infinity, so the same boundary
model that obstructs trace class also controls the trace defect.
\end{discussion}
\section{Real blow-up and the b-double space}

A smooth $n$-manifold with corners is locally modelled on
\[
 \mathbb R^{n,k}=[0,\infty)^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k},
 \qquad 0\leq k\leq n,
\]
with smooth transition maps in the usual extension sense. A boundary
hypersurface is a connected codimension-one boundary face. At a point
$p$, the codimension is the number of independent boundary defining
functions that vanish at $p$.

Let $S\subset X$ be an embedded boundary face. Its real blow-up
$[X;S]$ replaces $S$ by the inward-pointing spherical normal bundle
$S^+NS$. The blow-down map
\[
 \beta:[X;S]\longrightarrow X
\]
is a diffeomorphism away from the new boundary hypersurface and
collapses that hypersurface onto $S$.

For a compact manifold $X$ with boundary, the $b$-double space is
\[
 X_b^2=[X^2;\partial X\times\partial X].
\]
Its boundary hypersurfaces are the front face $\mathrm{ff}$ and the
lifts $\mathrm{lb}$ and $\mathrm{rb}$ of
$\partial X\times X$ and $X\times\partial X$. The lifted diagonal
$\Delta_b$ is the closure of the interior diagonal.

The geometry is summarized by the blow-down square
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 \Delta_b \arrow[r,hook] \arrow[d,"\beta|_{\Delta_b}"']
   & X_b^2 \arrow[d,"\beta"] \\
 \Delta \arrow[r,hook]
   & X^2 .
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad
\beta(\mathrm{ff})=\partial X\times\partial X.
\]
The blow-down is an isomorphism away from
\(\partial X\times\partial X\). Over that corner, the front face records
the ratio of the two normal variables and
therefore remembers how the corner is approached.

Near $\partial X$, choose product coordinates $(x,y)$ and $(x',y')$ on
the two factors. Two useful projective coordinate systems are
\[
 (x,s=x'/x,y,y')\quad\text{and}\quad
 (s'=x/x',x',y,y').
\]
In the first chart, $\mathrm{ff}=\{x=0\}$ and
$\mathrm{rb}=\{s=0\}$; in the second,
$\mathrm{ff}=\{x'=0\}$ and $\mathrm{lb}=\{s'=0\}$.
Near the interior of the front face one may instead use
\[
 r=x,\qquad z=\log(x/x'),\qquad y,\qquad Y=y-y',
\]
so that $\Delta_b=\{z=0,\;Y=0\}$.

\begin{question}
Why not work directly on \(X^2\)?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The ratio \(x/x'\) has no limit at \((x,x')=(0,0)\) on \(X^2\). Kernels of
translation-invariant operators on the cylinder depend on
\(\log(x/x')\), so they cannot be smooth functions of \(x,x'\) at that
corner. Blow-up replaces the corner by the set of limiting ratios. In the
projective chart \(x'=sx\), the same kernel is a function of
\(-\log s\), which is smooth in the interior of the front face and has
controlled asymptotics as \(s\to0\) or \(s\to\infty\). The singularity
has not been discarded; it has been separated into geometrically distinct
boundary faces.
\end{answer}

\subsection{A one-dimensional model}

On the full cylinder \(\mathbb R_t\), consider
\[
 P=-\partial_t^2+\epsilon^2,\qquad \epsilon>0.
\]
Near the end \(t=-\infty\), use the compactifying coordinate \(x=e^t\).
Then
\[
 \partial_t=x\partial_x,\qquad dt=\frac{dx}{x}.
\]
With respect to \(dt'=dx'/x'\), the inverse of \(P\) has kernel
\[
 K_{P^{-1}}(x,x')
 =\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
   e^{-\epsilon|\log(x/x')|}
 =\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
 \begin{cases}
  (x'/x)^\epsilon,&x'\leq x,\\
  (x/x')^\epsilon,&x\leq x'.
 \end{cases}
\]
On $X_b^2$ this kernel is conormal to $\Delta_b$, smooth across the
front face away from $\Delta_b$, and vanishes to order $\epsilon$ at
$\mathrm{lb}$ and $\mathrm{rb}$. This model explains why the
$b$-calculus resolves the simultaneous limit $x,x'\to0$ while retaining
the translation-invariant variable $\log(x/x')$.

\subsection{General and iterated blow-ups}

The construction is not limited to a corner of a double space. A
\emph{p-submanifold} \(S\) of a manifold with corners \(Z\) is a
submanifold that, in suitable product coordinates
\[
 (x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_\ell),
\]
is given by the vanishing of a subset of the \(x\)- and \(y\)-coordinates.
Its inward spherical normal bundle is therefore well defined, and the real
blow-up
\[
 [Z;S]=(Z\setminus S)\sqcup S^+NS
\]
has a canonical smooth structure.

In the model \(Z=[0,\infty)^2\), \(S=\{(0,0)\}\), the two projective charts
are
\[
 U_x:(x,s=x'/x),\qquad
 U_{x'}:(s'=x/x',x').
\]
On their overlap,
\[
 s'=s^{-1},\qquad x'=sx.
\]
These transition functions are smooth where \(s,s'>0\), and the front
face is \(x=0\) in the first chart and \(x'=0\) in the second.

\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge,row sep=large]
 U_x=\{(x,s)\}
 \arrow[rr,bend left=18,"{x'=sx,\ s'=s^{-1}}"]
 &&
 U_{x'}=\{(s',x')\}
 \arrow[ll,bend left=18,"{x=s'x',\ s=(s')^{-1}}"]
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\begin{proposition}[Coordinate invariance]
The blow-up \([Z;S]\), its front face, and its blow-down map are
independent up to canonical diffeomorphism of the product coordinates
used in the construction.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A product-coordinate change preserving \(S\) has, in the normal
variables, the form
\[
 \widetilde z=A(w)z+O(|z|^2),
\]
where \(A(w)\) is invertible and preserves the inward normal cone.
Writing \(z=r\omega\) gives
\[
 \widetilde r
 =r\,|A(w)\omega+O(r)|,\qquad
 \widetilde\omega
 =\frac{A(w)\omega+O(r)}
 {|A(w)\omega+O(r)|}.
\]
Both expressions are smooth down to \(r=0\); the second restricts there
to the induced map on the spherical normal bundle. Hence every allowed
coordinate change lifts smoothly, and the lifted changes satisfy the
same cocycle identities.
\end{proof}

Iterated blow-ups are used for \(X_b^3\) and \(X_{b,h}^2\). If two
centers are disjoint or meet transversely, their blow-ups commute. If one
center is contained in another, blowing up the smaller center first makes
the lift of the larger one a p-submanifold. These facts justify the rule
used below: blow up nested centers in increasing order of dimension and
then blow up the transverse lifts.

\begin{discussion}
The order is geometric, not cosmetic. Blowing up a larger center first can
destroy the product structure of a smaller center and make its lift
ill-defined. The triple-space construction specifies the order precisely
so that all three lifted projections become b-fibrations.
\end{discussion}

\section{The b-tangent and b-cotangent bundles}

Let $\mathcal V_b(X)$ be the Lie algebra of smooth vector fields tangent
to every boundary face. In local coordinates
\[
 (x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_{n-k})
 \in[0,\infty)^k\times\mathbb R^{n-k},
\]
it is the locally free $C^\infty(X)$-module generated by
\[
 x_1\partial_{x_1},\ldots,x_k\partial_{x_k},
 \partial_{y_1},\ldots,\partial_{y_{n-k}}.
\]
Consequently there is a vector bundle \({}^bTX\to X\) with
\[
 C^\infty(X;{}^bTX)=\mathcal V_b(X).
\]
The natural anchor \({}^bTX\to TX\) is an isomorphism in the interior
but drops rank at the boundary.

The dual bundle \({}^bT^*X\) has local frame
\[
 \frac{dx_1}{x_1},\ldots,\frac{dx_k}{x_k},
 dy_1,\ldots,dy_{n-k}.
\]
A positive $b$-density is locally
\[
 a(x,y)\left|
 \frac{dx_1}{x_1}\cdots\frac{dx_k}{x_k}
 dy_1\cdots dy_{n-k}\right|,
 \qquad a>0.
\]
Under the cylindrical variables $x_j=e^{t_j}$, this is the ordinary
product density $a\,|dt\,dy|$.

\section{b-differential operators and their symbols}

The algebra $\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)$ consists of differential
operators of order at most $m$ generated over $C^\infty(X)$ by
$\mathcal V_b(X)$. Locally,
\[
 P=\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq m}
 a_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)
 (x\partial_x)^\alpha\partial_y^\beta.
\]
For manifolds with corners, $(x\partial_x)^\alpha$ denotes
$\prod_j(x_j\partial_{x_j})^{\alpha_j}$.

The filtration by order satisfies
\[
 [\operatorname{Diff}_b^m,\operatorname{Diff}_b^\ell]
 \subseteq\operatorname{Diff}_b^{m+\ell-1}.
\]
It follows that the associated graded algebra is canonically
$\operatorname{Sym}({}^bTX)$. Thus the principal symbol is the
coordinate-independent homogeneous bundle map
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P):{}^bT^*X\setminus0
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{Hom}(\pi^*E,\pi^*F).
\]
In the coordinates above,
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P)(x,y;\xi,\eta)
 =\sum_{|\alpha|+|\beta|=m}
   a_{\alpha\beta}(x,y)(i\xi)^\alpha(i\eta)^\beta.
\]

The symbol is multiplicative:
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{m+\ell}(PQ)
 ={}^b\sigma_m(P)\,{}^b\sigma_\ell(Q),
\]
and fits into the exact sequence
\[
 0\longrightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_b^{m-1}(X;E,F)
 \longrightarrow\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)
 \xrightarrow{{}^b\sigma_m}
 \mathcal P^m
 ({}^bT^*X;\operatorname{Hom}(\pi^*E,\pi^*F))
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Here \(\mathcal P^m\) denotes smooth fibrewise homogeneous polynomials of
degree \(m\).

\begin{definition}
An operator $P\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)$ is $b$-elliptic if
\({}^b\sigma_m(P)(\zeta)\) is invertible for every nonzero
$\zeta\in{}^bT^*X$.
\end{definition}

Fix a positive $b$-density and Hermitian metrics on the bundles. If
$V\in\mathcal V_b(X)$, integration by parts gives
\[
 V^*=-V-\operatorname{div}_b(V).
\]
Because $\operatorname{div}_b(V)\in C^\infty(X)$, formal adjoints
preserve the calculus:
\[
 P\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;E,F)
 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 P^*\in\operatorname{Diff}_b^m(X;F,E).
\]
This also gives
\[
 {}^b\sigma_m(P^*)={}^b\sigma_m(P)^*.
\]
\section{Conormal functions and boundary weights}

Let \(X\) be a compact manifold with corners, with boundary hypersurfaces
\(H_1,\ldots,H_N\) and defining functions
\(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_N\).

\begin{definition}
The space of bounded conormal functions is
\[
 \mathcal A^0(X)
 =
 \{u\in L^\infty(X^\circ):V_1\cdots V_k u\in L^\infty(X^\circ)
 \text{ for all }V_j\in\mathcal V_b(X)\}.
\]
For a multiweight \(\gamma=(\gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_N)\), set
\[
 \rho^\gamma\mathcal A^0(X)
 =
 \rho_1^{\gamma_1}\cdots\rho_N^{\gamma_N}\mathcal A^0(X).
\]
\end{definition}

This definition is independent of the chosen defining functions. Indeed, a
second defining function has the form \(\widetilde\rho_j=a_j\rho_j\), where
\(a_j\) is smooth and positive, and multiplication by \(a_j^{\gamma_j}\)
preserves \(\mathcal A^0(X)\).

Conormality records uniform control under vector fields tangent to every
boundary face. It is weaker than smoothness: powers and logarithms of
boundary defining functions are conormal whenever they remain bounded with
the prescribed weight.

\begin{definition}
For \(\epsilon>0\), write
\[
 \mathcal A^{0,\epsilon}(X)
 =
 \mathcal C^\infty(X)+\rho^\epsilon\mathcal A^0(X),
 \qquad
 \rho^\epsilon=\prod_{j=1}^N\rho_j^\epsilon.
\]
Thus \(u\in\mathcal A^{0,\epsilon}(X)\) has a smooth boundary value and a
conormal remainder that vanishes at every boundary hypersurface.
\end{definition}

The same notation applies to sections of a smooth vector bundle after
choosing local trivializations; the resulting space is independent of those
trivializations.

\section{Conormal distributions and the small b-calculus}

Let \(Z\subset M\) be an embedded submanifold of codimension \(k\). In local
coordinates \((x,y)\), with \(Z=\{x=0\}\), a classical conormal distribution
has the form
\[
 u(x,y)
 =
 (2\pi)^{-k}\int_{\mathbb R^k}e^{ix\cdot\xi}a(y,\xi)\,d\xi,
\]
modulo a smooth term, where \(a\) is a classical symbol. The symbol order,
with the standard dimension shift, defines the conormal order \(m\) and the
space \(I^m(M,Z)\). This description is invariant under coordinate changes.

For vector bundles \(E,F\to X\), let
\({}^b\Omega_R\) denote the lifted right b-density bundle on \(X_b^2\).
Set
\[
 \mathcal H_{E,F}
 =
 \operatorname{Hom}(E,F)\otimes{}^b\Omega_R.
\]

\begin{definition}
The small b-pseudodifferential calculus is
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F)
 =
 \left\{
 A:
 K_A\in
 I^m\!\left(
 X_b^2,\Delta_b;
 \mathcal H_{E,F}
 \right),
 \quad
 K_A\text{ vanishes to infinite order at }\mathrm{lb}\cup\mathrm{rb}
 \right\}.
\]
\end{definition}

The convention relating \(m\) to the conormal order depends on the density
normalization; throughout these notes, \(m\) is the operator order. The
lifted diagonal \(\Delta_b\) meets the front face transversely, so restricting
the kernel to the front face gives another conormal distribution.

Every b-differential operator of order \(m\) belongs to
\(\Psi_b^m(X)\): its Schwartz kernel is a finite sum of derivatives of the
delta distribution on \(\Delta_b\). Operators in the small calculus act
continuously
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F):
 \mathcal C^\infty(X;E)\longrightarrow\mathcal C^\infty(X;F)
\]
and preserve the space \(\dot{\mathcal C}^\infty(X)\) of sections vanishing
to infinite order at the boundary.

The principal symbol is a homogeneous bundle map on

\[

\]
and fits into the exact sequence

 \longrightarrow\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)
 \xrightarrow{{}^b\sigma_m}
 S^{[m]}({}^bT^*X;\operatorname{Hom}(E,F))
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Here \(S^{[m]}=S^m/S^{m-1}\). An operator is b-elliptic when this symbol is

\section{Composition in the b-calculus}

Composition is most transparent on the b-triple space. Start with \(X^3\)
and blow up the triple corner and the lifted pairwise corners in increasing
order of dimension. The resulting manifold with corners \(X_b^3\) has
b-fibrations
\[
 \pi_{12},\pi_{23},\pi_{13}:X_b^3\longrightarrow X_b^2
\]
lifting the three coordinate projections.

Their roles in composition are displayed by
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge,row sep=large]
 & X_b^3
   \arrow[dl,"\pi_{12}"']
   \arrow[d,"\pi_{13}"]
   \arrow[dr,"\pi_{23}"] & \\
 X_b^2 & X_b^2 & X_b^2 .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
The left and right arrows pull back the two kernels; the middle arrow
integrates out the shared variable.

If \(A\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) and
\(B\in\Psi_b^{m'}(X;G,E)\), then
\[
 K_{A\circ B}
 =
 (\pi_{13})_*
 \bigl(\pi_{12}^*K_A\;\pi_{23}^*K_B\bigr).
\]
The lifted conormal sets meet cleanly, and the vanishing at the left and
right faces makes the push-forward integrable. The pull-back, product, and
push-forward theorems for conormal distributions therefore give
\[
 \Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\circ\Psi_b^{m'}(X;G,E)
 \subseteq
 \Psi_b^{m+m'}(X;G,F).
\]
The leading oscillatory amplitudes multiply, so
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{m+m'}(A\circ B)
 =
 {}^b\sigma_m(A)\,{}^b\sigma_{m'}(B).
\]

To see the order \(m+m'\) directly, work near the lifted diagonal and
write the two kernels as oscillatory integrals. Their product has amplitude
of order \(m+m'\). Stationary phase in the intermediate variable identifies
the two covectors and produces the usual symbol product. Away from the
lifted diagonal, at least one factor is smooth; the push-forward is then
smooth. At every boundary face, the exponent contributed by the product is
the sum of the pulled-back exponents minus the fibre-density exponent. The
infinite-order side-face vanishing in the small calculus places that sum
strictly above the integrability threshold.

The residual part of the full calculus allows polyhomogeneous expansions at
the boundary faces. In the positive-weight case used below, products remain
integrable and the index sets combine under the same pull-back and
push-forward formula. Precise index-set bookkeeping is needed only when a
leading exponent reaches an integrability threshold.

\section{Parametrices in the small calculus}

Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic. Choose
\(Q_0\in\Psi_b^{-m}(X;F,E)\) with
\[
 {}^b\sigma_{-m}(Q_0)={}^b\sigma_m(P)^{-1}.
\]
Then
\[
 P Q_0=I-R_1,\qquad R_1\in\Psi_b^{-1}(X;F).
\]
The symbolic Neumann construction successively removes the homogeneous
terms of \(R_1\). Borel summation produces
\(Q\in\Psi_b^{-m}(X;F,E)\) such that
\[
 P Q=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L,
 \qquad R_R,R_L\in\Psi_b^{-\infty}(X).
\]

This is a symbolic parametrix, not yet a Fredholm inverse. A residual kernel
in the small b-calculus need not be compact on an unweighted b-Sobolev
space: after passage to the cylindrical coordinate \(t=-\log x\), it may
retain translation-invariant behavior as \(t\to\infty\). The normal operator,
introduced next, detects precisely this missing boundary condition.

\begin{question}
Why is a smooth residual kernel not automatically compact?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
Compactness on a compact manifold follows from smoothing plus Rellich's
lemma. The \(b\)-metric turns a boundary collar into an infinite cylinder,
so translations can escape every compact set. If a residual operator has a
nonzero translation-invariant limit \(K_\infty(t-t',y,y')\), choose a fixed
compactly supported section \(u\) on the cylinder and translate it to
\(u_j(t,y)=u(t-j,y)\). The sequence \(u_j\) is bounded and has no convergent
subsequence, while \(Ku_j\) is asymptotic to the corresponding translate of
\(K_\infty u\). Hence \(K\) is not compact unless its boundary model
vanishes or decays.
\end{answer}


\section{The normal operator and indicial family}

The principal b-symbol controls high-frequency behavior, but it does not
control escape along the cylindrical end. The missing invariant is the
restriction of the lifted kernel to the front face.

\begin{definition}
For \(A\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\), the normal operator \(N(A)\) is the
translation-invariant operator on the inward normal cylinder of
\(\partial X\) obtained by restricting the lifted kernel \(K_A\) to the
front face.
\end{definition}

After choosing a boundary defining function \(x\) and writing
\(t=-\log x\), the front face is modeled by
\(\partial X\times\partial X\times\mathbb R_t\). Thus \(N(A)\) is a
suspended pseudodifferential operator on \(\partial X\). The normal map is
multiplicative:
\[
 N(AB)=N(A)N(B).
\]
It follows either from restriction of the composition formula to the front
face or from Fourier transformation in \(t\).

For a b-differential operator
\[
 P=\sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(x,y)(x\partial_x)^j\partial_y^\alpha,
\]
freezing the coefficients at \(x=0\) gives
\[
 N(P)=
 \sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(0,y)(-\partial_t)^j\partial_y^\alpha.
\]

\begin{definition}
The indicial family of \(P\) is
\[
 I(P,\lambda)
 =
 \left.x^{-i\lambda}P x^{i\lambda}\right|_{\partial X}
 =
 \sum_{j+|\alpha|\leq m}
 a_{j\alpha}(0,y)(i\lambda)^j\partial_y^\alpha.
\]
For a b-pseudodifferential operator, \(I(A,\lambda)\) is the Fourier
transform of \(N(A)\) in the translation variable, with
\[
 \widehat f(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{it\lambda}f(t)\,dt.
\]
\end{definition}

Changing \(x\) conjugates the indicial family by a smooth nonvanishing
factor, so its invertibility is intrinsic. Multiplicativity gives
\[
 I(AB,\lambda)=I(A,\lambda)I(B,\lambda).
\]

The two symbol maps control complementary regimes:
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=large,row sep=large]
 \Psi_b^m(X)
   \arrow[r,"{}^b\!\sigma_m"]
   \arrow[d,"N"']
 & S^{[m]}({}^bT^*X) \\
 \Psi_{\mathrm{sus}}^m(\partial X)
   \arrow[r,"\mathcal F_t"']
 & \{I(A,\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb R} .
\end{tikzcd}
\]
The principal symbol sees large covectors at a fixed point. The indicial
family sees bounded frequency while the base point escapes down the
cylinder. Full ellipticity requires invertibility in both regimes.

\begin{discussion}
This is why the normal operator is often called a second symbol. It is not
lower-order information: a smoothing \(b\)-operator has zero principal
symbol but may have a nonzero normal operator, and that nonzero boundary
model can destroy compactness.
\end{discussion}

For a product b-Dirac operator
\[
 D=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+A_{\partial}),
\]
one has
\[
 I(D,\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A_{\partial}).
\]
Consequently \(D\) is invertible at the boundary on the unweighted line
precisely when the tangential operator \(A_{\partial}\) is invertible.

\section{Weighted b-Sobolev spaces}

Fix a positive b-density and a positive b-Laplacian. For
\(s\in\mathbb R\), the b-Sobolev space \(H_b^s(X;E)\) is defined by
b-derivatives for integral \(s\), and by the b-pseudodifferential calculus
for arbitrary \(s\). Near the boundary this is the ordinary Sobolev space
in the variables \((t,y)=(-\log x,y)\).

For \(\delta\in\mathbb R\), set
\[
 x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 =
 \{x^\delta u:u\in H_b^s(X;E)\}.
\]
A b-operator of order \(m\) acts continuously as
\[
 P:x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 \longrightarrow
 x^\delta H_b^{s-m}(X;F).
\]
Conjugation reduces this mapping to the unweighted one:
\[
 x^{-\delta}Px^\delta:
 H_b^s(X;E)\longrightarrow H_b^{s-m}(X;F).
\]

With the convention \(x^{i\lambda}\) used above, the weight \(x^\delta\)
corresponds to the Mellin line
\[
 \operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta.
\]

Indeed, write \(\lambda=\tau-i\delta\). Then
\(x^{i\lambda}=x^{\delta}x^{i\tau}\). Thus conjugating by \(x^\delta\)
moves the real Fourier line for the cylindrical variable to
\(\operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta\); no sign convention is hidden.

\begin{definition}
A b-elliptic operator \(P\) is fully elliptic at weight \(\delta\) if
\[
 I(P,\lambda)\text{ is invertible whenever }
 \operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta.
\]
\end{definition}

For several boundary hypersurfaces, impose the corresponding condition at
each face with a multiweight. Below, \(X\) has one boundary hypersurface.

\section{Meromorphic inversion of the indicial family}

Suppose \(P\) is b-elliptic. Its indicial family is elliptic on the closed
manifold \(\partial X\). A parameter-dependent parametrix shows that
\(I(P,\lambda)\) is invertible for \(\lambda\) in suitable sectors with
large modulus. The analytic Fredholm theorem then applies.

\begin{theorem}[Analytic Fredholm theorem]
Let \(T(\lambda)\) be a holomorphic family of Fredholm operators of index
zero on a connected domain. If \(T(\lambda_0)\) is invertible at one point,
then \(T(\lambda)^{-1}\) is meromorphic. Every coefficient in the principal
part of a Laurent expansion at a pole has finite rank.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Fix \(\lambda_*\). Choose closed complements
\[
 X=\ker T(\lambda_*)\oplus X_1,
 \qquad
 Y=\operatorname{ran}T(\lambda_*)\oplus C,
\]
where \(C\) is finite-dimensional. Since the index is zero,
\(\dim C=\dim\ker T(\lambda_*)\). Relative to these decompositions, write
\[
 T(\lambda)=
 \begin{pmatrix}a(\lambda)&b(\lambda)\\
                 c(\lambda)&d(\lambda)
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The block \(d(\lambda_*):X_1\to\operatorname{ran}T(\lambda_*)\) is an
isomorphism, so \(d(\lambda)^{-1}\) is holomorphic near \(\lambda_*\). Block
Gaussian elimination reduces invertibility of \(T(\lambda)\) to that of
the finite-dimensional Schur complement
\[
 S(\lambda)=a(\lambda)-b(\lambda)d(\lambda)^{-1}c(\lambda):
 \ker T(\lambda_*)\longrightarrow C.
\]
Where \(S\) is invertible, the block inverse formula expresses
\(T(\lambda)^{-1}\) using \(d(\lambda)^{-1}\) and \(S(\lambda)^{-1}\).
Cramer's rule gives
\[
 S(\lambda)^{-1}
 =\frac{\operatorname{adj}S(\lambda)}{\det S(\lambda)},
\]
so this inverse is meromorphic. Its negative Laurent coefficients have
range contained in a finite-dimensional space obtained from \(C\) and
\(\ker T(\lambda_*)\).

It remains to exclude \(\det S\equiv0\) on every overlapping
neighborhood. That would make \(T(\lambda)\) noninvertible throughout the
connected domain, contrary to invertibility at \(\lambda_0\). Analytic
continuation therefore makes the local meromorphic inverses agree and
produces the claimed global meromorphic inverse.
\end{proof}

Applied to \(I(P,\lambda)\), this theorem yields a discrete set
\[
 \operatorname{Spec}_b(P)
 =
 \{\lambda\in\mathbb C:I(P,\lambda)\text{ is not invertible}\},
\]
called the boundary spectrum or set of indicial roots. The inverse
\(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\) is meromorphic with finite-rank residues.

A weight is admissible exactly when its Mellin line avoids
\(\operatorname{Spec}_b(P)\). Moving the line without crossing an indicial
root does not change the Fredholm index. When a root is crossed, the index
jump is determined by its algebraic multiplicity; the sign depends on the
direction in which the weight is moved.

\section{The full b-calculus and the Fredholm parametrix}

The symbolic parametrix constructed in the small calculus has smooth
remainders, but their normal operators need not vanish. Full ellipticity
allows one to remove this boundary error.

Let \(Q_0\in\Psi_b^{-m}\) be a symbolic parametrix for \(P\):
\[
 PQ_0=I-R_0,\qquad Q_0P=I-R_0',
 \qquad R_0,R_0'\in\Psi_b^{-\infty}.
\]
On an admissible Mellin line, define the boundary correction by
\[
 I(Q_1,\lambda)
 =
 I(Q_0,\lambda)
 +I(P,\lambda)^{-1}I(R_0,\lambda).
\]
Then \(I(PQ_1,\lambda)=I\). The inverse Mellin transform produces a kernel
with polyhomogeneous expansions at the boundary faces. Its exponents are
governed by the poles of \(I(P,\lambda)^{-1}\). This is the reason the
full b-calculus, rather than only the small calculus, is needed.

Here are the details of this correction. Choose a horizontal line
\(\Gamma_\delta=\{\operatorname{Im}\lambda=-\delta\}\) avoiding the boundary
spectrum. With cutoffs \(\chi(x)\chi(x')\), the inverse Mellin formula
\[
 K_C(x,y;x',y')
 =
 \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\Gamma_\delta}
 \left(\frac{x}{x'}\right)^{i\lambda}
 I(P,\lambda)^{-1}I(R_0,\lambda)(y,y')\,d\lambda
\]
defines a residual correction \(C\) near the front face. Parameter-elliptic
estimates make the integral rapidly convergent in
\(\operatorname{Re}\lambda\). Applying \(P\), using Mellin
multiplicativity, and restricting to the front face gives
\[
 N(P(Q_0+C)-I)=0.
\]
If the contour is shifted, the residue theorem adds terms
\[
 x^{i\lambda_0}(\log x)^j a_{\lambda_0,j}(y,y'),
\]
where \(\lambda_0\) is an indicial root and \(j\) is one less than the
order of the corresponding pole. These are precisely the
polyhomogeneous terms allowed in the full calculus.

The construction is performed on both sides. Any mismatch between the two
corrections is residual and has zero normal operator. A Neumann iteration
in the residual ideal removes its successive boundary coefficients; Borel
summation removes all of them at once. An interior symbolic correction then
restores infinite-order vanishing at the lifted diagonal. This yields the
two-sided parametrix below.

Iterating at the interior diagonal and at the boundary faces gives
\(Q\in\Psi_b^{-m}\) in the full calculus with
\[
 PQ=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L.
\]
At an admissible weight, \(R_R\) and \(R_L\) are residual operators whose
normal operators vanish and whose side-face exponents have the decay
required by that weight.

\begin{lemma}
The remainders \(R_R\) and \(R_L\) act compactly on weighted b-Sobolev
spaces.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A residual kernel gains arbitrarily many b-derivatives. Vanishing of its
normal operator removes the translation-invariant limit on the cylindrical
end, while its positive side-face weights give uniform decay there. Cut
the cylinder at \(t=T\). On the compact part, Rellich's lemma makes the
operator compact; on the tail, the operator norm tends to zero as
\(T\to\infty\). Hence the full operator is a norm limit of compact
operators.

More explicitly, let \(\chi_T\) equal one on \(t\leq T\) and vanish on
\(t\geq T+1\). The operator \(\chi_TR\chi_T\) factors as
\[
 H_b^s\xrightarrow{\,R\,}H_b^{s+N}
 \xrightarrow{\,\chi_T\,}H_b^s
\]
for any \(N>0\); the second arrow is compact by Rellich on the truncated
cylinder. The full-calculus estimates and \(N(R)=0\) give
\[
 \|(1-\chi_T)R\|+\|R(1-\chi_T)\|\longrightarrow0.
\]
Therefore
\[
 R-\chi_TR\chi_T
 =(1-\chi_T)R+\chi_TR(1-\chi_T)
\]
tends to zero in operator norm. This proves compactness without assuming
that the noncompact \(b\)-manifold itself has a compact Sobolev embedding.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fredholm theorem for b-operators]
Let \(P\in\Psi_b^m(X;E,F)\) be b-elliptic and fully elliptic at weight
\(\delta\). Then, for every \(s\in\mathbb R\),
\[
 P:x^\delta H_b^s(X;E)
 \longrightarrow
 x^\delta H_b^{s-m}(X;F)
\]
is Fredholm.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The full b-parametrix \(Q\) is bounded between the reverse weighted Sobolev
spaces, and
\[
 PQ=I-R_R,\qquad QP=I-R_L
\]
with compact remainders. Atkinson's theorem therefore implies that \(P\)
is Fredholm.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Elliptic regularity]
If \(Pu=f\), where \(u\in x^\delta H_b^{s_0}\) and
\(f\in x^\delta H_b^{s-m}\), then \(u\in x^\delta H_b^s\). In particular,
null-solutions are smooth in
the interior and polyhomogeneous at the boundary, with exponents drawn from
the indicial spectrum.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply the parametrix:
\[
 u=Qf+R_Lu.
\]
The first term gains \(m\) derivatives, and the residual term gains
arbitrarily many. Iteration gives the stated regularity. Mellin inversion
and contour shifting yield the boundary expansion; residues at indicial
poles produce powers of \(x\) and, at higher-order poles, logarithmic
terms.
\end{proof}

\section{The K-theoretic proof architecture}

The closed index theorem has two layers: an elliptic symbol defines a
\(K\)-class, and the analytic index is the unique push-forward of that
class normalized by the Bott operator. On a manifold with boundary, the
interior symbol alone is insufficient. One must also record an invertible
model at the cylindrical end. The b-calculus packages exactly these two
pieces \cite{Melrose,MelrosePiazza}.

Let \(\mathcal A_b\) be the operator-norm closure of the stabilized
order-zero b-calculus, and let \(\Sigma_b\) be its joint symbol algebra.
An element of \(\Sigma_b\) is a compatible pair
\[
 (a,q)=
 \bigl({}^b\sigma_0(P),I(P,\lambda)\bigr),
\]
where \(a\) is the b-principal symbol and \(q\) is the suspended indicial
family. Compatibility means that the principal symbol of \(q\) is the
restriction of \(a\) to the boundary. The full symbol sequence is
\[
 0\longrightarrow\mathcal K
 \longrightarrow\mathcal A_b
 \xrightarrow{\,j\,}\Sigma_b
 \longrightarrow0.
\]
Indeed, a b-operator with zero principal symbol and zero normal operator is
compact; the parametrix construction proves exactness at the other terms.

If \(P\) is fully elliptic, \(j(P)\) is invertible. After stabilization it
defines a class \([j(P)]\in K_1(\Sigma_b)\). The six-term exact sequence
has a connecting map
\[
 \partial:K_1(\Sigma_b)\longrightarrow
 K_0(\mathcal K)\cong\mathbb Z.
\]

\begin{lemma}[The connecting map is the Fredholm index]
With the usual sign convention for \(\partial\),
\[
 \partial[j(P)]=\operatorname{ind}P.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Lift \(j(P)^{-1}\) to a b-parametrix \(Q\). Then
\[
 I-QP\in\mathcal K,\qquad I-PQ\in\mathcal K.
\]
After a compact perturbation, the polar part \(V\) of \(P\) is a partial
isometry. The definition of the connecting map gives
\[
 \partial[j(P)]
 =[I-V^*V]-[I-VV^*].
\]
The first defect projection is the orthogonal projection onto
\(\ker P\), and the second is the projection onto \(\ker P^*\). Under
\(K_0(\mathcal K)\cong\mathbb Z\), their difference is
\(\dim\ker P-\dim\ker P^*=\operatorname{ind}P\).
\end{proof}

For the product Dirac operator, the unbounded joint elliptic data are
\[
 \left({}^b\sigma_1(D),\,
 I(D,\lambda)=
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A)\right).
\]
Composing on the right with the inverse of a fixed positive elliptic
family of order one normalizes this to an order-zero element of
\(\Sigma_b\) without changing invertibility or the index class.
Equivalently, one may use the bounded transform of \(D\), whose indicial
family includes the corresponding factor of order minus one.
If \(A\) is invertible, this pair is fully elliptic. If \(A\) has kernel,
replace it in the indicial family by
\[
 A_\varepsilon=A+\varepsilon\Pi_0,\qquad \varepsilon>0,
\]
where \(\Pi_0\) projects onto \(\ker A\). This smoothing perturbation is
finite rank, and the perturbed family is invertible. It chooses the same
side of the zero modes as the APS
projection \(\Pi_{\geq0}(A)\). Thus the APS condition is not extra data
unrelated to the symbol: it is the boundary trivialization needed to make
the joint symbol a \(K\)-class.

Denote the resulting stable class by
\[
 [\sigma_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)]
 \in K_{\mathrm{APS}}^0({}^bT^*X).
\]
This notation means stable homotopy classes of b-symbols equipped with
their invertible boundary trivializations. A neat embedding
\[
 i:(X,\partial X)\hookrightarrow
 (\mathbb R_+^N,\mathbb R^{N-1})
\]
defines a Thom push-forward of these relative classes. Normal deformation
and collar doubling identify the target with the Bott group
\[
 K_c^0(\mathbb R^{2N})\cong\mathbb Z.
\]
Call the resulting integer
\(\operatorname{t\text{-}ind}_{\mathrm{APS}}\).

\begin{theorem}[K-theoretic APS index theorem]
For a product-type graded Dirac operator,
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \operatorname{t\text{-}ind}_{\mathrm{APS}}
 [\sigma_{\mathrm{APS}}(D^+)].
\]
\end{theorem}

The theorem asserts that the following diagram commutes; the left vertical
map is the connecting-map index of the joint symbol.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge,row sep=large]
 K_{\mathrm{APS}}^0({}^bT^*X)
 \arrow[r,"i_!"]
 \arrow[d,"{\operatorname{a\text{-}ind}_{\mathrm{APS}}}"']
 &K_c^0(\mathbb R^{2N})
 \arrow[d,"\mathrm{Bott}"]\\
 \mathbb Z\arrow[r,equal]&\mathbb Z
\end{tikzcd}
\]

\begin{proof}
The proof follows the closed push-forward argument. In a tubular
neighborhood, the Thom class is represented analytically by the
fibrewise Bott operator
\[
 B_\nu=\sum_j c(e_j)\partial_{v_j}+\widehat c(v),
\]
on \(\Lambda^\bullet\nu_\mathbb C^*\), where
\(c(v)=\varepsilon(v^\flat)-\iota_v\) and
\(\widehat c(v)=\varepsilon(v^\flat)+\iota_v\).
Its even kernel is the Gaussian line and its odd kernel is zero.
Taking the graded product with \(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}\) therefore preserves
the index. On the boundary collar the normal Bott factor is product type,
so it carries the chosen indicial trivialization to the pushed-forward
operator without changing which zero modes are selected. Excision removes
the complement of the tubular neighborhood.

After the neat embedding and collar doubling, the class is an integer
multiple of the Bott generator. The Bott operator has index one, so both
the analytic index, computed by the connecting map above, and the
topological push-forward equal that integer. This proves the formula.
\end{proof}

This theorem proves the equality of two \emph{integer} indices. Ordinary
\(K\)-theory does not by itself determine the real number
\[
 \bar\eta(A)=\frac{\eta(A)+\dim\ker A}{2}.
\]
That number is a secondary, differential refinement of the boundary
\(K\)-class: it can vary continuously with the metric and connection even
when the underlying \(K\)-class is fixed. The heat-kernel and b-trace
argument below supplies its canonical real lift and proves the numerical
formula
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
 -\bar\eta(A).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
 \bar\eta(A)\equiv
 \int_X
 \left[\widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)\right]_{[n]}
 \pmod{\mathbb Z}.
\]
Thus the exponentiated eta invariant is the boundary transgression of the
local index form. The \(K\)-theoretic
proof explains integrality and homotopy invariance, while the heat proof
identifies the precise spectral correction.

\begin{question}
Why can \(\bar\eta(A)\) vary while the \(K\)-theoretic APS index is
constant?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
As the geometric data vary without a zero eigenvalue crossing, the local
integral and \(\bar\eta(A)\) vary by the same amount, so their difference
remains the fixed integer index. At a crossing, the spectral projection
changes by a finite-dimensional space; spectral flow changes the
\(K\)-theoretic boundary trivialization and accounts for the integer jump.
Ordinary \(K\)-theory records that jump, whereas the eta invariant records
the continuous real-valued refinement between jumps.
\end{answer}

\section{The b-trace defect}

Let \(A\) be a residual b-operator whose lifted kernel has a full expansion
at the front face and sufficient decay at the side faces. Its b-trace is the
finite part of the diagonal integral,
\[
 {}^b\Tr(A)
 =
 \operatorname*{FP}_{z=0}
 \int_X x^z\operatorname{tr}_E K_A(p,p)\,d\mu_b(p).
\]
The ordinary trace property fails because the finite part retains a
boundary contribution.

Use the Fourier convention fixed for the indicial family:
\[
 \widehat f(\lambda)=\int_{\mathbb R}e^{it\lambda}f(t)\,dt,
 \qquad
 f(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb R}e^{-it\lambda}
 \widehat f(\lambda)\,d\lambda.
\]

\begin{theorem}[b-trace defect formula]
If the products below are defined and residual, then
\[
 {}^b\Tr([A,B])
 =
 \frac{i}{2\pi}
 \int_{\mathbb R}
 \Tr_{\partial X}
 \left(
 \partial_\lambda I(A,\lambda)\,I(B,\lambda)
 \right)\,d\lambda.
\]
For graded bundles, the same formula holds with the supercommutator and
supertrace.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
For \(\operatorname{Re}z>0\), cyclicity of the ordinary trace gives
\[
 \Tr(x^z[A,B])=\Tr([x^z,A]B).
\]
Set
\[
 C_z=z^{-1}x^{-z}[x^z,A].
\]
This family extends to \(z=0\), and its indicial family there is
\[
 I(C_0,\lambda)=i\,\partial_\lambda I(A,\lambda).
\]
The trace of \(x^zC_zB\) has a simple pole at zero. Its residue is the
Fourier integral of \(I(C_0,\lambda)I(B,\lambda)\), divided by \(2\pi\).
Since \([x^z,A]B=z x^zC_zB\), that residue is the constant term of the
original trace. Finally \(i/(2\pi)=-1/(2\pi i)\).
\end{proof}

This identity is independent of the defining function: changing \(x\)
changes both the finite part and the indicial family by compensating terms.

\section{Dirac operators of product type}

Let \(X\) be an even-dimensional compact Riemannian manifold with boundary.
Assume that the metric and Clifford data are products near the boundary.
Let
\[
 E=E^+\oplus E^-
\]
be a graded Hermitian Clifford module with compatible connection. Its Dirac
operator is odd,
\[
 D=
 \begin{pmatrix}
 0&D^-\\
 D^+&0
 \end{pmatrix},
\]
and near \(\partial X\) it has the form
\[
 D=c(du)(\partial_u+A),
\]
where \(u\) is the inward normal coordinate and \(A\) is the self-adjoint
tangential Dirac operator.

Extend the collar coordinate \(u\) to negative values on the attached
outward cylinder, put \(t=-u\geq0\), and set \(x=e^{-t}=e^u\). Then
\(du=dx/x\) and \(\partial_u=x\partial_x\), so the product formula becomes
\[
 D=c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(x\partial_x+A),
\]
with the same \(A\) as above. This convention fixes the sign of the APS
correction to be \(-(\eta_A(0)+\dim\ker A)/2\).

The Weitzenböck formula is
\[
 D^2=\nabla^*\nabla+\mathcal R,
\]
where \(\mathcal R\) is the curvature endomorphism. For the spin Dirac
operator twisted by \(W\),
\[
 \mathcal R=\frac14\operatorname{scal}
 +\frac12\sum_{j,k}c(e_j)c(e_k)F^W(e_j,e_k).
\]

\]
where the final blow-up assigns weight one to spatial variables and weight

At the temporal front face, the model equation is the Euclidean heat
equation on each b-tangent fibre. At the boundary front face, the model is
the heat equation for the normal operator on the cylinder. Full ellipticity
makes the large-cylinder model invertible in the required weighted space.

\begin{proposition}[Compatibility of the heat models]
The Euclidean model at the temporal front face and the cylindrical normal
model at the boundary front face have the same restriction to their
intersection. They therefore determine a single leading kernel on
\(X_{b,h}^2\).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Near a boundary point use b-normal coordinates
\[
 (t,y)=(-\log x,y).
\]
After parabolic rescaling about the diagonal, the coefficients of \(D^2\)
freeze at the base point. The temporal model is therefore the Gaussian
heat kernel of the constant-coefficient operator on
\({}^bT_pX\). If the base point tends to the boundary, the same frozen
operator splits into the normal line in \(t\) and the tangential operator
on \(T_yY\). This is exactly the small-time restriction of the
translation-invariant normal heat kernel. Thus both models restrict to
\[
 (4\pi)^{-n/2}e^{-|V|^2/4}
\]
times parallel transport at the corner of the two faces.
\end{proof}

The compatibility permits one to extend the two prescribed normal
operators to a global initial parametrix. Successive transport equations
remove the Taylor coefficients of the error at the temporal face;
successive normal-operator inversions remove its coefficients at the
boundary face. Because the corrections agree at the corner, alternating
between the two faces improves the error without spoiling work already
done at the other face. Borel summation produces a kernel whose heat error
vanishes to infinite order at every temporal boundary.

Starting with the Gaussian model, solve the transport equations
successively along the temporal front face. Borel summation gives a
parametrix \(H_0(t)\) satisfying
\[
 (\partial_t+D^2)H_0(t)=R(t),
\]
where \(R\) vanishes to infinite order at \(t=0\). The Volterra series
\[
 H
 =
 H_0-H_0*R+H_0*R*R-\cdots
\]
converges for small \(t\), and the semigroup property extends it to all
positive times. Here
\[
 (K_1*K_2)(t)=\int_0^tK_1(t-s)K_2(s)\,ds.
\]
Thus \(H(t)=e^{-tD^2}\).

The convergence uses the ordered time simplex, a point that is easy to
lose in a formal Neumann-series argument. If
\(\|R(t)\|\leq C\) for \(0\leq t\leq t_0\), then
\[
 R^{*k}(t)
 =
 \int_{\substack{s_j\geq0\\s_1+\cdots+s_k=t}}
 R(s_1)\cdots R(s_k)\,ds_1\cdots ds_{k-1}
\]
and the simplex has volume \(t^{k-1}/(k-1)!\). Hence
\[
 \|R^{*k}(t)\|
 \leq \frac{C^k t^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}.
\]
The factorial proves absolute convergence. The same estimate after
applying \(b\)-derivatives proves convergence in the residual heat
calculus. Since \(R\) vanishes to infinite order at \(t=0\), so does every
correction term, and the initial value remains the identity.

The construction also proves Duhamel's formula. If \(L_r\) is a smooth
family of nonnegative self-adjoint elliptic operators, then
\[
 \frac{d}{dr}e^{-tL_r}
 =
 -\int_0^t
 e^{-(t-s)L_r}\dot L_r e^{-sL_r}\,ds.
\]
Both sides solve the same inhomogeneous heat equation with zero initial
value, which proves the identity by uniqueness.

\section{Small-time supertrace}

For an ordinary Laplace-type operator, the diagonal heat kernel has an
expansion
\[
 K(t;p,p)
 \sim
 (4\pi t)^{-n/2}\sum_{j=0}^\infty t^j a_j(p).
\]
For a Dirac operator, Clifford supertrace cancels all terms below total
degree \(n\). Getzler rescaling identifies the surviving term.

\begin{theorem}[Local index theorem]
For a graded Dirac operator,
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}
 \operatorname{Str}K(t;p,p)\,d\operatorname{vol}(p)
 =
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX,\nabla^{TX})
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S,\nabla^{E/S})
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
For the spin Dirac operator twisted by \(W\), the second factor is
\(\operatorname{ch}(W,\nabla^W)\).
\end{theorem}

On a manifold with cylindrical end, diagonal integration is interpreted as
a b-integral. The heat-space expansion shows
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}{}^b\Str(e^{-tD^2})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)
 \operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
Because the data are of product type, no additional local boundary density
appears.

\section{The eta invariant}

For \(\operatorname{Re}s\) large, define
\[
 \eta_A(s)
 =
 \sum_{\lambda\in\operatorname{Spec}(A)\setminus\{0\}}
 \operatorname{sign}(\lambda)|\lambda|^{-s}.
\]
The Mellin representation is
\[
 \eta_A(s)
 =
 \frac1{\Gamma((s+1)/2)}
 \int_0^\infty
 t^{(s-1)/2}\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2})\,dt.
\]
The large-time integral is entire after omission of the zero eigenspace.
The heat expansion gives a meromorphic continuation of the small-time
integral.

\begin{theorem}
The eta function of a self-adjoint elliptic differential operator of
positive order on a closed manifold is regular at \(s=0\).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Split the Mellin integral at \(t=1\). On the orthogonal complement of the
kernel, the large-time integrand is exponentially decreasing, so that
part is entire. For an operator of order \(r\) on a \(d\)-manifold, the
pseudodifferential heat calculus gives a polyhomogeneous expansion of
\(\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2})\) as \(t\downarrow0\). Termwise Mellin integration gives
the meromorphic continuation.

The possible residue at zero is a constant multiple of the
noncommutative residue of the order-zero operator
\[
 \operatorname{sgn}(A)=A|A|^{-1}
\]
on \(\ker A^\perp\). Modulo a finite-rank operator,
\(\operatorname{sgn}(A)=2\Pi_+-I\), where \(\Pi_+\) is the positive spectral
projection. The noncommutative residue vanishes on the identity, on
finite-rank operators, and on pseudodifferential projections. The possible
residue is therefore zero. For a Dirac-type operator the same cancellation
is visible directly in the parity of its local Clifford heat coefficients.
\end{proof}

For a Dirac-type operator, regularity is also visible from the parity of the
Clifford heat coefficients. Write
\[
 \eta(A)=\eta_A(0),\qquad h(A)=\dim\ker A.
\]

\section{Heat proof of the APS index theorem}

Assume first that \(A\) is invertible. Set
\[
 F(t)={}^b\Str(e^{-tD^2}).
\]
Since \(D\) commutes with \(e^{-tD^2}\),
\[
 F'(t)
 =
 -\frac12\,{}^b\Str
 \left([D,De^{-tD^2}]_{\mathrm s}\right).
\]
Apply the graded b-trace defect formula and use
\[
 I(D,\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A).
\]
Moreover,
\[
 I(De^{-tD^2},\lambda)
 =
 c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right)(i\lambda+A)
 e^{-t(\lambda^2+A^2)},
\qquad
 \partial_\lambda I(D,\lambda)=i\,c\!\left(\frac{dx}{x}\right).
\]
The Clifford relation \(c(dx/x)^2=-I\), together with the graded matrix
trace, leaves the term containing \(A\); the term odd in \(\lambda\)
integrates to zero. Finally,
\[
 \int_{\mathbb R}e^{-t\lambda^2}\,d\lambda
 =\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{t}}.
\]
Substitution into the defect formula, including the factor \(1/2\) above,
gives
\[
 F'(t)
 =
 -\frac1{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2}).
\]
This is the only place where the b-supertrace varies with \(t\).

The full heat kernel and the spectral gap at the boundary imply
\[
 \lim_{t\to\infty}F(t)=\operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}}).
\]
The local index theorem gives
\[
 \lim_{t\downarrow0}F(t)
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}.
\]
Integrating the trace-defect identity and using the Mellin formula at
\(s=0\) yields
\[
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}
 -\frac12\eta(A).
\]

Indeed,
\[
 F(\infty)-F(0)
 =
 -\frac1{2\sqrt\pi}
 \int_0^\infty t^{-1/2}\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2})\,dt
 =-\frac12\eta_A(0).
\]
At the lower endpoint, this means: first insert \(t^{s/2}\) for
\(\operatorname{Re}s\) large, use the Mellin identity, and then continue
to \(s=0\).

If \(A\) has kernel, perturb it by a finite-rank operator on
\(\ker A\), or equivalently use the APS spectral projection and pass to the
limit. The zero modes contribute \(h(A)/2\).

To determine the contribution and its sign, put
\(A_\varepsilon=A+\varepsilon\Pi_0\), where \(\Pi_0\) projects onto
\(\ker A\) and \(\varepsilon>0\). Then
\[
 \eta(A_\varepsilon)=\eta(A)+h(A).
\]
The decaying-mode condition on the perturbed cylinder converges to
\(\Pi_{\geq0}(A)(u|_{\partial X})=0\). The Fredholm index is constant for
\(\varepsilon>0\), so passage to the limit gives
\(-(\eta(A)+h(A))/2\).

\begin{theorem}[Atiyah--Patodi--Singer]
Let \(D^+\) be a product-type graded Dirac operator on an
even-dimensional compact manifold with boundary. With APS boundary
condition
\[
 \Pi_{\geq0}(A)(u|_{\partial X})=0,
\]
one has
\[
 \boxed{
 \operatorname{ind}(D^+_{\mathrm{APS}})
 =
 \int_X
 \left[
 \widehat A(TX)\operatorname{ch}(E/S)
 \right]_{[n]}
 -
 \frac{\eta_A(0)+\dim\ker A}{2}.
 }
\]
\end{theorem}

The interior term is local. The eta invariant and the kernel dimension are
the global spectral correction forced by the cylindrical end.

\begin{question}
Why can the boundary correction not be another local curvature integral?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The derivative \(F'(t)\) is the full spectral trace
\(\Tr(Ae^{-tA^2})\), not the integral of one fixed heat coefficient.
Integrating it from zero to infinity uses every eigenvalue of \(A\).
Small-time asymptotics control the meromorphic continuation, but the value
\(\eta_A(0)\) also contains large-time information and therefore depends on
the global spectral asymmetry. This is exactly the information lost when a
local index density is integrated on a manifold with boundary.
\end{answer}
